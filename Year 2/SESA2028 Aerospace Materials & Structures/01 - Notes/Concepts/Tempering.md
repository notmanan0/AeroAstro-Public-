# Tempering

**Tempering** reheats quenched martensite to below $A_1$ (typically 150-650 °C) for a set time.

## What happens

Carbon diffuses out of the supersaturated BCT lattice and precipitates as a **fine dispersion of carbides** (transition carbides, then $\mathrm{Fe_3C}$). The lattice relaxes towards BCC ferrite, dislocations recover, and residual stresses relax.

| Tempering temperature | Structure | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| ~200 °C | martensite + transition carbides | still very hard; brittleness greatly reduced |
| ~400 °C | tempered martensite (fine $\mathrm{Fe_3C}$) | good strength-toughness balance (springs) |
| ~600 °C | heavily tempered; laths recover and carbides spheroidise | lower strength, highest toughness (shafts, landing gear) |

Time and temperature are roughly interchangeable, because both drive diffusion.

**Alloying.** Mo, Cr, V and W slow softening. At high Mo or V, **secondary hardening** occurs: fine alloy carbides that resist coarsening, so strength is kept at high tempering temperatures (tool and high-temperature steels).

**Watch out for** temper embrittlement (P, Sn, Sb segregating to boundaries around 350-550 °C, prevented with Mo) and tempered-martensite embrittlement around 250-400 °C.

## Standard exam answer

Quench (austenite to martensite: hard and brittle because of trapped C and dislocations), then **temper** (C diffuses to fine carbides), which *mostly* keeps the strength while greatly improving ductility and toughness. Q&T medium-carbon and low-alloy steels (e.g. 4340) are the result.
