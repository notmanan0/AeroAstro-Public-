# Tempering

Tempering reheats quenched martensite to a temperature below the eutectoid, typically 200–650 °C, so that the trapped carbon diffuses out as a fine dispersion of carbides, lowering hardness a little and brittleness a lot.

As-quenched martensite is supersaturated with carbon and full of distortion and dislocations, too brittle for most uses. Reheating lets the carbon move: lightly tempered, the laths remain with fine carbides inside; heavily tempered, the laths break down and the carbides coarsen and spheroidise into a fine ferrite–carbide mixture. Strength and hardness fall with tempering temperature or time, which are roughly interchangeable because both drive diffusion, while ductility and toughness rise. In the lecture's quench-cracking example, a 0.4 % C steel water-quenched from 875 °C is tempered at 580 °C for an hour for exactly this trade.

![[materials_martensite_hardness_tempering.png|800]]

Alloying changes the curve. Molybdenum slows the softening; at higher levels it gives secondary hardening, where after an initial fall the hardness rises again as fine alloy carbides precipitate that do not coarsen, so strength is retained while brittleness is relieved. The same alloying elements that raise [[Hardenability and Jominy Test|hardenability]] therefore also decide what carbides form on tempering. The physics is close to the ageing of aluminium alloys ([[Precipitation Hardening]]): a fine precipitate dispersion strengthens, and coarsening at too high a temperature or too long a time loses it.
