# MMC vs CMC

Metal matrix composites put ceramic fibres or particles in a metal to raise specific stiffness at temperatures a resin cannot reach, while ceramic matrix composites put ceramic fibres in a ceramic to make a hot, stiff, inert material tough.

The two use the same kind of reinforcement for opposite reasons. A metal matrix is already tough and ductile; the ceramic adds stiffness per unit mass (and wear resistance or tuned thermal properties), at some cost in ductility, so the fibre must be strongly bonded to take load. A ceramic matrix is already stiff and hot-strong but brittle, because dislocations barely move and nothing blunts its flaws; the fibres, finer and freer of defects, bridge cracks in the matrix and then pull out, so the interface is made deliberately weak. Those weak interfaces are also oxidation paths, so a CMC can resist oxidation less well than a solid ceramic.

Both are expensive because they are made hot. MMCs come from powder pressed and sintered with particles, fibres diffusion-bonded between foils, or particles stirred or fibres squeezed into molten or mushy metal, and the reinforcement can react with the hot metal, as carbon does with titanium. CMCs come from repeated polymer infiltration and pyrolysis, pressure sintering of powders or slurries, or chemical vapour infiltration, and are dearer still. MMCs are used mostly in ground transport and electronics, with aerospace interest in designs such as the titanium-SiC bladed ring (bling) that removes most of a disc's bore. CMCs are aimed at the hottest parts of aero engines and at rocket nozzles; for many hot parts a ceramic [[Thermal Barrier Coatings|coating]] on a metal is the cheaper answer.

In design questions both are sized with the [[Rule of Mixtures]], adding the composite density. Watch for ceramic strengths that are really compressive, which the tensile rule of mixtures overstates, and for a ceramic's low fracture toughness when the part is in tension.
