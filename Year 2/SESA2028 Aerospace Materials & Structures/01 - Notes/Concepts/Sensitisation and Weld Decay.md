# Sensitisation and Weld Decay

Sensitisation is the loss of corrosion resistance along the grain boundaries of a stainless steel when chromium carbides precipitate there during slow cooling or reheating, and weld decay is the intergranular corrosion that follows in the heat-affected zone of a weld.

A stainless steel is protected by a chromium-rich passive oxide, which needs more than about 12 % chromium in solution. Held at an intermediate temperature, the steel precipitates $\mathrm{Cr_{23}C_6}$ on its grain boundaries, and the chromium for those carbides diffuses in from the metal next to them. That leaves a thin zone along each boundary below the level needed for a protective film. The depleted boundaries become anodes, the passive grain centres become cathodes, and a microscopic cell eats along the boundaries while the grains themselves look untouched. Welding is the usual cause, because the metal either side of the weld spends time in the dangerous range as it cools.

![[materials_weld_decay_cr_profile.png|800]]

There are three remedies. Cool quickly through the sensitising range so the carbides have no time to form. Lower the carbon content so that little carbide can form, as in the "L" grades such as 316L used for welding. Or stabilise the steel with titanium or niobium, which form more stable carbides than chromium and so keep the chromium in solution. Weld decay appears in past papers as the explanation for intergranular attack after slow cooling and for the difficulty of welding austenitic stainless steel (2013-14 B3(iii), 2015-16 B3(iii), 2016-17 A2(ii), 2017-18 A2(iii), 2020-21 MQ2(a)(iii), 2021-22 MQ1(c)).
