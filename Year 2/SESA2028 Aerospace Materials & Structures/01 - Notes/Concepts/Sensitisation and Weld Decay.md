# Sensitisation and Weld Decay

## What happens

Austenitic stainless steel that is **slowly cooled** through, or held in, about 500-800 °C (for example the heat-affected zone next to a weld, or long high-temperature service) precipitates **chromium carbides $\mathrm{Cr_{23}C_6}$ on grain boundaries**.

Carbon diffuses fast, so it comes from the whole grain. Chromium diffuses slowly, so it comes only from a thin zone next to the boundary. That zone falls **below about 12 % Cr** and can no longer keep a passive $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ film.

**Result:** a thin, active, Cr-depleted strip along every grain boundary (the **anode**), next to large passive grains (the **cathode**). This is a tiny galvanic cell with a very small anode, so rapid **intergranular attack**. Grains can drop out, and SCC is promoted.

**Weld decay** is this happening in bands either side of a weld, where the HAZ passed through the sensitisation range.

## Prevention (three answers the lecturer expects)

1. **Cool quickly** through the sensitisation range (quench after solution annealing) so carbides have no time to form. A post-weld solution anneal redissolves them.
2. **Reduce carbon**: "L" grades (304L, 316L, < 0.03 % C) have too little C to form much carbide.
3. **Stabilise**: add **Ti or Nb** (321, 347). They are stronger carbide formers than Cr, so they take the carbon and leave the Cr in solution.
