# Corrosion Protection

## The five routes (lecture summary)

**Materials selection | coatings | design | cathodic/anodic protection | environmental control**

## Electrochemical protection

| Method | Principle | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Sacrificial anode** (the slide labels it "anodic protection") | connect a **more active** metal (Mg, Zn, Al) that corrodes instead; the structure becomes the cathode | Mg anodes on buried pipelines, Zn anodes on ship hulls, galvanising |
| **Impressed-current cathodic protection** | a DC supply forces the structure to be the **cathode**; an inert or scrap anode is consumed | offshore platforms, pipelines, reinforced concrete |

Strictly, both are forms of *cathodic* protection. In other texts "anodic protection" means holding a passivating metal in its passive range with a potentiostat.

## Barrier and coating routes

- **Paints** work mainly as high-resistance barriers to ionic and electronic current, not as perfect oxygen seals.
- **Metallic coatings**: sacrificial (Zn on steel) or noble (Cr, Ni plating). Noble coatings are dangerous if scratched, because they create a small exposed anode.
- **Conversion coatings**: anodising Al and Ti thickens the protective oxide.
- **Coherent native oxides**: choose Cr > 12 %, Al or Ti.

## Design and detailing

- Know where the anode is; **avoid small anode / large cathode**.
- Avoid crevices, sharp edges and water traps; add drainage holes.
- Welded joints rather than bolted (no crevices), with weld decay managed.
- Avoid dissimilar-metal contact, or insulate it.
- **Seal the system**: once the trapped $\mathrm O_2$ is used up, corrosion stops (central-heating circuits).

## Environmental control

Temperature, flow velocity, dissolved $\mathrm O_2$, ion concentration, **inhibitors**, cleaning.
