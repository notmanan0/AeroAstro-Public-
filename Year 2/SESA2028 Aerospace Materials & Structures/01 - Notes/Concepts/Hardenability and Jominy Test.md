# Hardenability and Jominy Test

![[Figures/materials_jominy_readoff_2023_24.png]]

**Hardenability** is a steel's ability to form martensite **to depth**, through the whole section, on quenching. It is **not** the same as hardness. Maximum hardness depends mainly on C content; hardenability depends on alloying and austenite grain size.

## Jominy end-quench test

1. Austenitise a standard bar (25 mm diameter × 100 mm).
2. Spray water on **one end only**. The cooling rate falls from about 225 °C/s at the quenched end to about 2 °C/s at the far end.
3. Grind a flat along the bar (to get below the decarburised skin) and measure hardness against distance.

- A flat curve means high hardenability (alloy steels, e.g. 4340).
- A steeply falling curve means low hardenability (plain C).

## Using Jominy data

Every Jominy distance corresponds to a cooling rate, which matches particular positions in real bars quenched in particular media:

- 9.8 mm Jominy = centre of a **28 mm oil-quenched** bar. Martensite there means a 28 mm bar through-hardens in oil.
- 25 mm Jominy = centre of a 75 mm bar = ½R of a 100 mm bar.

**2023-24 MQ2(ii), 50 mm oil-quenched bar:** surface 7 mm → 53.5 HRC; ¾R 11.5 mm → 48.5; ½R 14 mm → 45.5; centre 15.5 mm → 43.5 HRC.

## Applications

- **Quality control**: batch-to-batch composition consistency.
- **Selection**: together with CCT diagrams, pick a steel and quench medium that will harden the section **without** needing a drastic quench. Very fast quenches of large parts cause residual stress, distortion and quench cracking.

## Raising hardenability

Alloy with Mn, Mo, Cr, Ni, V, B (these shift the ferrite and pearlite curves to the right); raise the austenitising temperature (coarser $\gamma$ grains, fewer nucleation sites). Beyond about 0.6 % C, more carbon retards martensite completion (retained austenite).
