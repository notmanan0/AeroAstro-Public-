# Hardenability and Jominy Test

Hardenability is how easily a steel forms martensite through the whole section of a part, and the Jominy end-quench test measures it by quenching one end of a standard austenitised bar and recording hardness against distance from that end.

Hardenability is not hardness. The hardness of martensite depends mainly on its carbon; hardenability depends on how slowly the steel can be cooled and still miss the ferrite and pearlite noses, which alloying and austenite grain size control. In the Jominy test the water jet cools the quenched end at about 225 °C/s and the far end at about 2 °C/s, so one bar samples a whole range of cooling rates; a flat is ground to get below the decarburised skin before hardness is measured. A plain carbon steel's curve drops steeply from the quenched end; a well-alloyed steel's stays high a long way along.

![[materials_jominy_readoff_2023_24.png|750]]

Look-up charts link each Jominy distance to a position in a real bar of a given diameter and quench medium. For example, 9.8 mm from the quenched end matches the centre of a 28 mm bar quenched in oil, so a steel fully martensitic at that distance will through-harden such a bar; the centre of a 75 mm bar matches 25 mm. Surfaces cool faster than centres, and thin bars faster than thick ones. The data are used for quality control between batches and, with CCT diagrams, to choose steel, section and quench medium together.

Carbon (up to about 0.6 %), manganese, nickel, vanadium, chromium and molybdenum all raise hardenability by slowing the diffusional ferrite and pearlite transformation, and a higher austenitising temperature does the same by growing the austenite grains, which removes nucleation sites. Higher hardenability lets a gentler quench, such as oil instead of water, produce martensite, which matters because very fast quenching of large or complex parts causes residual stress, distortion and quench cracking.
