# Martensite

**Martensite** forms when austenite ($\gamma$, FCC, with C in solution) is cooled **faster than the critical cooling rate**, so the carbon has no time to diffuse out to form ferrite and cementite.

## Mechanism

- A **diffusionless, displacive (shear)** transformation: atoms move cooperatively by less than one atomic spacing, close to the speed of sound. The lecturer's image is soldiers wheeling on a parade ground.
- **Bain strain**: a body-centred tetragonal (BCT) cell can be drawn within two FCC cells. It contracts along one axis ($z$) and expands along the other two.
- **Carbon is trapped** in octahedral interstices, which makes the cell tetragonal ($c/a$ grows with C).
- Morphology: fine **laths** (low C) or plates (high C), with alternating shear directions and **very high dislocation density**.

## Why hard and brittle

Supersaturated carbon distorts the lattice, and the dense dislocation substructure (from accommodating the shape change) blocks further slip. So hardness is very high (it rises steeply with %C, about 4× pearlite at the same C), but there is little ductility or toughness, plus residual stresses from the volume increase.

## Athermal

The transformation starts at $M_s$ and is complete at $M_f$. The fraction formed depends on **the temperature reached, not the holding time**. High C and many alloying elements lower $M_s$ and $M_f$, which can leave **retained austenite**.

## Making it useful

**Temper** it ([[Tempering]]). Surface-only martensite (carburising, nitriding, induction hardening) gives a hard case over a tough core.

## Ti martensite (2024-25 MQ1(c))

Also diffusionless shear ($\beta\to\alpha'$), but HCP and supersaturated with *substitutional* $\beta$ stabilisers rather than interstitial C, so it is only moderately stronger. Both are metastable phases that can be tempered or aged.
