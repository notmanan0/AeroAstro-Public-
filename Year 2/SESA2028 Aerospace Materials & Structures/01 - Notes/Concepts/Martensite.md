# Martensite

Martensite is the hard, brittle, body-centred tetragonal phase that forms when austenite is cooled too fast for carbon to diffuse, by a diffusionless, coordinated shear of the iron lattice that traps the carbon in solution.

On slow cooling, FCC austenite becomes BCC ferrite plus cementite by diffusion, the carbon moving out of the forming ferrite. Quenched fast enough, the carbon cannot move, yet the iron still changes structure: two FCC cells contain a body-centred cell, and a shear that compresses it along one axis turns it into a body-centred tetragonal cell. All the atoms move together, like soldiers wheeling on a parade ground, at close to the speed of sound. The shape change is taken up by many fine laths sheared in alternating directions, which also multiplies dislocations, so the product is highly distorted, highly dislocated and supersaturated with carbon: very hard and very brittle. At the same carbon content it is roughly four times as hard as pearlite, and its hardness rises steeply with carbon until, above about 0.6 % C, retained austenite starts to lower it.

The transformation is athermal. It starts at $M_s$ and finishes at $M_f$; how much martensite forms depends on how far below $M_s$ the steel is taken, not on how long it is held, and between the two temperatures austenite and martensite coexist. Bainite forms at intermediate rates by shear with some diffusion: upper bainite has carbides between its laths, lower bainite within them, and both are hard and look much like martensite.

As-quenched martensite is too brittle to use, so it is [[Tempering|tempered]]. Titanium alloys form an analogous $\alpha'$ martensite on quenching from $\beta$, but with no trapped interstitial carbon it is only moderately harder ([[Titanium Alloy Classes]]).
