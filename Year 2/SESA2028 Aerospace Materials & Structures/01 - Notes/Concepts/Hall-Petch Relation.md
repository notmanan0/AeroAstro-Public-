# Hall-Petch Relation

The Hall–Petch relation $\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k\,d^{-1/2}$ says that a metal's yield stress rises as its grain size $d$ falls, where $\sigma_0$ is the lattice's own resistance to dislocation motion and $k$ a material constant.

A dislocation exists only as a defect of a particular crystal lattice, so it cannot cross a grain boundary, where one lattice ends and another at a different orientation begins. Dislocations gliding on one slip plane pile up against the boundary and their stress fields add, concentrating stress on the neighbouring grain. Yield spreads across the boundary when that concentrated stress starts slip in the next grain. In a fine-grained metal the pile-ups are short, so less stress is concentrated and more applied stress is needed; the pile-up argument gives the inverse square-root dependence.

Grain refinement is the one strengthening mechanism that also improves toughness: finer grains limit the slip-band pile-ups that can start cleavage and lower the ductile–brittle transition temperature ([[Ductile-Brittle Transition]]). Grain size is controlled by working and recrystallisation, by grain-refining additions such as zirconium in magnesium alloys, and in steels by the heat treatments of [[SESA2028 M7 - Steels - Phase Transformations, Heat Treatment and Alloying|M7]]. HCP metals have large $k$, so it matters especially for magnesium: with $\sigma_0\approx10$ MPa and $k\approx0.28$ MPa√m (illustrative), going from 50 μm to 10 μm grains raises the yield stress from about 50 to 99 MPa. At high temperature the effect reverses, because grain boundaries become paths for diffusion and sliding, which is why creep-resistant turbine blades remove them altogether ([[Creep Curve and Mechanisms]]).
