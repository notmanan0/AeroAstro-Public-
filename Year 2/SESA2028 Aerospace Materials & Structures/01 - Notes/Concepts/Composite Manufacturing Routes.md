# Composite Manufacturing Routes

Composite manufacturing routes are the ways of combining fibres and resin into a shaped part; because a composite's material is made at the same time as the structure, the route sets its void content, fibre fraction, alignment and cost.

Hand lay-up is the cheap, flexible craft route: a gel coat for the surface finish, dry cloth laid in an open mould, resin poured on and worked in with a roller to drive out air, and excess scraped off. It suits complex one-off shapes but is slow, operator-dependent and leaves high void content and resin-rich regions. Pultrusion pulls fibres through a resin bath and a heated die; it is fast and automated but gives only constant cross-sections such as rods, pipes and I-beams. Resin transfer moulding injects resin into a dry preform in a closed two-part mould, giving better infiltration and quality in large parts, but the moulds are expensive and need production volume. Autoclave moulding of prepreg, cloth pre-impregnated with partially cured resin and stored frozen, under a vacuum bag with heat and pressure gives void contents below 1 % and is the aerospace standard, at high cost. Compression moulding in heated two-part moulds also consolidates well, for parts such as helicopter and turbine blades. Filament winding lays resin-wet fibre helically or circumferentially on a mandrel, quickly and at high fibre fraction, for pipes and pressure vessels such as carbon over an aluminium liner for hydrogen, but it is limited in shape.

The quality issues common to all routes are voids, resin-rich areas of low fibre fraction, and the fibre–matrix bond, which depends on the proprietary fibre sizing. Cost includes equipment, time and skill, and disposal or recycling at end of life, not just raw materials. For very large single parts, such as the A380 rear pressure bulkhead, prepreg and autoclaves become impractical and infusion routes such as resin film infusion take over.
