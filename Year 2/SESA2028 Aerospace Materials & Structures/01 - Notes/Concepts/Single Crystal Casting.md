# Single Crystal Casting

## Why remove grain boundaries?

At turbine-blade temperatures, grain boundaries are where **creep sliding, cavitation and oxidation** happen. Blade development has therefore gone:

1. **Equiaxed** polycrystal: boundaries in every direction.
2. **Directionally solidified (DS)**: columnar grains **parallel to the centrifugal stress**, so there is no resolved shear across boundaries and no transverse boundaries.
3. **Single crystal (SX)**: **no grain boundaries at all**.

## Extra benefits of SX

- The boundary-strengthening elements (C, B, Zr, Hf), which lower the melting point, can be removed. That raises the incipient-melting temperature and allows a higher solution heat treatment, so **more and finer $\gamma'$**.
- Growth along ⟨001⟩ gives a **low elastic modulus** along the blade axis, which reduces thermal stress and improves thermal-fatigue life.

## Investment ("lost wax") casting route

1. Make a detailed **wax pattern** around ceramic (silica) **cores** that form the cooling passages.
2. Assemble patterns onto a wax "tree" with runners.
3. **Dip** repeatedly in ceramic slurry and stucco to build a shell.
4. **Dewax** in a furnace and fire the shell.
5. **Pour** the superalloy in vacuum.
6. **Withdraw the mould slowly** from the hot zone for a controlled temperature gradient, giving directional solidification from a chill plate. A **helical "pigtail" grain selector** (a "multiple-turn constriction") lets only one grain through into the blade: a **single crystal**.
7. Leach out the cores chemically, then heat treat, coat and machine the fir-tree root.

## Limitations

Expensive; defects such as stray grains and freckles; anisotropic properties; low-angle boundaries; cannot be welded easily.

Related: Whittle needed 650 °C at 200 MPa and Nichrome led to modern alloys. Gas temperatures now exceed 1200 °C, above the melting point, thanks to SX + cooling + TBC.
