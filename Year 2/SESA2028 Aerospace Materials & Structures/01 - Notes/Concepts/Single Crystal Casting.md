# Single Crystal Casting

Single-crystal casting makes a turbine blade with no grain boundaries at all, by investment casting it, solidifying it directionally from one end, and letting a helical grain selector pass only one grain into the blade.

Grain boundaries are where creep is worst, because diffusion along them lets them slide and cavitate, and where oxidation attacks. Blades therefore moved from equiaxed grains, to directionally solidified columnar grains whose boundaries run parallel to the centrifugal stress and so carry no resolved shear, to single crystals with no boundaries, which give the best creep resistance.

The shape comes from investment, or lost-wax, casting. A wax pattern of the blade is made with silica cores where the internal cooling channels will be, and many patterns are assembled on a tree with a runner system. Repeated dipping in ceramic slurry and drying builds a shell; the wax is melted out in a furnace, leaving a mould ready for the molten alloy. After pouring, the mould is withdrawn slowly from the furnace so solidification starts at the base and moves upwards, which gives columnar grains. A pigtail, a multiple-turn helical constriction between the chill and the blade, lets only one grain orientation through, so the whole blade grows as a single crystal. The cores are dissolved out afterwards.

Removing grain boundaries also removes the need for boundary-strengthening additions, and the finished blade is then internally cooled and given a [[Thermal Barrier Coatings|thermal barrier coating]]. Together these let gas temperatures exceed the alloy's melting point.
