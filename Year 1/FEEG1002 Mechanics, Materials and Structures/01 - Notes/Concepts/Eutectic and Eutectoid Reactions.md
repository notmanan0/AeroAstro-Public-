# Eutectic and Eutectoid Reactions

A eutectic reaction is a liquid turning on cooling into two different solids at a single temperature and composition, $L\rightarrow\alpha+\beta$; a eutectoid reaction is the solid-state counterpart, one solid turning into two others, $\gamma\rightarrow\alpha+\mathrm{Fe_3C}$ in steel.

Both happen at an invariant point where three phases meet. In lead–tin the eutectic point is 61.9 wt% Sn at 183 °C:

$$
L(61.9\ \text{wt\% Sn})\ \xrightarrow{183\,^\circ\text{C}}\ \alpha(18.3\ \text{wt\% Sn})+\beta(97.8\ \text{wt\% Sn})
$$

The eutectic alloy melts and freezes at one temperature, lower than either pure metal, with no pasty range, which is why it is used as solder. Because the liquid has to split into two solids of very different composition, and atoms can diffuse only short distances in the time available, the two phases grow side by side as thin alternating plates, the lamellar eutectic structure. In steel the eutectoid is at 0.76 wt% C and 727 °C, and the same short-range diffusion gives pearlite, alternating plates of ferrite and cementite.

![[m6_eutectic_microstructures.png|900]]

An alloy off the eutectic composition starts to freeze as one phase on its own, the primary (or proeutectic) phase, and the remaining liquid moves along the liquidus to the eutectic composition; at the eutectic temperature that liquid then turns into eutectic. Hypoeutectic alloys (left of the eutectic) give primary α in a eutectic matrix, hypereutectic alloys primary β. The amounts come from two different tie lines: primary phase and eutectic just above the eutectic temperature, using the line from the α limit to the eutectic composition, and total α and β just below it, using the line across the whole α + β field. Hypo- and hypereutectoid steels work in exactly the same way, with proeutectoid ferrite or cementite in place of the primary phase.
