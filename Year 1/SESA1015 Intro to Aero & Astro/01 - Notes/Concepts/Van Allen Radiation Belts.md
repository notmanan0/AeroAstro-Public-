# Van Allen Radiation Belts

The Van Allen belts are two doughnut-shaped regions around the Earth in which charged particles are trapped by the geomagnetic field: an inner belt of high-energy protons ($E\geq100$ MeV) at about 1.2 to 2.5 Earth radii from the centre and an outer belt of energetic electrons ($E>0.5$ MeV) at about 3 to 7 Earth radii.

The Earth's field is roughly a dipole, compressed on the day side by the solar wind and drawn out into a magnetotail on the night side. A charged particle spirals around a field line, and as it moves towards a pole the field strengthens until the particle is reflected at a mirror point; it bounces between hemispheres and drifts around the Earth, so the population fills a shell. The belts change with magnetic storms driven by the solar wind.

The belts set the radiation dose, the energy deposited per unit mass of material. The rad(Si) is 100 erg per gram of silicon, so $1\ \text{rad}=10^{-5}\ \text{J}/10^{-3}\ \text{kg}=0.01$ J/kg and 100 rad $=1$ Gy. Behind 5 mm of aluminium the yearly dose is about 20 rad for a 500 km, 28° orbit (under the inner belt), about $6\times10^2$ rad at 700 km polar (through the outer-belt horns near the poles), about $6\times10^3$ rad at GEO (in the outer belt) and about $10^4$ rad for a highly elliptic orbit that crosses both. A ten-year GEO mission therefore accumulates about 60 krad(Si), 600 J/kg. Shielding stops the electrons efficiently, so the first few millimetres of aluminium cut the dose by orders of magnitude, but the remaining dose from energetic protons and bremsstrahlung falls only slowly with more thickness, and each millimetre costs 2.7 kg/m². Beyond that, radiation-tolerant electronics, error correction and redundancy are the response. The other effects of the particles are single event upsets, solar-cell degradation and spacecraft charging.

The belts also shape the orbit zones: the 2000 km top of LEO keeps orbits below the inner belt, and MEO navigation constellations sit between the belts' peaks ([[Earth Orbit Zones]]).
