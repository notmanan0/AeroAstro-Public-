# SESA3047 1.1 - Dynamic Systems and Control Architectures

> [!abstract] Summary
> An aerospace vehicle is a physical object and a dynamic system whose position, velocity, attitude and internal quantities change with time. A control system changes that behaviour to meet an objective, while a model represents only the aspects needed for a stated task. Passive control relies on inherent physics; active control uses sensing, decision-making and powered actuation. Open-loop control does not use the resulting output, feedback corrects measured error, and feedforward anticipates a known command or measured disturbance. The complete plant, actuators, sensors and controller must be analysed as one interacting dynamic system.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** you must identify the parts of a control system or choose between open-loop, feedback and feedforward.
> - **Assumptions:** block-diagram description; negative feedback with $e=r-y_m$.
> - **Method:** 1. Identify plant, actuator, sensor and controller. 2. Label $r$, $e$, $u_c$, $y$, $y_m$, $d$. 3. Classify the architecture. 4. Predict the response to a disturbance and to model error. 5. Compare steady-state error between architectures.
> - **Common mistakes:** confusing the plant output $y$ with the measured output $y_m$; claiming feedforward rejects an **unmeasured** disturbance.
> - **Practise:** [[#9. Worked example: three architectures on one plant (suggested)]] · [[SESA3047 Mock Questions - Chapters 1-4#Q1. Classifying and linearising a drone altitude model|Mock Q1]]

## The topic in one physical picture

Picture the lecture's example: an aircraft is asked to raise its nose to a new pitch attitude, and a gust nudges it partway through (L2, ll. 249–256). Three questions decide what happens.

1. **What is being moved?** The aircraft is the *plant*. Its attitude and rates change with time, so it is a *dynamic system*. The equations we write for it are a *model*, never the aircraft itself (ll. 59–69).
2. **Who pushes, and with what?** The controller decides, the actuator (a control surface) turns that decision into a force, and the gust pushes as well, uncommanded.
3. **Does anything look at the result?** If nothing looks (**open loop**), the gust's push stays in the final attitude. If a sensor measures the attitude and the controller acts on the difference (**feedback**), the push is corrected after it appears. If a sensor sees the gust coming and the controller acts first (**feedforward**), the push is cancelled before it shows.

**Passive** control is whatever the airframe's own geometry, mass distribution and damping do with no sensing at all. **Active** control is everything above that needs power, sensors and a controller. The sections below name each piece of this picture. [[#9. Worked example: three architectures on one plant (suggested)|§9]] then puts numbers on it.

> [!tip] One sentence to remember
> Open loop *hopes*, feedback *corrects*, feedforward *anticipates* (L2, ll. 295–296, 300–306).

## 1. Why dynamics and control matter

Aircraft and spacecraft must remain safe, stable and reliable while they:

- track a trajectory or commanded attitude;
- hold altitude, speed or pointing direction;
- reject disturbances such as gusts, turbulence, gravity-gradient torque or solar-radiation pressure;
- operate with uncertain parameters, sensor errors and actuator limits.

A spacecraft attitude error may misalign antennas, optical sensors or solar panels. An aircraft motion error may compromise trajectory, passenger comfort, structural loads or stability. Control is therefore part of the vehicle's dynamics, not an accessory added after the dynamics are understood.

## 2. System, dynamic system and model

A **system** is a collection of interacting elements considered as one whole. A **dynamic system** has quantities that evolve with time in response to inputs, forces, moments, initial conditions and disturbances.

An aerospace abstraction is

$$
\text{input/command}+\text{disturbance}
\longrightarrow \text{vehicle dynamics}
\longrightarrow \text{observable response}.
$$

Examples of evolving quantities include:

| Aircraft | Spacecraft |
|---|---|
| position, speed, attitude, angular velocity | orbit, attitude, angular velocity, reaction-wheel speed, propellant mass |

A **model** is a purposeful representation of selected behaviour under stated assumptions. It is not the physical system. The model may omit structural flexibility, thermal behaviour or component detail if those effects do not matter for the current question.

## 3. Passive and active control

### Passive control

Passive control modifies response through inherent mechanical properties or natural environmental interaction. It needs no continuous measurement, real-time decision or commanded actuation during normal operation.

Examples:

- structural damping treatments, isolators and tuned mass absorbers;
- aircraft geometry, centre-of-mass location and aerodynamic surfaces establishing inherent stability;
- spacecraft spin stabilisation and passive nutation damping.

### Active control

Active control uses external power and commanded actuation. A typical system contains sensors, a controller and actuators.

Examples:

- flight-control computers commanding aerodynamic surfaces;
- reaction wheels, control-moment gyroscopes, magnetic torquers or thrusters for spacecraft attitude;
- force actuators for active vibration suppression.

Passive and active approaches may coexist. Physical design establishes the baseline dynamics; active control modifies those dynamics to meet tighter requirements.

> [!important] Design order
> In an active system, stability is a prerequisite. Performance targets such as tracking speed or disturbance rejection matter only after the closed system is stable.

## 4. Anatomy of an active aerospace control system

For negative feedback, the information path is

$$
r\longrightarrow \boxed{\Sigma}\longrightarrow e
\longrightarrow \text{controller}
\longrightarrow \text{actuator}
\longrightarrow \text{plant}
\longrightarrow y,
$$

with the measured output returned through the sensor as the **feedback signal**. The handout calls the $\Sigma$ node the **comparison point** (Chapter 1, p. 4). In the simplest sign convention,

$$
\boxed{e=r-y_m}.
$$

The rows follow the handout's list of elements, in its order and with its names (Chapter 1, pp. 3–4):

| Element | Role | Aerospace examples |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | Physical system to be controlled | aircraft, spacecraft, flexible structure |
| Reference input $r$ | Desired behaviour | pitch angle, altitude, attitude, angular rate |
| Error signal $e$ | Difference between the reference input and the feedback signal | $e=r-y_m$ |
| Controller | Determines the required action | flight-control computer, attitude-control law |
| Control signal | Information command sent to actuator | voltage or digital command |
| Actuator | Converts command into physical action | control-surface servo, reaction wheel, thruster |
| Control forces | Physical forces and moments applied to the plant | aerodynamic moment, wheel torque, thrust |
| External disturbances | Uncommanded external influences on the plant | gust, turbulence, radiation pressure |
| Output $y$ | Selected plant response | position, velocity, attitude |
| Measured output $y_m$ | Sensor representation of output | may include noise, bias, delay, limited resolution |
| Sensor | Converts a physical response into usable information | IMU, gyro, accelerometer, Pitot/air-data probe |
| Feedback signal | Information returned by the sensor to the comparison point | $y_m$, compared with $r$ to form $e$ |

> [!warning] Signal versus force
> A control signal is information. The actuator turns it into the force or moment that changes the plant motion.

### Aircraft pitch example

1. The pilot or autopilot requests a new pitch attitude $r$.
2. The controller forms the error from the measured attitude or pitch rate.
3. The actuator moves a control surface.
4. Aerodynamic forces and moments change the aircraft motion.
5. Sensors measure the response and close the loop.
6. A gust enters as an external disturbance.

The same logic applies to spacecraft attitude, although the sensors and actuators change.

## 5. Open-loop control

Open-loop control does not measure the resulting plant output to modify the command:

$$
\text{command}\rightarrow\text{controller}\rightarrow\text{actuator}\rightarrow\text{plant}\rightarrow y.
$$

The action may follow a time schedule, prescribed command or predictive model. The handout notes that open-loop control is *"sometimes referred to as predetermined or programmed control"* (Chapter 1, p. 5).

Examples:

- fire a spacecraft thruster for a calculated duration without measuring the achieved attitude change;
- move a control surface according to a predetermined schedule without using aircraft motion to correct it;
- the lecture's everyday case: a **scheduled home thermostat** that sets different temperatures at different times of day without measuring the room temperature.

> [!example] Lecture gust example
> An aircraft commands a pitch change while a gust adds about $5^\circ$ partway through. Open loop applies the pre-computed control action anyway and overshoots the target by the gust's contribution. Feedback measures the $5^\circ$ already present and applies a correspondingly smaller action, so it still reaches the target. Disturbance rejection is the defining advantage of closing the loop (L2, ll. 249–256).

**Advantages:** simple, direct and potentially adequate for predictable short-duration tasks.

**Limitations:** cannot correct modelling error, parameter uncertainty, actuator imperfection or unmeasured disturbance after the action begins.

## 6. Closed-loop feedback control

Feedback measures the plant response, compares it with the reference and uses the error to adjust later control action.

This can:

- improve tracking;
- reduce sensitivity to disturbances and parameter uncertainty;
- change the apparent damping, stiffness and natural response of the plant.

Feedback is not automatically beneficial. Delay, noise, saturation or poor controller design can cause excessive actuation, oscillation or instability.

## 7. Feedforward control

Feedforward uses a known command or a measured disturbance to act **before** the disturbance creates an output error. It is anticipatory rather than corrective.

Example: a forward-looking radar detects rising terrain. A feedforward path commands the required manoeuvre before the aircraft reaches it. Altitude feedback then removes residual error due to imperfect modelling or actuation.

Feedforward commonly needs:

- a measurement of the disturbance or preview signal;
- a model that maps that signal to a suitable control action.

It may be combined with either an open-loop or feedback architecture.

## 8. Comparison

The columns follow the handout's comparison table (Chapter 1, p. 7):

| Arrangement | How the control action is determined | Main consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Open loop | uses a prescribed command or schedule; the plant output is not used to modify the action | simple and direct, but unable to correct output errors |
| Feedback | uses the measured plant output and compares it with the reference input | corrects deviations and rejects disturbances, but needs careful design to avoid stability issues (noise, delay, saturation) |
| Feedforward | uses the known or measured disturbances (or a command preview) to anticipate the required action | acts before an output error develops, but depends strongly on model and measurement accuracy |
| Combined | feedforward anticipates the required action; feedback corrects the residual error | combines anticipatory and corrective control, but needs a more complex architecture with more interactions to validate |

## 9. Worked example: three architectures on one plant (suggested)

> [!info] Status
> **Suggested, not lecturer-set.** Chapter 1 and L2 describe the architectures in words only and use no equations. This example puts numbers on three lectured claims: open loop cannot remove a disturbance (L2, ll. 243–248); feedback measures it and corrects (ll. 253–256); feedforward acts before an output error develops (ll. 292–296). It also checks the handout's remark that feedback can *"modify the apparent dynamic behaviour of the plant"* (Chapter 1, p. 6). The first-order equation below is a deliberately simple **model** of roll-rate response. In the lecture's terms, it is not the aircraft (L2, ll. 59–69).

### Set-up

Model the aircraft's roll-rate response to aileron as a first-order system:

$$
\tau\dot y+y=Ku+d.
$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---|
| $y$ | roll rate (output) | starts at 0 |
| $u$ | aileron deflection (control) | set by the controller |
| $d$ | steady roll rate the gust alone would produce (disturbance) | 0, or $+5$ °/s from $t=1.5$ s |
| $\tau$ | time constant of the plant | 0.5 s |
| $K$ | true plant gain | 8 (°/s)/°, or 6.4 if the model is 20% wrong |
| $\hat K$ | the gain the controller's model assumes | 8 (°/s)/° |
| $r$ | reference roll rate | 20 °/s |

For a constant input, the steady state is reached when the rate stops changing. Set $\dot y=0$:

$$
y_{ss}=Ku+d.
$$

### (a) Open loop

The open-loop controller holds a model of the plant and inverts it to choose the command (L2, ll. 196–203):

$$
u=\frac{r}{\hat K}=\frac{20}{8}=2.5^\circ.
$$

Substitute into the steady state:

$$
y_{ss}=\frac{K}{\hat K}\,r+d.
$$

- Model exact, no gust: $y_{ss}=1\times20+0=20$ °/s.
- Model exact, gust: $y_{ss}=20+5=25$ °/s. The whole gust appears in the output, which is the lecture's *"the resulting state of the system will be 25"* (l. 251).
- Plant gain 20% low ($K=6.4$), no gust: $y_{ss}=0.8\times20=16$ °/s. The modelling error also passes straight through.

The command does not depend on $y$, so the dynamics are the plant's own, with time constant $\tau=0.5$ s.

### (b) Proportional feedback

The controller acts on the measured error, *"a proportional control signal"* (L2, l. 223):

$$
u=k_p(r-y),\qquad k_p=1.125\ ^\circ\text{ per }^\circ/\text{s}.
$$

Substitute into the plant:

$$
\tau\dot y+y=Kk_p(r-y)+d.
$$

Collect the $y$ terms on the left:

$$
\tau\dot y+(1+Kk_p)\,y=Kk_p\,r+d.
$$

Divide by $1+Kk_p$:

$$
\frac{\tau}{1+Kk_p}\,\dot y+y=\frac{Kk_p\,r+d}{1+Kk_p}.
$$

This has the same form as the plant, so read off the new time constant and steady state:

$$
\boxed{\tau_{cl}=\frac{\tau}{1+Kk_p},\qquad
y_{ss}=\frac{Kk_p\,r+d}{1+Kk_p}.}
$$

The steady error is

$$
e_{ss}=r-y_{ss}=\frac{(1+Kk_p)r-Kk_p r-d}{1+Kk_p}=\frac{r-d}{1+Kk_p}.
$$

With the nominal plant, the loop gain is $Kk_p=8\times1.125=9$:

$$
\tau_{cl}=\frac{0.5}{10}=0.05\ \mathrm s,
$$

$$
y_{ss}=\frac{9\times20+d}{10}=18.0\ ^\circ/\mathrm s\ (d=0),\quad 18.5\ ^\circ/\mathrm s\ (d=5).
$$

With the plant gain 20% low, $Kk_p=6.4\times1.125=7.2$:

$$
\tau_{cl}=\frac{0.5}{8.2}=0.0610\ \mathrm s,\qquad
y_{ss}=\frac{7.2\times20}{8.2}=17.56\ ^\circ/\mathrm s.
$$

### (c) Feedforward, alone and with feedback

A gust sensor measures $d$ (L2, ll. 267–270). The controller subtracts its predicted effect from the open-loop command and adds the feedback term:

$$
u=\frac{r-d}{\hat K}+k_p(r-y).
$$

Substitute and set $\dot y=0$:

$$
y_{ss}=\frac{K}{\hat K}(r-d)+Kk_p(r-y_{ss})+d.
$$

Collect $y_{ss}$:

$$
(1+Kk_p)\,y_{ss}=Kk_p\,r+\frac{K}{\hat K}(r-d)+d.
$$

If the model is exact, $K/\hat K=1$ and the $d$ terms cancel:

$$
(1+Kk_p)\,y_{ss}=Kk_p\,r+r=(1+Kk_p)\,r,
$$

$$
y_{ss}=r=20\ ^\circ/\mathrm s\quad\text{with or without the gust.}
$$

If the plant gain is 20% low and there is no gust:

$$
8.2\,y_{ss}=144+0.8\times20=160,
$$

$$
y_{ss}=19.51\ ^\circ/\mathrm s.
$$

**Feedforward alone** is the same law with $k_p=0$, so $y_{ss}=(K/\hat K)(r-d)+d$. It gives 20 °/s with the exact model, gust or not. With the 20% gain error it gives 16 °/s, exactly the open-loop result, because nothing measures the output.

### Summary

| Architecture | $\tau$ or $\tau_{cl}$ | Exact model, no gust | Exact model, gust $d=5$ | Gain 20% low, no gust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open loop | 0.5 s | 20 | **25** | 16 |
| Feedforward only | 0.5 s | 20 | 20 | 16 |
| Proportional feedback | 0.05 s (0.061 s) | 18 | 18.5 | 17.56 |
| Feedforward + feedback | 0.05 s (0.061 s) | 20 | 20 | 19.51 |

Steady roll rates in °/s; reference 20 °/s. Time constants in brackets are for the 20%-low plant.

![[amc_control_step_responses.png|900]]

> [!check] Numerical check
> `generate_amc_figures.py` (function `first_order_check`) evaluates the closed-form steady states above. It asserts 20, 25, 16 (open loop), 18, 18.5, 17.56098 (feedback), 20, 20, 16 (feedforward) and 20, 20, 19.51220 (feedforward + feedback), and $\tau_{cl}=0.05$ s and $0.5/8.2=0.06098$ s. It then integrates the nominal feedback loop with a $10^{-5}$ s explicit step, gust included. The result matches the exact piecewise-exponential solution plotted in the figure to within 0.01 °/s.

> [!tip] What the loop gain buys
> Every feedback result is divided by $1+Kk_p$: the time constant (ten times faster here), the effect of the gust (5 °/s becomes 0.5 °/s), and the sensitivity to the plant gain. A 20% drop in $K$ moves the open-loop output by 20% (20 to 16 °/s) but the feedback output by only 2.4% (18 to 17.56 °/s). This is the handout's *"reduce sensitivity to disturbances and uncertainty, and modify the apparent dynamic behaviour"* (Chapter 1, p. 6) in numbers. Feedforward changes none of the dynamics: its time constant stays at 0.5 s.

> [!warning] Feedback is not free
> - Pure proportional feedback leaves a steady error $(r-d)/(1+Kk_p)$, here 2 °/s with no gust. Removing it needs integral action, which is SESA2027 material ([[SESA2027 B1 - Control System Fundamentals and PID Control]]), not Chapter 1.
> - Raising $k_p$ shrinks the error but raises the demand on the actuator. At $t=0$ the feedback command is already $k_pr=22.5^\circ$ of aileron, close to a typical deflection limit. Saturation, sensor noise and delay are exactly what can make a high-gain loop oscillate or go unstable (L2, ll. 232–235; Chapter 1, p. 6). This first-order model, with no delay, cannot show that.
> - Feedforward is only as good as its model and its disturbance sensor. With the 20% gain error it alone leaves a 4 °/s error, and it would do nothing about an unmeasured disturbance.
