# SESA3047 1.1 - Dynamic Systems and Control Architectures

> [!abstract] Summary
> An aerospace vehicle is a physical object and a dynamic system whose position, velocity, attitude and internal quantities change with time. A control system changes that behaviour to meet an objective, while a model represents only the aspects needed for a stated task. Passive control relies on inherent physics; active control uses sensing, decision-making and powered actuation. Open-loop control does not use the resulting output, feedback corrects measured error, and feedforward anticipates a known command or measured disturbance. The complete plant, actuators, sensors and controller must be analysed as one interacting dynamic system.

## 1. Why dynamics and control matter

Aircraft and spacecraft must remain safe, stable and reliable while they:

- track a trajectory or commanded attitude;
- hold altitude, speed or pointing direction;
- reject disturbances such as gusts, turbulence, gravity-gradient torque or solar-radiation pressure;
- operate with uncertain parameters, sensor errors and actuator limits.

A spacecraft attitude error may misalign antennas, optical sensors or solar panels. An aircraft motion error may compromise trajectory, passenger comfort, structural loads or stability. Control is therefore part of the vehicle's dynamics, not an accessory added after the dynamics are understood.

## 2. System, dynamic system and model

A **system** is a collection of interacting elements considered as one whole. A **dynamic system** has quantities that evolve with time in response to inputs, forces, moments, initial conditions and disturbances.

An aerospace abstraction is

$$
\text{input/command}+\text{disturbance}
\longrightarrow \text{vehicle dynamics}
\longrightarrow \text{observable response}.
$$

Examples of evolving quantities include:

| Aircraft | Spacecraft |
|---|---|
| position, speed, attitude, angular velocity | orbit, attitude, angular velocity, reaction-wheel speed, propellant mass |

A **model** is a purposeful representation of selected behaviour under stated assumptions. It is not the physical system. The model may omit structural flexibility, thermal behaviour or component detail if those effects do not matter for the current question.

## 3. Passive and active control

### Passive control

Passive control modifies response through inherent mechanical properties or natural environmental interaction. It needs no continuous measurement, real-time decision or commanded actuation during normal operation.

Examples:

- structural damping treatments, isolators and tuned mass absorbers;
- aircraft geometry, centre-of-mass location and aerodynamic surfaces establishing inherent stability;
- spacecraft spin stabilisation and passive nutation damping.

### Active control

Active control uses external power and commanded actuation. A typical system contains sensors, a controller and actuators.

Examples:

- flight-control computers commanding aerodynamic surfaces;
- reaction wheels, control-moment gyroscopes, magnetic torquers or thrusters for spacecraft attitude;
- force actuators for active vibration suppression.

Passive and active approaches may coexist. Physical design establishes the baseline dynamics; active control modifies those dynamics to meet tighter requirements.

> [!important] Design order
> In an active system, stability is a prerequisite. Performance targets such as tracking speed or disturbance rejection matter only after the closed system is stable.

## 4. Anatomy of an active aerospace control system

For negative feedback, the information path is

$$
r\longrightarrow \boxed{\Sigma}\longrightarrow e
\longrightarrow \text{controller}
\longrightarrow \text{actuator}
\longrightarrow \text{plant}
\longrightarrow y,
$$

with measured output returned through the sensor. In the simplest sign convention,

$$
\boxed{e=r-y_m}.
$$

| Element | Role | Aerospace examples |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | Physical system to be controlled | aircraft, spacecraft, flexible structure |
| Reference input $r$ | Desired behaviour | pitch angle, altitude, attitude, angular rate |
| Error $e$ | Difference between reference and feedback | $r-y_m$ |
| Controller | Determines the required action | flight-control computer, attitude-control law |
| Control signal | Information command sent to actuator | voltage or digital command |
| Actuator | Converts command into physical action | control-surface servo, reaction wheel, thruster |
| Control force/moment | Physical action on plant | aerodynamic moment, wheel torque, thrust |
| Disturbance | Uncommanded influence | gust, turbulence, radiation pressure |
| Output $y$ | Selected plant response | position, velocity, attitude |
| Measured output $y_m$ | Sensor representation of output | may include noise, bias, delay, limited resolution |
| Sensor | Converts a physical response into usable information | IMU, gyro, accelerometer, Pitot/air-data probe |

> [!warning] Signal versus force
> A control signal is information. The actuator turns it into the force or moment that changes the plant motion.

### Aircraft pitch example

1. The pilot or autopilot requests a new pitch attitude $r$.
2. The controller forms the error from the measured attitude or pitch rate.
3. The actuator moves a control surface.
4. Aerodynamic forces and moments change the aircraft motion.
5. Sensors measure the response and close the loop.
6. A gust enters as an external disturbance.

The same logic applies to spacecraft attitude, although the sensors and actuators change.

## 5. Open-loop control

Open-loop control does not measure the resulting plant output to modify the command:

$$
\text{command}\rightarrow\text{controller}\rightarrow\text{actuator}\rightarrow\text{plant}\rightarrow y.
$$

The action may follow a time schedule, prescribed command or predictive model.

Examples:

- fire a spacecraft thruster for a calculated duration without measuring the achieved attitude change;
- move a control surface according to a predetermined schedule without using aircraft motion to correct it;
- the lecture's everyday case: a **scheduled home thermostat** that sets different temperatures at different times of day without measuring the room temperature.

> [!example] Lecture gust example
> An aircraft commands a pitch change while a gust adds about $5^\circ$ partway through. Open loop applies the pre-computed control action anyway and overshoots the target by the gust's contribution. Feedback measures the $5^\circ$ already present and applies a correspondingly smaller action, so it still reaches the target. Disturbance rejection is the defining advantage of closing the loop (L2, ll. 249–256).

**Advantages:** simple, direct and potentially adequate for predictable short-duration tasks.

**Limitations:** cannot correct modelling error, parameter uncertainty, actuator imperfection or unmeasured disturbance after the action begins.

## 6. Closed-loop feedback control

Feedback measures the plant response, compares it with the reference and uses the error to adjust later control action.

This can:

- improve tracking;
- reduce sensitivity to disturbances and parameter uncertainty;
- change the apparent damping, stiffness and natural response of the plant.

Feedback is not automatically beneficial. Delay, noise, saturation or poor controller design can cause excessive actuation, oscillation or instability.

## 7. Feedforward control

Feedforward uses a known command or a measured disturbance to act **before** the disturbance creates an output error. It is anticipatory rather than corrective.

Example: a forward-looking radar detects rising terrain. A feedforward path commands the required manoeuvre before the aircraft reaches it. Altitude feedback then removes residual error due to imperfect modelling or actuation.

Feedforward commonly needs:

- a measurement of the disturbance or preview signal;
- a model that maps that signal to a suitable control action.

It may be combined with either an open-loop or feedback architecture.

## 8. Comparison

| Arrangement | Information used | Strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open loop | command or schedule only | simple and direct | cannot correct output error |
| Feedback | measured plant output | corrects residual error and rejects disturbances | instability, noise and delay sensitivity |
| Feedforward | command preview or measured disturbance | acts before an output error develops | depends strongly on model and disturbance measurement |
| Combined | feedforward plus measured output | anticipatory action with corrective cleanup | more components and interactions to validate |
