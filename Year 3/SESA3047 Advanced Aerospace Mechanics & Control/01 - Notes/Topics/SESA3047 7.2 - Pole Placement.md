# SESA3047 7.2 - Pole Placement

> [!abstract] Summary
> Proportional state feedback $\mathbf u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x$ turns $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$ into $\dot{\mathbf x}=(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)\mathbf x$, so the closed-loop poles are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$. Pole placement chooses $\mathbf K$ by matching the coefficients of $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K))$ with those of the desired characteristic polynomial. With one input and a fully controllable system there are exactly as many gains as coefficients, so any set of poles (real or in conjugate pairs) can be reached with a unique $\mathbf K$; for Eq. 7.11, moving the poles from $-1,-2$ to $-2,-3$ needs $\mathbf K=[4\ \ 2]$. With several inputs there are more gains than equations, $\mathbf K$ is not unique, and the course turns to the linear-quadratic regulator (Chapter 8).

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** asked to design a proportional state-feedback controller that gives stated closed-loop eigenvalues, or to say whether pole placement can be used.
> - **Assumptions:** linear model about an equilibrium, $\mathbf x$ the perturbation; all states available for feedback (measured, or fully observable and estimated); fully controllable; single input for a unique $\mathbf K$; desired poles real or in conjugate pairs.
> - **Method:** 1. Check controllability: $\operatorname{rank}[\mathbf B\ \mathbf A\mathbf B\ \cdots]=n$. 2. Write $\mathbf K=[k_1\ \cdots\ k_n]$ and form $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$. 3. Expand $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K))$. 4. Expand $\prod(\lambda-\lambda_i)$ for the desired poles. 5. Match coefficients of each power of $\lambda$ and solve for the $k_i$; check with the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$.
> - **Common mistakes:** forgetting the minus sign in $\mathbf u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x$ (it flips every gain); giving $\mathbf K$ the wrong size ($\mathbf B\mathbf K$ must be $n\times n$, so $\mathbf K$ is $m\times n$).
> - **Practise:** [[#2. A two-state example (7.11–7.15)]] · [[#Practice problem 1: stabilising an unstable system]] · [[SESA3047 Cambridge Solutions - 7 Controllability, Observability and Pole Placement#3F2 2019 Q1 - Aircraft pole placement with thrust|Cambridge 3F2 2019 Q1]]

Pole placement is the module's first control design technique. It uses the full state: every state is multiplied by a gain and fed back to the actuator. The idea is a direct extension of the proportional control of [[SESA2027 B1 - Control System Fundamentals and PID Control|SESA2027 B1]], but where a proportional controller there multiplied one error signal by one gain, the state-feedback controller has a gain for every state. Those gains change the state matrix itself, and with it the eigenvalues, which [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability|5.4]] showed decide stability, speed and damping.

Whether this works depends on controllability ([[SESA3047 7.1 - Controllability and Observability|7.1]]). If the system is fully controllable and has a single input, the closed-loop poles can be put anywhere, and the gains that do it are unique. The method also needs every state to be available, which is why the module assumes full observability. With more than one input, the same calculation leaves free gains over, and another method has to pick them.

## 1. Proportional state feedback (7.10)

### Assumptions

- A linear time-invariant model $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$ about an equilibrium, with $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf u$ the perturbations from trim.
- Every state is available to the controller.
- The aim is regulation: return the state to the equilibrium $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ (there is no reference input).

### The closed-loop state matrix

Proportional state feedback sets the input to a constant matrix times the state:

$$
\mathbf u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x .
$$

Substitute into the state equation (Eq. 7.10):

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u=\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf B\mathbf K\mathbf x=(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)\,\mathbf x.\qquad(7.10)
$$

The closed loop is again a free system, with $\mathbf A$ replaced by $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$. The control has, in effect, modified the state matrix, so the closed-loop poles are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$ and the closed-loop response is $\mathbf x(t)=e^{(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)t}\mathbf x_0$.

For the product $\mathbf B\mathbf K$ to have the same size as $\mathbf A$, $n\times n$, with $\mathbf B$ of size $n\times m$, the gain matrix must be $m\times n$: one row per input, one column per state. With a single input, $\mathbf K$ is a row vector of $n$ gains.

## 2. A two-state example (7.11–7.15)

Consider (Eq. 7.11)

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}u.\qquad(7.11)
$$

The open-loop characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2+3\lambda+2=(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)$, so the eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$. The system is already stable, since both lie in the left half-plane. The aim is to make it faster by moving them to $-2$ and $-3$.

The first step is to check that this is possible. The controllability matrix is $\mathcal C=[\mathbf B\ \ \mathbf A\mathbf B]=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&-3\end{bmatrix}$ (Eq. 7.12), with determinant $-1$, so its rank is 2 and the system is fully controllable ([[SESA3047 7.1 - Controllability and Observability#Worked example: the Eq. 7.11 system|7.1 §3]]).

With one input and two states, $\mathbf K=[k_1\ \ k_2]\in\mathbb R^{1\times2}$. Form the product:

$$
\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1&k_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\k_1&k_2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Subtract it from $\mathbf A$:

$$
\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2-k_1&-3-k_2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&-1\\2+k_1&\lambda+3+k_2\end{bmatrix},
$$

and its determinant is the closed-loop characteristic polynomial (Eq. 7.13):

$$
|\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)|=\lambda(\lambda+3+k_2)-(-1)(2+k_1)=\lambda^2+(3+k_2)\lambda+(2+k_1).\qquad(7.13)
$$

The desired poles $-2$ and $-3$ have the characteristic polynomial (Eq. 7.14)

$$
(\lambda+2)(\lambda+3)=\lambda^2+5\lambda+6.\qquad(7.14)
$$

Equate the coefficients of Eqs. 7.13 and 7.14 power by power (Eq. 7.15):

$$
\lambda^1:\ 3+k_2=5\ \Rightarrow\ k_2=2,\qquad\lambda^0:\ 2+k_1=6\ \Rightarrow\ k_1=4,\qquad\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.15)
$$

> [!check] Closed-loop eigenvalues
> $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-6&-5\end{bmatrix}$, with trace $-5$ and determinant $6$, so $\lambda^2+5\lambda+6=0$ and $\lambda=-2,-3$.

![[amc_ch7_pole_placement_responses.png|900]]

Started from $\mathbf x_0=[1,0]^T$, the closed loop settles faster than the open loop (left panel). The price is the control effort: $u=-4x_1-2x_2$ starts at $-4$ and decays with the state.

The control law here is $u=-4x_1-2x_2$. Read against Eq. 7.11, where $x_2=\dot x_1$, it is proportional-plus-derivative control of $x_1$: $k_1$ adds stiffness (the constant term of the polynomial) and $k_2$ adds damping (the $\lambda$ coefficient).

## 3. Why full controllability allows any poles

The example worked because each gain landed in its own coefficient. That is not a coincidence of Eq. 7.11.

### Systems already in companion form

Eq. 7.11 has the companion (controllable canonical) structure: ones on the superdiagonal, the negated coefficients of the characteristic polynomial in the last row, and the input entering only the last state. For $n=2$,

$$
\mathbf A_c=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-a_0&-a_1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A_c)=\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_0 .
$$

Feedback with $\mathbf K_c=[k_1\ \ k_2]$ changes only the last row:

$$
\mathbf A_c-\mathbf B_c\mathbf K_c=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-(a_0+k_1)&-(a_1+k_2)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det\big(\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A_c-\mathbf B_c\mathbf K_c)\big)=\lambda^2+(a_1+k_2)\lambda+(a_0+k_1).
$$

Each gain shifts exactly one coefficient, so any desired polynomial $\lambda^2+a_1'\lambda+a_0'$ is reached with $k_1=a_0'-a_0$ and $k_2=a_1'-a_1$, uniquely. The same holds for any $n$: $n$ gains, $n$ coefficients, one each.

### Any controllable single-input system

A fully controllable single-input system can always be brought to companion form by a change of state variables $\mathbf x=\mathbf T\mathbf z$, and the transformation is built from the controllability matrix. For $n=2$, with $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=\lambda^2+a_1\lambda+a_0$,

$$
\mathbf T=\mathcal C\begin{bmatrix}a_1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},
$$

and then $\mathbf T^{-1}\mathbf A\mathbf T=\mathbf A_c$ and $\mathbf T^{-1}\mathbf B=\mathbf B_c$. $\mathbf T$ is invertible exactly when $\mathcal C$ is, which is where controllability enters. In the new variables the gains are found as above, and $\mathbf u=-\mathbf K_c\mathbf z=-\mathbf K_c\mathbf T^{-1}\mathbf x$, so

$$
\mathbf K=\mathbf K_c\mathbf T^{-1}.
$$

For practice problem 1 below, $\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $a_1=-6$, $a_0=13$, and $\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}0&5\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathbf T=\begin{bmatrix}0&5\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-6&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\-4&1\end{bmatrix}$, and indeed $\mathbf T^{-1}\mathbf A\mathbf T=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-13&6\end{bmatrix}$. Collected into one expression, this route is Ackermann's formula, $\mathbf K=[0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1]\,\mathcal C^{-1}\varphi_d(\mathbf A)$, with $\varphi_d$ the desired characteristic polynomial evaluated at $\mathbf A$; MATLAB's `place` and `acker` do the same job numerically.

Two conditions are hidden in "any poles". $\mathbf K$ is real, so $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K$ is real and its complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs: a single complex pole on its own cannot be placed. And an uncontrollable mode cannot be moved at all ([[SESA3047 7.1 - Controllability and Observability#What an uncontrollable system looks like|7.1 §3]]).

## 4. One gain against many: root locus and pole placement

Root locus in [[SESA2027 B2 - Root Locus Method|SESA2027 B2]] varies one gain and traces the closed-loop poles along fairly narrow paths. That seems to contradict the claim that the poles can go anywhere, but it does not. Feeding back only $x_1$ in Eq. 7.11, $u=-k_1x_1$, gives $\lambda^2+3\lambda+(2+k_1)$: one parameter, so the poles move on a one-dimensional curve (they meet at $-1.5$ and then split vertically). Feeding back only $x_2$ gives $\lambda^2+(3+k_2)\lambda+2$, and for $k_2\ge0$ the poles stay on the real axis, moving apart with their product fixed at 2. With both gains, the two coefficients of the polynomial can be set independently, and two coefficients are all a second-order polynomial has.

![[amc_ch7_root_locus_vs_state_feedback.png|900]]

For example, the lightly damped pair $-1\pm2j$ lies on neither locus, but $\lambda^2+2\lambda+5$ needs only $3+k_2=2$ and $2+k_1=5$, so $\mathbf K=[3\ \ {-1}]$.

## 5. Two inputs: more gains than equations (7.16–7.18)

Suppose the system had two inputs (Eq. 7.16):

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf u.\qquad(7.16)
$$

$\mathbf K$ is now $2\times2$ (Eq. 7.17):

$$
\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}k_1&k_2\\k_3&k_4\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.17)
$$

The product is

$$
\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k_1&k_2\\k_3&k_4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}k_3&k_4\\k_1+k_3&k_2+k_4\end{bmatrix},
$$

so

$$
\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda+k_3&-1+k_4\\2+k_1+k_3&\lambda+3+k_2+k_4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is $(\lambda+k_3)(\lambda+3+k_2+k_4)-(-1+k_4)(2+k_1+k_3)$. Expand the first product:

$$
\lambda^2+(3+k_2+k_4)\lambda+k_3\lambda+3k_3+k_2k_3+k_3k_4 .
$$

Expand the second:

$$
(-1+k_4)(2+k_1+k_3)=-2-k_1-k_3+2k_4+k_1k_4+k_3k_4 .
$$

Subtract and collect; the $k_3k_4$ terms cancel (Eq. 7.18):

$$
|\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)|=\lambda^2+(3+k_2+k_3+k_4)\lambda+(2+k_1+4k_3+k_2k_3-2k_4-k_1k_4).\qquad(7.18)
$$

The algebra is messy, but that is not the difficulty. Matching to $\lambda^2+5\lambda+6$ gives two equations for four unknowns, so the system is underdetermined. There is not one $\mathbf K$ but infinitely many. Choose any $k_3$ and $k_4$ (with $k_4\neq1$); then

$$
k_2=2-k_3-k_4,\qquad k_1=\frac{4-4k_3-k_2k_3+2k_4}{1-k_4}.
$$

| $k_3$ | $k_4$ | $\mathbf K$ | closed-loop poles |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | $\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ | $-2,-3$ |
| 1 | 0 | $\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ | $-2,-3$ |
| 0 | $-1$ | $\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$ | $-2,-3$ |
| 0.5 | 0.5 | $\begin{bmatrix}5&1\\0.5&0.5\end{bmatrix}$ | $-2,-3$ |

The first row switches the second input off and recovers the single-input answer of §2. The gains all give the same poles but different eigenvectors, so different responses and different control effort. Coefficient matching alone cannot choose between them, so for multiple inputs the course does not use pole placement; it uses the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) of Chapter 8, which picks $\mathbf K$ by minimising a cost that weighs state error against control effort.

## 6. Practice problems 1 and 3 (7.4)

### Practice problem 1: stabilising an unstable system

The system is (Eq. 7.19)

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}u.\qquad(7.19)
$$

Its open-loop characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-6\lambda+13$ (trace 6, determinant $8+5=13$), so $\lambda=3\pm2j$: an unstable, growing oscillation.

Controllability: $\mathbf A\mathbf B=[5,\ 2]^T$, so

$$
\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}0&5\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det\mathcal C=0\cdot2-5\cdot1=-5\neq0 .
$$

The system is fully controllable and has one input, so pole placement can be used.

With $\mathbf K=[k_1\ \ k_2]$,

$$
\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-1-k_1&2-k_2\end{bmatrix},
$$

so

$$
\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-4&-5\\1+k_1&\lambda-2+k_2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is the diagonal product minus the off-diagonal product:

$$
(\lambda-4)(\lambda-2+k_2)-(-5)(1+k_1)=(\lambda-4)(\lambda-2+k_2)+5+5k_1 .
$$

Expand the first product:

$$
\lambda^2+(k_2-6)\lambda+8-4k_2+5+5k_1=\lambda^2+(k_2-6)\lambda+(13+5k_1-4k_2).
$$

The desired poles $-1\pm2j$ give

$$
(\lambda+1-2j)(\lambda+1+2j)=(\lambda+1)^2+4=\lambda^2+2\lambda+5 .
$$

Match the coefficients:

$$
\lambda^1:\ k_2-6=2\ \Rightarrow\ k_2=8,\qquad\lambda^0:\ 13+5k_1-32=5\ \Rightarrow\ k_1=\frac{24}{5}=4.8 .
$$

So $\mathbf K=[4.8\ \ 8]$. Check: $\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-5.8&-6\end{bmatrix}$ has trace $-2$ and determinant $-24+29=5$, giving $\lambda^2+2\lambda+5$. The same $\mathbf K$ follows from the companion-form route of §3: $\mathbf K_c=[5-13,\ 2-(-6)]=[-8,\ 8]$ and $\mathbf K_c\mathbf T^{-1}=[4.8,\ 8]$. The right panel of the figure in §2 shows the open loop diverging within a second and the closed loop settling with the damping ratio $\zeta=1/\sqrt5=0.45$ of the chosen pair.

### Practice problem 3: linearise, check, place

The nonlinear system is (Eq. 7.21)

$$
\dot x=-x+z-x^2z-y^2,\qquad\dot y=z,\qquad\dot z=-4(x+1)^2-z+u.\qquad(7.21)
$$

#### Equilibria

With the control term set to zero, $\dot y=0$ gives $z=0$. Then $\dot z=0$ gives $-4(x+1)^2=0$, so $x=-1$. Then $\dot x=0$ gives $1+0-0-y^2=0$, so $y=\pm1$. There are two equilibria, $(-1,1,0)$ and $(-1,-1,0)$.

#### Linearisation and state-space form

Linearise about $(x_0,y_0,z_0)=(-1,1,0)$ ([[Linearisation about an Equilibrium Point]]). The partial derivatives are

$$
\frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial x}=-1-2xz,\quad\frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial y}=-2y,\quad\frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial z}=1-x^2;\qquad\frac{\partial\dot z}{\partial x}=-8(x+1),\quad\frac{\partial\dot z}{\partial z}=-1,\quad\frac{\partial\dot z}{\partial u}=1 .
$$

and $\dot y=z$ is already linear. At $(-1,1,0)$ these are $-1$, $-2$, $0$; $0$, $-1$, $1$. With $\delta x,\delta y,\delta z$ the perturbations from the equilibrium, the linearised system with control in state-space form is

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\delta x\\\delta y\\\delta z\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-2&0\\0&0&1\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta x\\\delta y\\\delta z\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}u .
$$

$\mathbf A$ is block upper triangular: $\delta x$ on its own above a $2\times2$ block in $\delta y,\delta z$, so its eigenvalues are $-1$ (from $\delta x$) and those of $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$, namely $0$ and $-1$. The open loop is marginally stable at best. (About the other equilibrium, $(-1,-1,0)$, the only change is $\partial\dot x/\partial y=+2$.)

#### Controllability

With $\mathbf B=[0,0,1]^T$:

$$
\mathbf A\mathbf B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf A^2\mathbf B=\mathbf A\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-2\\0&1&-1\\1&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Expand along the first row, whose only non-zero entry is $-2$ at position (1,3), sign $+$:

$$
\det\mathcal C=-2\begin{vmatrix}0&1\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-2(0-1)=2\neq0 .
$$

The system is fully controllable: the input drives $\delta z$, $\delta z$ drives $\delta y$, and $\delta y$ drives $\delta x$.

#### Pole placement

The target closed-loop eigenvalues are $-1$, $0$, $-3$. With $\mathbf K=[k_1\ k_2\ k_3]$, only the last row of $\mathbf A$ changes:

$$
\lambda\mathbf I-(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\mathbf K)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda+1&2&0\\0&\lambda&-1\\k_1&k_2&\lambda+1+k_3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Expand along the first row:

$$
(\lambda+1)\big[\lambda(\lambda+1+k_3)+k_2\big]-2\big[0\cdot(\lambda+1+k_3)-(-1)k_1\big].
$$

Multiply out:

$$
\lambda^3+(2+k_3)\lambda^2+(1+k_2+k_3)\lambda+(k_2-2k_1).
$$

The desired polynomial is $\lambda(\lambda+1)(\lambda+3)=\lambda^3+4\lambda^2+3\lambda$. Matching:

$$
2+k_3=4\Rightarrow k_3=2,\qquad1+k_2+2=3\Rightarrow k_2=0,\qquad k_2-2k_1=0\Rightarrow k_1=0 .
$$

So $\mathbf K=[0\ \ 0\ \ 2]$ and $u=-2\,\delta z$. This makes sense from the open-loop eigenvalues $-1,0,-1$: the targets keep $-1$ and $0$ and move one $-1$ to $-3$, and $\delta z$ is the state that obeys $\dot{\delta z}=-\delta z+u$, so $u=-2\delta z$ turns it into $\dot{\delta z}=-3\delta z$. The requested pole at $0$ leaves the closed loop marginally stable: a constant offset in $\delta y$ (and the $\delta x$ it drives) does not decay. A designer would normally place all three poles in the left half-plane.

## 7. An aircraft example: the Navion spiral with ailerons

As a check of scale, take the Navion lateral model of [[SESA3047 6.3 - Lateral Modes and their Approximations|6.3]]: using it and the aileron column of $\mathbf B$ from [[SESA3047 7.1 - Controllability and Observability#Worked example: one actuator for the Navion's lateral motion|7.1 §3]], place the spiral pole at $-0.2$ s⁻¹ (time constant 5 s instead of 114 s) and leave the roll subsidence and Dutch roll where they are. The single input is controllable, so the gain is unique:

$$
\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}0.00005&-0.0064&-0.0225&-0.0613\end{bmatrix},\qquad\delta_A=-\mathbf K\mathbf x=-0.00005\,v+0.0064\,p+0.0225\,r+0.0613\,\phi
$$

($\delta_A$ in rad; $v$ in m/s, $p$ and $r$ in rad/s, $\phi$ in rad). A bank of $10^\circ$ to the right calls for $0.6^\circ$ of aileron. With $L_{\delta_A}<0$ that gives a rolling moment to the left, which levels the wings. A wing-leveller is mainly bank-angle feedback, which is what the large $\phi$ gain says. Lab 2 Task 3 does the same for the Boeing 747 longitudinal model with MATLAB's `place`.
