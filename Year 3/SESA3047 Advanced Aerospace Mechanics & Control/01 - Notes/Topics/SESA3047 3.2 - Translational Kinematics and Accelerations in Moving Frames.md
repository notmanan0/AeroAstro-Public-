# SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames

> [!abstract] Summary
> A point P is watched by an observer in a frame $F_a$ and by one riding a moving, rotating frame $F_b$. Differentiating $\mathbf p_{P/O}=\mathbf p_{Q/O}+\mathbf p_{P/Q}$ once with the [[Transport Theorem]] gives the velocity, $\mathbf v_{P/a}=\mathbf v_{P/b}+(\mathbf v_{Q/a}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p_{P/Q})$: the relative velocity plus the **transport velocity**. Differentiating again gives five terms:
>
> $$\mathbf a_{P/a}=\mathbf a_{P/b}+\mathbf a_{Q/a}+\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p)+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b},$$
>
> including the **centripetal** and **Coriolis** terms. On a rotating Earth these appear in Newton's law as apparent centrifugal and Coriolis forces. Under the flat-Earth model they vanish, and the inertial acceleration equals the Earth-relative one. For an aircraft we write the velocity in body axes, $\mathbf v^{FRD}=[u,v,w]^T$. Its derivative seen from the Earth is then $[\dot u+qw-rv,\ \dot v+ru-pw,\ \dot w+pv-qu]^T$, not $[\dot u,\dot v,\dot w]^T$. The position follows from the **translational kinematic equation** $\dot{\mathbf p}^{NED}=\mathbf C^T_{FRD/NED}\mathbf v^{FRD}$: rotate the body velocity into NED first, then integrate.

## The topic in one physical picture

Walk down the aisle of an airliner that is turning. Relative to the cabin you walk in a straight line at a steady 1.5 m/s, so you feel no need to accelerate. Yet relative to the ground you are:

1. carried along with the aircraft (the cabin origin moves and accelerates);
2. swung round by the turn (a point fixed in the cabin goes round a circle, so it has a centripetal acceleration);
3. moving within a rotating cabin. As you walk aft, the turn keeps changing the direction of your walking velocity, and you also move to a part of the cabin with a different velocity. These two effects add up to the **Coriolis** acceleration, $2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{rel}$.

Your feet feel those last two as a sideways push. The cabin is not an inertial frame, so "straight and steady in the cabin" is not "unaccelerated". §3.2 makes this bookkeeping exact, then applies it to two questions: is the Earth a good enough inertial frame, and how do we get from body-axis velocity to position on a map?

## 1. Position and velocity of a point in a moving frame (§3.2.1)

![[amc_moving_frame_derivation.png|900]]

### Set-up (Eq. 3.20, Fig. 3.3)

- $F_a$: the reference frame, origin $O$ (later: inertial or Earth-fixed);
- $F_b$: a moving frame, origin $Q$, rotating at $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ relative to $F_a$ (later: the body or the Earth);
- $P$: the point of interest.

$$
\mathbf p_{P/O}=\mathbf p_{Q/O}+\mathbf p_{P/Q}.
$$

### Differentiate in F_a (Eqs. 3.21–3.22)

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/O}={}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{Q/O}+{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/Q}.
$$

- $\mathbf p_{P/O}$ and $\mathbf p_{Q/O}$ are measured from $O$, a point of $F_a$, so their $F_a$-derivatives are simply velocities: ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/O}=\mathbf v_{P/a}$ and ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{Q/O}=\mathbf v_{Q/a}$.
- $\mathbf p_{P/Q}$ is the position as seen in the **moving** frame. Its natural derivative is the one seen in $F_b$, ${}^b\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/Q}=\mathbf v_{P/b}$. Convert with the transport theorem:

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/Q}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}_{P/Q}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q}=\mathbf v_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q}.
$$

Substitute:

$$
\mathbf v_{P/a}=\mathbf v_{Q/a}+\mathbf v_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q}.
$$

### Rearrange (Eq. 3.23)

$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_{P/a}=\underbrace{\mathbf v_{P/b}}_{\text{relative}}+\underbrace{\left(\mathbf v_{Q/a}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q}\right)}_{\text{transport velocity}}}
$$

> [!tip] What "transport velocity" means
> It is the velocity, seen in $F_a$, of the point **of $F_b$** that is at P at this instant. If P were glued to $F_b$ ($\mathbf v_{P/b}=\mathbf 0$), it would move with exactly that velocity. This is the rigid-body velocity formula $\mathbf v_P=\mathbf v_Q+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{P/Q}$ from Year 1 ([[Relative Velocity Equation for Rigid Bodies]]), with an extra relative term when P moves within the body.

### Worked example: tracking a target by radar (suggested, after the notes' application)

The notes' application (p. 6): an inertial navigation system gives the aircraft's $\mathbf v_{Q/a}$ and $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$; the radar gives the target's position $\mathbf p_{P/Q}$ and velocity $\mathbf v_{P/b}$ **relative to the aircraft**. All in one set of coordinates, at an instant when FRD is aligned with NED:

- $\mathbf v_{Q/a}=[200,0,0]^T$ m/s (flying north);
- $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}=[0,0,0.05]^T$ rad/s (turning right);
- $\mathbf p_{P/Q}=[2000,500,0]^T$ m (ahead and to the right);
- $\mathbf v_{P/b}=[-150,-50,0]^T$ m/s (closing, drifting left on the scope).

**Transport term:**

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p=\begin{bmatrix}0\cdot0-0.05\cdot500\\0.05\cdot2000-0\cdot0\\0\cdot500-0\cdot2000\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-25\\100\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{m/s}.
$$

**Target's true velocity:**

$$
\mathbf v_{P/a}=\begin{bmatrix}-150\\-50\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}200\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-25\\100\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}25\\50\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{m/s}.
$$

The target is really moving slowly north-east at 56 m/s. On the scope it seemed to drift left at 50 m/s, but most of that was the radar platform turning right.

## 2. Acceleration of a point in a moving frame (§3.2.1, Eqs. 3.24–3.26)

Differentiate Eq. 3.23 in $F_a$, one term at a time. Write $\boldsymbol\omega=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$, $\boldsymbol\alpha={}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$ and $\mathbf p=\mathbf p_{P/Q}$.

**Term 1: the relative velocity** is defined in $F_b$, so use the transport theorem:

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf v}_{P/b}={}^b\dot{\mathbf v}_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}=\mathbf a_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}.
$$

**Term 2: the origin's velocity** is already an $F_a$ quantity:

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf v}_{Q/a}=\mathbf a_{Q/a}.
$$

**Term 3: the cross product.** Product rule first:

$$
{}^a\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\left(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p\right)={}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf p+\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}.
$$

The first part is $\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p$. For the second, ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}$ was found in §1:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}=\boldsymbol\omega\times\left(\mathbf v_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p\right)=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p).
$$

**Add the three terms (Eq. 3.25):**

$$
\mathbf a_{P/a}=\left(\mathbf a_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}\right)+\mathbf a_{Q/a}+\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p).
$$

$\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}$ appears **twice**, once from each of terms 1 and 3. Collect (Eq. 3.26):

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_{P/a}=\underbrace{\mathbf a_{P/b}}_{\text{relative}}+\underbrace{\mathbf a_{Q/a}+\boldsymbol\alpha_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times(\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q})}_{\text{transport acceleration}}+\underbrace{2\,\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf v_{P/b}}_{\text{Coriolis}}}
$$

| Term | Meaning (notes' table, p. 7) |
|---|---|
| $\mathbf a_{P/a}$ | acceleration of P seen in $F_a$ |
| $\mathbf a_{P/b}$ | acceleration of P measured in the moving frame |
| $\mathbf a_{Q/a}$ | acceleration of the moving frame's origin |
| $\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p$ | from the frame's angular acceleration (tangential, or "Euler", term) |
| $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p)$ | **centripetal**: points towards the rotation axis, size $\omega^2\times$(distance from the axis) |
| $2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}$ | **Coriolis**: only when P moves within the rotating frame |

> [!tip] Where the factor 2 comes from
> Half the Coriolis term is the rotation turning the **direction** of the relative velocity (term 1). The other half is P moving to a part of the rotating frame that has a **different transport velocity** (term 3). See [[Coriolis and Centripetal Acceleration]].

> [!check] Centripetal is really $-\omega^2\mathbf p_\perp$
> BAC−CAB ([[SESA3047 2.1 - Vector Operations and the Transport Theorem|2.1]]): $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p)=\boldsymbol\omega(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf p)-\mathbf p\,\omega^2=-\omega^2\left(\mathbf p-\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\cdot\mathbf p)\right)=-\omega^2\mathbf p_\perp$, where $\mathbf p_\perp$ is the part of $\mathbf p$ perpendicular to the axis.

![[amc_coriolis_picture.png|900]]

### Worked example: walking aft in a turning aircraft (suggested)

Use the level turn of [[SESA3047 3.1 - Rotational Kinematics and Euler-Angle Rates#10.1 Body rates in a steady level turn (suggested)|3.1 §10.1]]: $\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}=[0,\ 0.02832,\ 0.04905]^T$ rad/s, constant, so $\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf 0$. A passenger 10 m ahead of the centre of mass, on the centreline, walks aft at a steady 1.5 m/s: $\mathbf p=[10,0,0]^T$ m, $\mathbf v_{P/b}=[-1.5,0,0]^T$ m/s, $\mathbf a_{P/b}=\mathbf 0$.

**Centripetal.** First $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p=[q\cdot0-r\cdot0,\ r\cdot10-0,\ 0-q\cdot10]^T=[0,\ 0.4905,\ -0.2832]^T$. Then

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p)=\begin{bmatrix}q(-0.2832)-r(0.4905)\\r\cdot0-0\cdot(-0.2832)\\0\cdot0.4905-q\cdot0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.0321\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{m/s}^2.
$$

It points aft, towards the turn axis through the centre of mass, with size $\omega^2\times10=0.0032\times10$ ✓.

**Coriolis.** $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}=[0,\ r(-1.5),\ -q(-1.5)]^T=[0,-0.0736,0.0425]^T$, so

$$
2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/b}=[0,\ -0.147,\ 0.085]^T\ \text{m/s}^2.
$$

The Coriolis term is **five times** the centripetal term here, and sideways (to the left, and a little down). To walk straight down the aisle the passenger must push sideways on the floor. On a ship or a fairground ride the same effect is easy to feel.

## 3. Acceleration in different Earth models (§3.2.2)

### Rotating Earth (Eqs. 3.27–3.31)

Take $F_a=F_i$ (inertial) and $F_b=F_e$ (Earth-fixed, origin $Q$ at the Earth's centre). Then:

- the Earth spins at a constant rate, so $\boldsymbol\alpha_{e/i}=\mathbf 0$ (3.27);
- the Earth's centre is taken as unaccelerated, $\mathbf a_{Q/i}=\mathbf 0$ (its orbital acceleration, $\approx0.006$ m/s², is neglected).

Strike those terms from Eq. 3.26 (Eq. 3.28) to get Eq. 3.29:

$$
\mathbf a_{P/i}=\mathbf a_{P/e}+\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times(\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q})+2\,\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times\mathbf v_{P/e}.
$$

Newton's second law holds in the **inertial** frame (3.30): $\sum\mathbf F_{real}=m\mathbf a_{P/i}$. Multiply Eq. 3.29 by $m$, substitute, and move the rotation terms to the force side (Eq. 3.31):

$$
\boxed{m\,\mathbf a_{P/e}=\sum\mathbf F_{real}\underbrace{-\,m\,\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times(\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times\mathbf p_{P/Q})}_{\text{centrifugal force}}\underbrace{-\,2m\,\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}\times\mathbf v_{P/e}}_{\text{Coriolis force}}}
$$

> [!important] Apparent forces
> The centrifugal and Coriolis "forces" are not real forces. They are the price of writing Newton's law in a **non-inertial** (rotating) frame.
> - Centrifugal: directed away from the Earth's axis.
> - Coriolis: perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}$ and $\mathbf v_{P/e}$, so it steers a moving object to the left or right. In the northern hemisphere it deflects horizontal motion to the **right**.

### How big are they? (suggested numbers)

$\Omega=|\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}|=7.292\times10^{-5}$ rad/s, $R=6371$ km, latitude $\lambda=50^\circ$ (Southampton):

| Effect | Formula | Value | Fraction of $g$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| centrifugal | $\Omega^2R\cos\lambda$ | 0.0218 m/s² | 0.22% |
| Coriolis, aircraft at 250 m/s heading north | $2\Omega V\sin\lambda$ | 0.0279 m/s² | 0.28% |
| Coriolis, car at 5 m/s | $2\Omega V\sin\lambda$ | 0.00056 m/s² | 0.006% |

In NED components at latitude $\lambda$, $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}=\Omega[\cos\lambda,0,-\sin\lambda]^T$. For $\mathbf v=[250,0,0]^T$ (north), $2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v=[0,-0.0279,0]^T$, so the apparent Coriolis force per unit mass, $-2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$, is $+0.0279$ m/s² **east**: to the right ✓. (The centrifugal term is also normally absorbed into the measured "effective" $g$.)

> [!tip] Why the effect still matters
> 0.3% of $g$ sounds negligible for handling qualities, and it is. But an acceleration error of 0.028 m/s² held for 10 minutes displaces a dead-reckoned position by $\tfrac12at^2=\tfrac12(0.028)(600)^2\approx5$ km. That is why navigation systems, long-range ballistic trajectories and orbital mechanics keep the Earth's rotation, while handling-qualities studies drop it (notes, p. 8).

### Flat Earth (Eqs. 3.32–3.33)

Set $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}=\mathbf 0$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_{P/i}=\mathbf a_{P/e}}
$$

The Earth-relative acceleration **is** the inertial acceleration. This is valid for conventional aircraft over limited distances and times, and degrades for long-range or long-duration flights, very fast vehicles and precise navigation, where rotation, curvature, local vertical changes and the variation of gravity matter (notes, p. 8).

## 4. Differentiating the velocity in the body frame (§3.2.2 Eq. 3.34, §3.2.4 Eqs. 3.41–3.42)

The aircraft's velocity relative to Earth, $\mathbf v_{cm/e}$, is one physical vector. Under flat Earth its Earth-frame derivative is the inertial acceleration (3.33). But aerodynamic and propulsive forces are naturally written in **body** axes, so we want everything in FRD.

**Transport theorem**, from the Earth frame to the body frame (Eq. 3.41; the notes write ${}^b\dot{(\cdot)}$ and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big|_b$ interchangeably):

$$
\mathbf a_{cm/i}={}^e\dot{\mathbf v}_{cm/e}={}^b\dot{\mathbf v}_{cm/e}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf v_{cm/e}.
$$

**In FRD components.** ${}^b\dot{\mathbf v}$ is just the derivative of the components, $[\dot u,\dot v,\dot w]^T$; $\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}=[p,q,r]^T$. The cross product, row by row:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix}\times\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\w\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}qw-rv\\ru-pw\\pv-qu\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Add (Eq. 3.42):

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a^{FRD}_{cm/e}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot u+qw-rv\\\dot v+ru-pw\\\dot w+pv-qu\end{bmatrix}}
$$

> [!important] Key idea (notes, p. 10)
> $[\dot u,\dot v,\dot w]^T$ is **not** the acceleration, because the FRD axes rotate with the aircraft. In the level turn of 3.1 the aircraft flies at constant $u=100$ m/s, so $\dot u=\dot v=\dot w=0$, yet it is accelerating towards the centre of the turn: $\mathbf a=[0,\ ru,\ -qu]^T=[0,\ 4.91,\ -2.83]^T$ m/s². Its magnitude is $5.66$ m/s² $=V\dot\psi=g\tan30^\circ$ ✓. These are exactly the "$qW-rV$" terms of the SESA2027 equations of motion ([[SESA2027 A1 - Dynamic Systems and Aircraft Equations of Motion#Newton's second law in the body frame|SESA2027 A1]]). Relating $\mathbf a$ to the forces is dynamics: the translational dynamic equation is Chapter 4.

## 5. The position state equation (§3.2.3, Eqs. 3.35–3.40)

![[amc_translational_kinematics.png|900]]

**Position** of the centre of mass from the local NED origin $Q$ (Eq. 3.35):

$$
\mathbf p^{NED}_{cm/Q}=\begin{bmatrix}p_N&p_E&p_D\end{bmatrix}^T=\begin{bmatrix}x&y&z\end{bmatrix}^T.
$$

**Its Earth-frame derivative** is the Earth-relative velocity in NED components (3.36):

$$
{}^e\dot{\mathbf p}^{NED}_{cm/Q}=\mathbf v^{NED}_{cm/e}.
$$

**But the velocity is carried in FRD**, $\mathbf v^{FRD}_{cm/e}=[u,v,w]^T$ (3.37), because the forces that change it are easiest to write there. Convert with the transpose of the DCM (3.38), since $\mathbf C^{-1}=\mathbf C^T$:

$$
\mathbf v^{NED}_{cm/e}=\mathbf C^T_{FRD/NED}\,\mathbf v^{FRD}_{cm/e}.
$$

**Translational kinematic equation** (3.39):

$$
\boxed{{}^e\dot{\mathbf p}^{NED}_{cm/Q}=\mathbf C^T_{FRD/NED}\,\mathbf v^{FRD}_{cm/e}}
$$

In full (3.40); the matrix is the transpose of [[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#Multiplying it out (the step the notes compress into one line)|Eq. 2.38]], so its **columns** are the body axes in NED (checked symbolically):

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\dot x\\\dot y\\\dot z\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_\theta c_\psi&-c_\phi s_\psi+s_\phi s_\theta c_\psi&s_\phi s_\psi+c_\phi s_\theta c_\psi\\c_\theta s_\psi&c_\phi c_\psi+s_\phi s_\theta s_\psi&-s_\phi c_\psi+c_\phi s_\theta s_\psi\\-s_\theta&s_\phi c_\theta&c_\phi c_\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\w\end{bmatrix}.
$$

> [!warning] Rotate first, then integrate
> Body-axis velocity components **cannot** be integrated directly to get north, east and down (notes, p. 9). In the figure the aircraft holds $u=100$ m/s, $v=w=0$ and turns at $r=0.05$ rad/s. Integrating $u$ as if it were northward speed predicts a straight 12 km dash north. The correct NED velocity rotates with the heading, and the aircraft flies a 2 km-radius circle.

### Worked example: climbing on a heading of 060° (suggested)

$\psi=60^\circ$, $\theta=10^\circ$, $\phi=0$, and $\mathbf v^{FRD}=[150,0,5]^T$ m/s (a small positive $w$: the air meets the wing from below).

With $\phi=0$ the matrix simplifies ($c_\phi=1$, $s_\phi=0$):

$$
\dot x=c_\theta c_\psi\,u+s_\theta c_\psi\,w=0.9848(0.5)(150)+0.1736(0.5)(5)=73.86+0.43=74.29\ \text{m/s},
$$

$$
\dot y=c_\theta s_\psi\,u+s_\theta s_\psi\,w=0.9848(0.8660)(150)+0.1736(0.8660)(5)=127.93+0.75=128.68\ \text{m/s},
$$

$$
\dot z=-s_\theta\,u+c_\theta\,w=-0.1736(150)+0.9848(5)=-26.05+4.92=-21.12\ \text{m/s}.
$$

So the aircraft climbs at $21.1$ m/s (NED $z$ is down, so $\dot z<0$ is climbing), and the ground track is $\arctan(128.68/74.29)=60^\circ$ ✓.

> [!check] Flight-path angle
> $\gamma=\arcsin(21.12/150.08)=8.09^\circ$. The angle of attack is $\alpha=\arctan(w/u)=\arctan(5/150)=1.91^\circ$, and in wings-level flight $\gamma=\theta-\alpha=10^\circ-1.91^\circ=8.09^\circ$ ✓.

## Notes on the source

> [!warning] Small slips in `Chapter-3.pdf` §3.2
> - p. 4, Eq. 3.18: *"the relative velocity between Earth-fixed frame $F_e$ and an inertial reference frame"* should be the relative **angular** velocity $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}$.
> - p. 6, Eq. 3.24: the second relative-velocity term is printed $\mathbf v_{\dot P/b}$; it means ${}^a\dot{\mathbf v}_{P/b}$, the $F_a$-derivative of $\mathbf v_{P/b}$.
> - p. 8, Eq. 3.31: the square bracket around the centrifugal term opens as "$m[$" but closes after an extra parenthesis; the content is $m\,\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p)$.
> - p. 9: the transport theorem is written with $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big|_b$ instead of ${}^b\dot{(\cdot)}$ (footnote 1); the two mean the same.

## Common mistakes

1. Writing the acceleration in body axes as $[\dot u,\dot v,\dot w]^T$.
2. Forgetting the factor 2 in the Coriolis term, or using $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{P/a}$ (absolute velocity) instead of the relative $\mathbf v_{P/b}$.
3. Integrating $u,v,w$ to get $x,y,z$ without rotating to NED.
4. Using $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$ instead of its **transpose** to go from FRD to NED.
5. Treating centrifugal and Coriolis forces as real forces in an inertial-frame free-body diagram.
