# SESA3047 2.1 - Vector Operations and the Transport Theorem

> [!abstract] Summary
> Chapter 2 builds the kinematic tools needed for the six-degree-of-freedom equations of motion. The **dot product** measures how far one vector acts along another and gives the scalar $\mathbf u^T\mathbf v$ in components. The **cross product** measures perpendicularity and gives a vector normal to both. In components it can be written as a matrix product $\tilde{\mathbf u}\mathbf v$ with the skew-symmetric **cross-product matrix**. Triple products, in particular $\mathbf u\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=\mathbf v(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)-\mathbf w(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$, simplify expressions such as centripetal acceleration, $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)=-\omega^2\mathbf r$. The time derivative of a vector depends on the observer. The **transport theorem** ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$ converts a derivative seen in a rotating frame $F_b$ into one seen in $F_a$. It is the single most important tool for writing Newton's laws in body axes.

## The chapter in one physical picture

Sit in the cockpit of an aircraft that is pulling up. The nose is fixed in front of you, so from your seat the nose direction never changes. An observer on the ground sees the nose sweep upwards. Both are right. They are different observers, and the rate at which a vector changes depends on who is watching.

The chapter gives you exactly the tools to reconcile the two views:

1. **Dot and cross products** decompose vectors into "how much along" and "how much across". They are the arithmetic of kinematics.
2. **The cross-product matrix** turns $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\cdot)$ into a matrix, so a computer can do it.
3. **The transport theorem** says that the ground observer sees whatever the pilot sees, **plus** the extra change caused by the pilot's frame rotating: $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p$.

Section 2.2 of these notes ([[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles]]) then deals with the other half of the problem: converting the **components** of a vector between the ground and body frames.

## 1. Vectors, components and notation (recap)

A vector is a physical object with magnitude and direction: position, velocity, angular velocity, force, moment. In an orthonormal Cartesian coordinate system with unit vectors $\mathbf e_x,\mathbf e_y,\mathbf e_z$,

$$
\mathbf u=u_x\mathbf e_x+u_y\mathbf e_y+u_z\mathbf e_z,
\qquad
\mathbf u\ \leftrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}u_x\\u_y\\u_z\end{bmatrix}\ (\text{Eq. 2.5}).
$$

The column is the vector's component array **in that coordinate system**; Chapter 1 wrote it $[\mathbf u]^c$. The whole chapter rests on two facts about an orthonormal basis:

$$
\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j=\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&i=j\\0&i\neq j\end{cases},
\qquad
\mathbf e_x\times\mathbf e_y=\mathbf e_z,\ \ \mathbf e_y\times\mathbf e_z=\mathbf e_x,\ \ \mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_x=\mathbf e_y .
$$

Reversing any cross product flips its sign, and $\mathbf e_i\times\mathbf e_i=\mathbf 0$.

**Board notation (L4, ll. 9–42).** On the whiteboard a vector is written with an **underline**, $\underline{p}$, and scalars without one (ll. 11–16). Vectors are always **column** vectors, written in the notes as $\mathbf p=[p_x\ p_y\ p_z]^T$ (ll. 23–26). The magnitude is $|\mathbf u|$ (l. 146). The lecturer's emphasis (ll. 27–31, 46–51): the physical quantity never changes, only its **component representation** depends on the coordinate system chosen.

## 2. The dot product

### Definition and meaning

$$
\boxed{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u=|\mathbf v||\mathbf u|\cos\alpha}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.1})
$$

Here $\alpha$ is the smaller angle between the vectors. The result is a **scalar**. Geometrically, $|\mathbf v|\cos\alpha$ is the length of the shadow that $\mathbf v$ casts on the direction of $\mathbf u$. The dot product measures **parallelism**: how much one vector acts in the direction of another.

| Case | $\alpha$ | $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v$ |
|---|---|---|
| orthogonal | $90^\circ$ | $0$ |
| parallel | $0^\circ$ | $+|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|$ |
| anti-parallel | $180^\circ$ | $-|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|$ |

Properties (Eqs. 2.2–2.4): commutative, $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u$; distributive, $(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf w=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w$; scalar-associative, $(\lambda\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v=\lambda(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$.

> [!note] Transcript wording (L4, ll. 182–185)
> The recording calls the third property *"the associative property"*. It is **scalar** associativity (homogeneity): a scalar factor can be moved freely. The dot product has no ordinary associative law, because $(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf w$ is not even defined: $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v$ is a scalar, and you cannot dot a scalar with a vector. The distributive law on l. 183 is lost in the transcription; it is the one written above.

The lecturer stresses the two limiting cases as a test of **parallelism** (L4, ll. 157–177): $\alpha=0$ gives the largest possible value $|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|$, $\alpha=90^\circ$ gives zero, and $\alpha=180^\circ$ gives $-|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|$.

### Derivation of the component (matrix) form

The notes state Eq. 2.6 without proof. It follows in one line from distributivity and $\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j=\delta_{ij}$:

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\Big(\sum_i u_i\mathbf e_i\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_j v_j\mathbf e_j\Big)=\sum_i\sum_j u_iv_j\,(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j)=\sum_i\sum_j u_iv_j\,\delta_{ij}.
$$

Of the nine terms, only the three with $i=j$ survive (panel 2 of the figure below):

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z=\mathbf u^T\mathbf v}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.6})
$$

> [!warning] Same coordinates only
> $\mathbf u^T\mathbf v$ is valid only if both component arrays are written in the **same orthonormal coordinate system**. Dotting a body-axis array with an NED array is meaningless until one of them has been transformed with a DCM (see 2.2).

**Where $\cos\alpha$ comes from.** Set $\mathbf v=\mathbf u$ to get $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=|\mathbf u|^2$. Expand $|\mathbf u-\mathbf v|^2=(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)=|\mathbf u|^2+|\mathbf v|^2-2\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v$. The law of cosines for the triangle with sides $\mathbf u$, $\mathbf v$, $\mathbf u-\mathbf v$ gives $|\mathbf u-\mathbf v|^2=|\mathbf u|^2+|\mathbf v|^2-2|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\alpha$. Comparing the two gives Eq. 2.1. The geometric and component definitions agree.

### Example: velocity along a flight path (Eq. 2.7)

If $\mathbf e_t$ is the unit tangent to a prescribed path, the signed speed along the path is

$$
v_t=\mathbf e_t\cdot\mathbf v .
$$

$v_t>0$ means moving along $\mathbf e_t$; $v_t<0$ means moving against it. In the lecture (L4, ll. 197–207) the point is that $\mathbf e_t$ must be a **unit** vector, $|\mathbf e_t|=1$. Only then is $\mathbf e_t\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf v|\cos\alpha$ the component itself, "without any multiplication factor" (l. 203).

*Numbers (suggested):* $\mathbf v=[240,\ 30,\ -10]^T$ m/s and a path tangent $\mathbf e_t=[\cos10^\circ,\ \sin10^\circ,\ 0]^T$, both in the same coordinates:

$$
v_t=240(0.9848)+30(0.1736)+(-10)(0)=241.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$

## 3. The cross product

### Definition and meaning

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\sin\alpha\,\mathbf n}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.8})
$$

$\mathbf n$ is the unit normal to the plane of $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$, chosen by the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers from $\mathbf u$ to $\mathbf v$ and the thumb gives $\mathbf n$. The result is a **vector**. Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$. The cross product measures **perpendicularity**: it is largest for orthogonal vectors and zero for parallel ones.

Properties:

$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u)\quad(\text{anti-commutative, 2.9}),
$$

$$
\lambda(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=(\lambda\mathbf u)\times\mathbf v=\mathbf u\times(\lambda\mathbf v)\quad(2.10),
\qquad
\mathbf u\times(\mathbf v+\mathbf w)=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v+\mathbf u\times\mathbf w\quad(2.11).
$$

### Derivation of the component form

Expand both vectors on the basis and use the cross-product table. Each of the nine products $u_iv_j(\mathbf e_i\times\mathbf e_j)$ is either zero ($i=j$) or $\pm$ the third unit vector:

| | $v_x\mathbf e_x$ | $v_y\mathbf e_y$ | $v_z\mathbf e_z$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $u_x\mathbf e_x$ | $\mathbf 0$ | $+u_xv_y\,\mathbf e_z$ | $-u_xv_z\,\mathbf e_y$ |
| $u_y\mathbf e_y$ | $-u_yv_x\,\mathbf e_z$ | $\mathbf 0$ | $+u_yv_z\,\mathbf e_x$ |
| $u_z\mathbf e_z$ | $+u_zv_x\,\mathbf e_y$ | $-u_zv_y\,\mathbf e_x$ | $\mathbf 0$ |

Collect by unit vector:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}u_yv_z-u_zv_y\\u_zv_x-u_xv_z\\u_xv_y-u_yv_x\end{bmatrix}}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.15})
$$

This is the same as the determinant rule of Eq. 2.14,

$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\u_x&u_y&u_z\\v_x&v_y&v_z\end{vmatrix}
=\mathbf i\begin{vmatrix}u_y&u_z\\v_y&v_z\end{vmatrix}-\mathbf j\begin{vmatrix}u_x&u_z\\v_x&v_z\end{vmatrix}+\mathbf k\begin{vmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{vmatrix}.
$$

Mind the **minus sign on $\mathbf j$**: $-(u_xv_z-u_zv_x)=u_zv_x-u_xv_z$, the middle entry above. That sign is the most common slip in exams.

### The cross-product (skew) matrix

Every entry of Eq. 2.15 is linear in $\mathbf v$, so the cross product can be written as a matrix acting on $\mathbf v$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}0&-u_z&u_y\\u_z&0&-u_x\\-u_y&u_x&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}v_x\\v_y\\v_z\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\mathbf u}\,\mathbf v}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.16})
$$

*Check row 1:* $0\cdot v_x-u_zv_y+u_yv_z=u_yv_z-u_zv_y$ ✓.

Properties that follow immediately (and are used constantly later):

- **Skew-symmetric:** $\tilde{\mathbf u}^T=-\tilde{\mathbf u}$, so the diagonal is zero and there are only 3 independent entries.
- $\tilde{\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$ (a vector crossed with itself).
- $\tilde{\mathbf u}\,\mathbf v=-\tilde{\mathbf v}\,\mathbf u$ (anti-commutativity in matrix form).
- **Memory aid:** zeros on the diagonal. $u_z$ sits just off the diagonal in the top-left, $u_x$ in the bottom-right, $u_y$ in the corners. The **upper** triangle reads $-u_z,\ u_y,\ -u_x$.

See [[Cross-Product Matrix]].

> [!important] "What you need to remember" (L4, ll. 286–298)
> The lecture builds $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$ from the determinant (ll. 255–285), then singles out the skew matrix as the thing to memorise: *"what is very important to remember is this"* (l. 297). It *"simplifies a lot the calculation that we need to do later on to obtain the six degrees of freedom system"* (ll. 298–299). Expect to write $\tilde{\mathbf u}$ from memory in the exam.
>
> The transcript's description of the matrix (ll. 289–295) is unreadable, but it does record the two structural facts worth checking yours against: the **diagonal is all zero**, and the off-diagonal entries come in **$\pm$ pairs** ($u_z$ and $-u_z$, $u_y$ and $-u_y$, $u_x$ and $-u_x$).

> [!example] Numbers (suggested)
> $\mathbf u=[1,2,3]^T$, $\mathbf v=[4,5,6]^T$:
> $$\tilde{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}0&-3&2\\3&0&-1\\-2&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-15+12\\12-6\\-8+5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-3\\6\\-3\end{bmatrix}.$$
> Check that it is perpendicular to $\mathbf u$: $\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=-3+12-9=0$ ✓.

## 4. Triple products

### Scalar triple product

$$
\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)=\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.12})
$$

**Why it is cyclic.** Dotting $\mathbf u$ with Eq. 2.15 for $\mathbf v\times\mathbf w$ gives exactly the $3\times3$ determinant with rows $\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w$. A cyclic shift of rows is two row swaps, and each swap flips the sign, so the value is unchanged. Geometrically it is the signed volume of the parallelepiped on $\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w$. The volume is positive if the three vectors form a right-handed set. (This is why $\det\mathbf C=+1$ for a right-handed DCM in 2.2.)

### Vector triple product ("BAC − CAB")

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)=\mathbf v\,(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)-\mathbf w\,(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.13})
$$

**Proof ($x$-component).** Let $\mathbf c=\mathbf v\times\mathbf w$, so $c_y=v_zw_x-v_xw_z$ and $c_z=v_xw_y-v_yw_x$. Then

$$
[\mathbf u\times\mathbf c]_x=u_yc_z-u_zc_y=u_y(v_xw_y-v_yw_x)-u_z(v_zw_x-v_xw_z)
=v_x(u_yw_y+u_zw_z)-w_x(u_yv_y+u_zv_z).
$$

Add and subtract $u_xv_xw_x$:

$$
=v_x(u_xw_x+u_yw_y+u_zw_z)-w_x(u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z)=v_x(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)-w_x(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\ \checkmark
$$

The $y$ and $z$ components follow by cycling $x\to y\to z$.

> [!warning] The cross product is not associative
> $\mathbf v\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf w)\neq(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u)\times\mathbf w$ in general. Never drop or move brackets in a repeated cross product.

## 5. Example: centripetal acceleration (Eqs. 2.17–2.20)

A point $B$ moves on a circle about a fixed centre $O$ with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$. The position $\mathbf r=\overrightarrow{OB}$ has constant length.

**Velocity.** $B$ is fixed on a rigid arm rotating at $\boldsymbol\omega$, so (by the transport theorem, §8, with $\mathbf r$ fixed in the rotating arm)

$$
\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r .
$$

**Acceleration.** The speed is constant but the direction is not. The velocity is itself a vector fixed in the rotating arm, so it too changes only by rotation:

$$
\mathbf a_c=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf r=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^2\mathbf r .
$$

**Derivation of $-\omega^2\mathbf r$ (the step the notes skip).** Apply BAC−CAB with $\mathbf u=\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\mathbf w=\mathbf r$:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)=\boldsymbol\omega\,(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf r)-\mathbf r\,(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\omega).
$$

In matrix form this reads $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^2=\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T-\omega^2\mathbf I$, a useful identity in its own right. For the circular motion of Fig. 2.3, $\boldsymbol\omega\perp\mathbf r$, so $\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf r=0$ and

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_c=-\omega^2\mathbf r}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.20})
$$

It points from $B$ back towards $O$, with magnitude $\omega^2r=|\mathbf v|^2/r$, the familiar $v^2/r$.

> [!note] Lecture version (L5, ll. 1–47)
> The lecture opens with this example, held over from L4. Its message (ll. 32–46) is about **method**: applying the cross product twice "by the classical rule" is messy, whereas $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf r$ is two matrix–vector products, which is how MATLAB or Python code would do it, especially *"when you have many, many products"*. A student asked how to apply the right-hand rule for each step (ll. 19–28): curl $\boldsymbol\omega$ into $\mathbf r$ to get $\mathbf v$ (tangential), then curl $\boldsymbol\omega$ into $\mathbf v$ to get $\mathbf a_c$ (inwards).
>
> **Slip:** at l. 37 the result is transcribed as *"omega square bar"*. It is $\mathbf a_c=-\omega^2\mathbf r$, and the **minus sign is the physics**: the acceleration points opposite to $\mathbf r$, towards the centre (ll. 41–42).

> [!note] If $\mathbf r$ is not perpendicular to $\boldsymbol\omega$
> Split $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_\parallel+\mathbf r_\perp$ along and across $\boldsymbol\omega$. The general formula gives $\boldsymbol\omega(\omega r_\parallel)-\omega^2(\mathbf r_\parallel+\mathbf r_\perp)=-\omega^2\mathbf r_\perp$. The acceleration points towards the **axis**, not towards the origin. For example, a point on a spinning disc away from its centre plane still accelerates towards the shaft.

*Numbers (suggested):* $\boldsymbol\omega=[0,0,2]^T$ rad/s and $\mathbf r=[0.5,0,0]^T$ m give $\mathbf v=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf r=[0,1,0]^T$ m/s and $\mathbf a_c=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf v=[-2,0,0]^T$ m/s² $=-\omega^2\mathbf r$ ✓.

> [!tip] Why the matrix form matters
> The notes point out that $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf r$ needs only a matrix–vector multiplication. Every flight simulator and attitude estimator is written this way. You will meet $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}$ again in the rotational equations of motion ($\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega$) and in the DCM kinematic equation (preview in 2.2).

## 6. Vector derivatives depend on the observer

A derivative measures how a vector changes in **magnitude and direction**. Direction is only meaningful relative to some reference, so the derivative depends on the frame from which the change is observed (§2.2 intro). Notation:

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}=\left.\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf p}{\mathrm dt}\right|_a\quad\text{(observed from }F_a\text{)},
\qquad
{}^b\dot{\mathbf p}=\left.\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf p}{\mathrm dt}\right|_b\quad\text{(observed from }F_b\text{)}.
$$

This is the notation Chapter 1 flagged ([[SESA3047 1.2 - Dynamic Models, Frames and Earth Models#10. Position, velocity and derivative notation|1.2 §10]]).

## 7. The angular velocity vector

**Angular velocity** $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ is the rate at which frame $F_b$ rotates relative to $F_a$. Its magnitude is the rotation rate. Its direction is the instantaneous rotation axis, with the sense given by the right-hand rule. See [[Angular Velocity Vector]].

The notes list three properties. Each has a short justification:

1. **Relative motion:** $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}=-\boldsymbol\omega_{a/b}$. If $F_b$ turns clockwise as seen from $F_a$, then $F_a$ turns anticlockwise as seen from $F_b$ at the same rate.
2. **Additive over frames:** $\boldsymbol\omega_{c/a}=\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$. Apply the transport theorem twice to a vector fixed in $F_c$: ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p=(\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}\times\mathbf p)+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$. This must equal $\boldsymbol\omega_{c/a}\times\mathbf p$ for every $\mathbf p$, so the angular velocities add.
3. **Its derivative is the same in either frame:** ${}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}={}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}$. Put $\mathbf p=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ into the transport theorem; the extra term $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}=\mathbf 0$.

The lecture covers these at L5, ll. 63–96. Its memory aid for the additive law (l. 88): in $\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ the two **inner** letters $b$ "go away" and the outer letters $c/a$ remain, exactly like the DCM chain rule $\mathbf C_{c/b}\mathbf C_{b/a}=\mathbf C_{c/a}$ in 2.2.

> [!note] Transcript wording (L5, ll. 71 and 92)
> l. 71 says $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ *"means the velocity that a reference frame B passed with respect to A"*. It is the **angular** velocity of $F_b$ relative to $F_a$ (as l. 77 then says correctly). l. 92, *"if we take the derivative in one of the frames, there is no difference"*, is property 3: the derivative of $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ itself is the same seen from $F_a$ or $F_b$. It does **not** mean derivatives of other vectors are frame-independent.

### Angular acceleration is not additive (derivation)

The notes remark "*not true for angular acceleration*" without proof. Differentiate the additive law in $F_a$:

$$
{}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{c/a}={}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{c/b}+{}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}.
$$

The first term is the derivative of $\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}$, which is naturally measured in $F_b$. Transport it:

$$
\boxed{{}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{c/a}={}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{c/b}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}+{}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}}
$$

The cross term $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}$ is why simply adding the angular accelerations is wrong. Physically, a rotor spinning at a constant rate relative to an aircraft that is turning at a constant rate still has an angular acceleration relative to the ground. This is the origin of **gyroscopic** precession torques on propellers and engine spools.

## 8. The transport theorem

### Statement

$$
\boxed{\underbrace{{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}}_{\text{derivative in }F_a}=\underbrace{{}^b\dot{\mathbf p}}_{\text{derivative in }F_b}+\underbrace{\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p}_{\text{rotational contribution}}}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.22})
$$

- ${}^b\dot{\mathbf p}$: the change of $\mathbf p$ **observed within** the rotating frame $F_b$;
- $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$: the extra change seen from $F_a$ **because $F_b$ is rotating**.

It holds for **any** vector (position, velocity, momentum, angular momentum), not only positions. Translation of the frame origin does not appear, because moving a free vector without turning it changes neither its length nor its direction. See [[Transport Theorem]].

![[amc_transport_theorem_derivation.png|900]]

### Derivation 1: the displacement picture (the notes' route)

Follow the tip of $\mathbf p$ (from $Q$ to $P$) for a short time $\delta t$ (Fig. 2.4, redrawn in panel 4):

1. The observer in $F_b$ sees $P$ move to $P'$. The vector becomes $\mathbf p_1=\mathbf p+{}^b\dot{\mathbf p}\,\delta t$.
2. The observer in $F_a$ additionally sees the whole of $F_b$ turn through $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\delta t$. That carries the tip a further $(\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p)\,\delta t$ (panel 2). The vector becomes $\mathbf p_2$.
3. $F_a$ also sees $Q$ translate by $\mathbf v_{Q/a}\delta t$. That moves the point $P$ further, to $P'''$, but it does not change the **vector** $\mathbf p$, because both of its ends move together.

So

$$
\mathbf p_2-\mathbf p={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}\,\delta t+(\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p)\,\delta t\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.21}),
$$

and dividing by $\delta t\to0$ gives Eq. 2.22.

> [!question] The question asked in the lecture: why does the last step not count? (L5, ll. 168–190 and 236–250)
> A student asked whether the move from $P''$ to $P'''$ is "just translation". It is, and that is exactly why it is dropped. The lecturer's argument (ll. 170–177): a derivative of a vector measures the change in its **magnitude and direction**. Compare the vector from $Q$ to $P''$ with the vector from $Q'$ to $P'''$. They are the same arrow, slid rigidly along. Same length, same direction, so **zero** contribution to $\mathrm d\mathbf p/\mathrm dt$.
>
> Contrast the two steps that do count:
> - $P\to P'$: $P$ moves within $F_b$, so the arrow changes as $F_b$ sees it (${}^b\dot{\mathbf p}\,\delta t$);
> - $P'\to P''$: $F_b$ turns, so the arrow swings round ($(\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p)\,\delta t$).
>
> The translation of $Q$ matters for the **velocity of the point** $P$, which is $\mathbf v_{Q/a}+{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}$ (§10 below), but not for the derivative of the **vector** $\mathbf p$.

> [!tip] Remember it as
> *"Derivative seen from $a$ = derivative seen from $b$ + $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times$ (the vector)."* The lecturer calls it both the **transport theorem** and the **transport equation** (L5, ll. 199–200, 252–253), and says outright: *"this is the expression that you need to remember"* (l. 199).

### Derivation 2: differentiate the components (recommended for exams)

This route is more rigorous and shows exactly where each term comes from.

**Step 1: a vector fixed in $F_b$ only rotates** (panel 2). Take a unit vector $\mathbf b_i$ of $F_b$ at angle $\gamma$ to $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$. In $\delta t$ it turns through $|\boldsymbol\omega|\delta t$ about the axis. Its tip moves on a circle of radius $|\mathbf b_i|\sin\gamma$, a distance $|\boldsymbol\omega|\,\delta t\,|\mathbf b_i|\sin\gamma=|\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf b_i|\,\delta t$, in the direction of $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf b_i$. Hence

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf b}_i=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf b_i .
$$

**Step 2: product rule** (panel 3). Write $\mathbf p=p_1\mathbf b_1+p_2\mathbf b_2+p_3\mathbf b_3$ and differentiate as seen from $F_a$:

$$
{}^a\dot{\mathbf p}=\underbrace{\dot p_1\mathbf b_1+\dot p_2\mathbf b_2+\dot p_3\mathbf b_3}_{\text{all that }F_b\text{ can see }={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}}+\underbrace{p_1\,{}^a\dot{\mathbf b}_1+p_2\,{}^a\dot{\mathbf b}_2+p_3\,{}^a\dot{\mathbf b}_3}_{\text{the axes themselves turn}}.
$$

**Step 3: factor.** Using Step 1 and distributivity,

$$
\sum_ip_i(\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf b_i)=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\sum_ip_i\mathbf b_i=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p .
$$

The result is Eq. 2.22.

> [!tip] Intuition
> An observer in $F_b$ uses axes $\mathbf b_i$ that look fixed to them, so they only notice changes in the **numbers** $p_i$. An observer in $F_a$ sees those same numbers change **and** sees the axes swing round. The transport theorem is just the product rule with that second effect written as $\boldsymbol\omega\times$.

### Special cases (Eqs. 2.23–2.25)

| Case | Because | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf p$ fixed in $F_b$ | ${}^b\dot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf 0$ | ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$ (2.23) |
| frames not rotating relative to each other | $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}=\mathbf 0$ | ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}$ (2.24) |
| $\mathbf p$ parallel to the rotation axis | $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p=\mathbf 0$ | ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}$ (2.25) |
| $\mathbf p=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}$ | $\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf 0$ | ${}^a\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}={}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{b/a}$ |

The second case includes frames that **translate**, even with acceleration, without rotating. A derivative is observer-independent unless the observers rotate relative to each other.

The lecture goes through the first three cases in order at L5, ll. 256–314. A student answers the first one in class: *"there's only the rotational… contribution"* (l. 270).

> [!warning] Third special case: what the recording says versus what it means (L5, ll. 282–312)
> The transcript describes the third case as both frames *"moving and rotating"* with $P\neq P'$, and *"the rotation is passing through the point P"* (ll. 287–292). The precise condition is that $\mathbf p$ lies **along the rotation axis**, i.e. $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\parallel\mathbf p$. Then $\sin\alpha=0$ in $|\boldsymbol\omega||\mathbf p|\sin\alpha$, so the cross product vanishes (ll. 302–312) and only ${}^b\dot{\mathbf p}$ survives. A point on the axis is carried round by the rotation without its position vector (from a point $Q$ on the axis) changing direction.

## 9. Worked examples

### 9.1 The aircraft nose direction (the notes' example, Eqs. 2.26–2.27)

Let $\mathbf u$ be the unit vector along the body $x$-axis (the nose). It is fixed to the airframe:

$$
{}^b\dot{\mathbf u}=\mathbf 0 .
$$

From the Earth frame, special case 1 gives

$$
{}^e\dot{\mathbf u}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf u .
$$

![[amc_nose_direction_example.png|640]]

*Numbers (suggested):* in a pull-up at pitch rate $q=0.1$ rad/s, $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}=[0,\ q,\ 0]^T$ in FRD:

$$
{}^e\dot{\mathbf u}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0.1\\0\end{bmatrix}\times\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\cdot0-0\cdot0\\0\cdot1-0\cdot0\\0\cdot0-0.1\cdot1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-0.1\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$

The $-z$ direction in FRD is **up**, so the nose swings upwards at 0.1 rad/s, as expected for a pull-up. The components of $\mathbf u$ in body axes never change. Its direction changes continuously as seen from Earth.

### 9.2 Wingtip velocity in a roll (suggested)

An aircraft flies at $\mathbf v_{G/e}=[200,0,0]^T$ m/s in body axes and rolls right at $p=1$ rad/s. Find the velocity of the right wingtip, $\mathbf p=[0,\ 15,\ 0]^T$ m from the centre of gravity $G$.

The wingtip is fixed in $F_b$, so special case 1 applies to $\mathbf p$. Then

$$
\mathbf v_{tip/e}=\mathbf v_{G/e}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf p
=\begin{bmatrix}200\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\times\begin{bmatrix}0\\15\\0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}200\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\15\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}200\\0\\15\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$

The right wingtip moves **down** ($+z$) at 15 m/s, as it should in a right roll. That extra 15 m/s changes the local angle of attack at the tip. This is the physical origin of roll damping, which appears later as the derivative $L_p$.

## 10. Preview: rigid-body velocity and acceleration

*(Extension: this is where Chapter 2 leads; it is not written out in the Chapter 2 notes.)*

For a point $P$ with $\mathbf r_P=\mathbf r_Q+\mathbf p$, differentiate in $F_a$ and transport $\mathbf p$:

$$
\mathbf v_{P/a}=\mathbf v_{Q/a}+{}^b\dot{\mathbf p}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p .
$$

Transport once more to get the acceleration:

$$
{}^a\ddot{\mathbf p}={}^b\ddot{\mathbf p}+2\,\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^b\dot{\mathbf p}+\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf p+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p).
$$

The terms are, in order: relative acceleration, **Coriolis**, **angular (Euler)**, and **centripetal**. The last is exactly the §5 result. In SESA2027 the force equations in body axes contained $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ terms ($qW-rV$, …). They are the transport theorem applied to the velocity vector: ${}^e\dot{\mathbf v}={}^b\dot{\mathbf v}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf v$ ([[Euler Angles and Rotation Matrices]], "Rotating frame").

## 11. Common mistakes

1. Taking $\mathbf u^T\mathbf v$ or $\tilde{\mathbf u}\mathbf v$ with the two arrays in **different** coordinate systems.
2. Losing the minus sign on the middle ($\mathbf j$) component of the determinant.
3. Writing $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$ as $\mathbf r\times\boldsymbol\omega$, which flips the answer.
4. Moving brackets in $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)$.
5. Forgetting the $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p$ term when a derivative is taken in a rotating frame, or adding it when both observers share the same rotation.
6. Adding angular accelerations of nested frames without the $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\boldsymbol\omega_{c/b}$ cross term.
