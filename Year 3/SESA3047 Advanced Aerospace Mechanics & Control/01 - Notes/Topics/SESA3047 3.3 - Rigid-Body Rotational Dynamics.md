# SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics

> [!abstract] Summary
> Referring the motion to the **centre of mass** separates translation from rotation. Linear momentum is $\mathbf Q=m\mathbf v_{cm}$, and Newton gives $\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a_{cm/i}$. For rotation, sum the moment of momentum of every mass element about the cm. The term in $\mathbf v_{cm}$ integrates to zero, because $\int\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm=\mathbf 0$ about the cm. The rest becomes $-\int\tilde{\mathbf r}^2\mathrm dm\,\boldsymbol\omega$, which defines the **inertia matrix**:
>
> $$\mathbf h^{FRD}_{cm/i}=\mathbf J^{FRD}\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}.$$
>
> $\mathbf J$ is symmetric and, in body axes, **constant**. Newton for rotation, $\sum\mathbf M_{cm}={}^i\dot{\mathbf h}$, is converted to body axes with the transport theorem. Since $\dot{\mathbf J}=\mathbf 0$ there, this gives the **rotational dynamic equation**
>
> $${}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\mathbf J^{-1}\left(\sum\mathbf M_{cm}-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega\right).$$
>
> The gyroscopic term $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ couples the axes. In principal axes the equation reduces to Euler's equations, e.g. $J_x\dot p=L+(J_y-J_z)qr$. Together with the two kinematic equations of 3.1 and 3.2, it gives 9 of the 12 state equations of the 6-DOF model. The translational dynamics complete them in Chapter 4.

## The topic in one physical picture

Hold a bicycle wheel by its axle and spin it. Now try to tilt the axle: the wheel twists sideways instead. Throw a tennis racket spinning about its handle-to-tip axis and it spins cleanly. Throw it spinning about the axis across the strings and it also spins cleanly. Throw it spinning about the third axis, in the plane of the strings across the handle, and it flips over every turn.

None of these needs an exotic force. They all come from one fact: for a rigid body **angular momentum is not generally parallel to angular velocity**, $\mathbf h=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$, and $\mathbf h$, not $\boldsymbol\omega$, is what moments change. When $\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\mathbf h$ point in different directions, the body rotating carries $\mathbf h$ round with it. Keeping $\mathbf h$ fixed in inertial space then requires $\boldsymbol\omega$ to change: that is the $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ term.

For an aircraft this matters whenever it rolls and yaws at the same time: inertia coupling, spinning propellers and engines, roll-coupling departures of high-performance jets.

## 1. Why use the centre of mass (§3.3 introduction)

Taking the **centre of mass** (cm) as the reference point lets us write the translational and rotational equations separately:

- **Translation:** the acceleration of the cm depends only on the **sum** of the forces. It does not matter where they act.
- **Rotation:** the moments about the cm account for any force whose line of action misses the cm.

The two sets remain coupled in a full aircraft model (aerodynamic forces depend on attitude and rates), but they no longer share terms such as $m\,\mathbf r_{cm}\times\mathbf a$. The equations are written in **body-fixed FRD** coordinates.

## 2. Linear momentum and Newton's second law (§3.3.1, Eqs. 3.43–3.46)

**Definition** (3.43): sum the momentum of every element of the body $B$:

$$
\mathbf Q=\int_B\mathbf v_{P/i}\,\mathrm dm.
$$

**Use the rigid-body velocity** of an element at $\mathbf r$ from the cm, $\mathbf v_{P/i}=\mathbf v_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}\times\mathbf r$ ([[SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames#Rearrange (Eq. 3.23)|3.2]] with $\mathbf v_{P/b}=\mathbf 0$):

$$
\mathbf Q=\int_B\mathbf v_{cm/i}\,\mathrm dm+\int_B\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm.
$$

$\mathbf v_{cm}$ and $\boldsymbol\omega$ are the same for every element, so take them out:

$$
\mathbf Q=\mathbf v_{cm/i}\int_B\mathrm dm+\boldsymbol\omega\times\int_B\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm.
$$

The **definition of the centre of mass** is $\int_B\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm=\mathbf 0$ when $\mathbf r$ is measured from it. So (3.44)

$$
\boxed{\mathbf Q=m\,\mathbf v_{cm/i}}.
$$

**Newton's second law** in the inertial frame (3.45), for constant mass (3.46):

$$
\sum\mathbf F_{ext}={}^i\dot{\mathbf Q}=m\,\mathbf a_{cm/i}.
$$

The forces are aerodynamic, propulsive, control and gravitational. Combining this with the body-axis acceleration $[\dot u+qw-rv,\dots]^T$ of [[SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames#4. Differentiating the velocity in the body frame (§3.2.2 Eq. 3.34, §3.2.4 Eqs. 3.41–3.42)|3.2 §4]] gives the translational dynamic equation (Chapter 4).

## 3. Angular momentum about the centre of mass (§3.3.2, Eqs. 3.47–3.63)

![[amc_angular_momentum_derivation.png|900]]

**Newton for rotation** (3.47): the resultant moment about the cm equals the **inertial** rate of change of angular momentum,

$$
\sum\mathbf M_{cm}={}^i\dot{\mathbf h}_{cm/i}.
$$

**One particle** (3.48, Fig. 3.5): for a mass $m$ at $\mathbf r_{P/O}$ with velocity $\mathbf v_{P/i}$,

$$
\mathbf h_{P/O}=\mathbf r_{P/O}\times m\,\mathbf v_{P/i}.
$$

### Step 1: one mass element (Eqs. 3.49–3.50)

A rigid body is a continuum of elements. The element $\delta m$ at $\mathbf r$ from the cm has velocity $\mathbf v_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}\times\mathbf r$, hence momentum

$$
\delta\mathbf P=\left(\mathbf v_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}\times\mathbf r\right)\delta m,
$$

and moment of momentum about the cm

$$
\delta\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\delta\mathbf P=\mathbf r\times\left(\mathbf v_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}\times\mathbf r\right)\delta m.
$$

### Step 2: integrate and drop the centre-of-mass term (Eqs. 3.51–3.54)

$$
\mathbf h_{cm/i}=\iiint_B\mathbf r\times\mathbf v_{cm/i}\,\mathrm dm+\iiint_B\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,\mathrm dm.
$$

In the first integral $\mathbf v_{cm/i}$ is the same for every element:

$$
\iiint_B\mathbf r\times\mathbf v_{cm/i}\,\mathrm dm=\left(\iiint_B\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm\right)\times\mathbf v_{cm/i}=\mathbf 0\times\mathbf v_{cm/i}=\mathbf 0.
$$

> [!tip] Why this is the reason for choosing the cm
> About any other point the first integral would leave a term $m\,\mathbf r_{cm}\times\mathbf v$ that couples the angular momentum to the translational velocity. About the cm it vanishes, and rotation separates cleanly from translation.

### Step 3: turn the double cross product into a matrix (Eqs. 3.55–3.57)

To integrate we need coordinates. Choose **body-fixed FRD**, so that the mass distribution does not change with time (3.52): $\mathbf r^{FRD}=[x,y,z]^T$. Write each cross product with the [[Cross-Product Matrix]], $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\tilde{\mathbf a}\mathbf b$:

$$
\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)=\tilde{\mathbf r}\,(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).
$$

Swap the order of the inner cross product; it changes sign (anticommutativity):

$$
=\tilde{\mathbf r}\,(-\mathbf r\times\boldsymbol\omega)=-\tilde{\mathbf r}\,\tilde{\mathbf r}\,\boldsymbol\omega.
$$

$\boldsymbol\omega$ is the same everywhere in a rigid body, so it leaves the integral:

$$
\mathbf h^{FRD}_{cm/i}=\left(-\iiint_B\tilde{\mathbf r}^{\,2}\,\mathrm dm\right)\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}.
$$

### Step 4: square the skew matrix (Eqs. 3.58–3.59)

$$
\tilde{\mathbf r}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-z&y\\z&0&-x\\-y&x&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row 1 of $\tilde{\mathbf r}$ times each column:

$$
(0)(0)+(-z)(z)+(y)(-y)=-(y^2+z^2),
$$

$$
(0)(-z)+(-z)(0)+(y)(x)=xy,
$$

$$
(0)(y)+(-z)(-x)+(y)(0)=xz.
$$

Rows 2 and 3 follow the same pattern (checked symbolically):

$$
\tilde{\mathbf r}^{\,2}=\begin{bmatrix}-(y^2+z^2)&xy&xz\\xy&-(x^2+z^2)&yz\\xz&yz&-(x^2+y^2)\end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Step 5: the inertia matrix (Eqs. 3.60–3.63)

Insert the minus sign and integrate entry by entry:

$$
\mathbf J^{FRD}=-\iiint_B\tilde{\mathbf r}^{\,2}\,\mathrm dm=\iiint_B\begin{bmatrix}y^2+z^2&-xy&-xz\\-xy&x^2+z^2&-yz\\-xz&-yz&x^2+y^2\end{bmatrix}\mathrm dm=\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}&-J_{xy}&-J_{xz}\\-J_{xy}&J_{yy}&-J_{yz}\\-J_{xz}&-J_{yz}&J_{zz}\end{bmatrix},
$$

with **moments of inertia** and **products of inertia** (3.61)

$$
J_{xx}=\iiint_B(y^2+z^2)\,\mathrm dm,\quad J_{yy}=\iiint_B(x^2+z^2)\,\mathrm dm,\quad J_{zz}=\iiint_B(x^2+y^2)\,\mathrm dm,
$$

$$
J_{xy}=\iiint_Bxy\,\mathrm dm,\quad J_{xz}=\iiint_Bxz\,\mathrm dm,\quad J_{yz}=\iiint_Byz\,\mathrm dm.
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathbf h^{FRD}_{cm/i}=\mathbf J^{FRD}\,\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}}\qquad(3.63)
$$

### Properties of J

- **Symmetric**: $J_{xy}=J_{yx}$ and so on (3.63 text).
- **Constant in body axes** for a rigid body, even though the body turns relative to the inertial frame. In NED axes it would change every time the aircraft rotated. This is the reason for working in FRD.
- **Depends on the origin and the axes**: unlike the scalar mass, it changes if you move the reference point or rotate the axes.
- **Positive diagonal**: each $J_{xx}$ is $\int(\text{distance from the axis})^2\mathrm dm>0$.
- **Principal axes** (beyond the notes; see [[Inertia Matrix]]): there is always a set of body axes in which $\mathbf J$ is diagonal. Only about a principal axis are $\mathbf h$ and $\boldsymbol\omega$ parallel.
- **Aircraft symmetry**: an aircraft symmetric about its $x$–$z$ plane has $J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$. Only $J_{xz}$ survives, because the nose and fin are not on the same axis.

> [!warning] Sign convention
> The notes define $J_{xy}=+\int xy\,\mathrm dm$ and put the **minus signs in the matrix** (3.62). Some textbooks fold the sign into the definition, writing $I_{xy}=-\int xy\,\mathrm dm$ with no signs in the matrix. Check which convention a data sheet uses before substituting a product of inertia. SESA2024's [[Inertia Matrix]] and SESA2027 use the same convention as here.

### Worked example: a dumbbell, and why h is not parallel to ω (suggested)

Two 1 kg point masses at $\mathbf r_1=[1,1,0]^T$ m and $\mathbf r_2=[-1,-1,0]^T$ m, so the cm is at the origin.

**Each mass** contributes $y^2+z^2=1$ to $J_{xx}$, $x^2+z^2=1$ to $J_{yy}$, $x^2+y^2=2$ to $J_{zz}$, and $xy=1$ to $J_{xy}$ ($xz=yz=0$). For both masses:

$$
J_{xx}=2,\quad J_{yy}=2,\quad J_{zz}=4,\quad J_{xy}=2\ \text{kg m}^2,
\qquad
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&0\\-2&2&0\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Spin it about $x$** at $\boldsymbol\omega=[1,0,0]^T$ rad/s:

$$
\mathbf h=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega=[2,-2,0]^T\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}.
$$

$\mathbf h$ points at $-45^\circ$ in the $x$–$y$ plane, **not** along $\boldsymbol\omega$. Direct check for mass 1: $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_1=[0,0,1]^T$ and $\mathbf r_1\times\mathbf v=[1,-1,0]^T$; mass 2 gives the same, so the total is $[2,-2,0]^T$ ✓.

(The eigenvalues of $\mathbf J$ are 0, 4 and 4. The zero belongs to the rod axis $[1,1,0]/\sqrt2$: point masses on an axis have no inertia about it.)

## 4. The rotational dynamic equation (§3.3.3, Eqs. 3.64–3.71)

![[amc_rotational_dynamics_derivation.png|900]]

### Step 1: Newton for rotation, inertial derivative (3.64)

$$
\sum\mathbf M_{cm}={}^i\dot{\mathbf h}_{cm/i}.
$$

### Step 2: transport theorem to the body frame (3.65–3.66)

$\mathbf h$ is simplest in body axes, so convert the derivative:

$$
{}^i\dot{\mathbf h}_{cm/i}={}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}\times\mathbf h_{cm/i}.
$$

The first term is the rate of change seen by someone riding the body; the second is the change caused by the body frame rotating. Express everything in FRD:

$$
\left(\sum\mathbf M_{cm}\right)^{FRD}={}^b\dot{\mathbf h}^{FRD}_{cm/i}+\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}\times\mathbf h^{FRD}_{cm/i}.
$$

### Step 3: substitute h = Jω (3.67–3.70)

$$
\left(\sum\mathbf M_{cm}\right)^{FRD}={}^b\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\left(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega\right)+\boldsymbol\omega\times\left(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega\right).
$$

**First term, product rule:**

$$
{}^b\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)=\dot{\mathbf J}\boldsymbol\omega+\mathbf J\,{}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}.
$$

The mass distribution is fixed in body axes, so $\dot{\mathbf J}^{FRD}=\mathbf 0$ (3.68):

$$
{}^b\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)=\mathbf J\,{}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}.
$$

**Second term, skew matrix** (3.69):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\,\mathbf J\,\boldsymbol\omega.
$$

**Together** (3.70):

$$
\left(\sum\mathbf M_{cm}\right)^{FRD}=\mathbf J\,{}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}^{FRD}_{b/i}+\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^{FRD}_{b/i}\,\mathbf J\,\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}.
$$

### Step 4: solve for the state derivative (3.71)

$\mathbf J$ is invertible (positive definite for a real body). Subtract the gyroscopic term and premultiply by $\mathbf J^{-1}$:

$$
\boxed{{}^b\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}^{FRD}_{b/i}=\left(\mathbf J^{FRD}\right)^{-1}\left[\left(\sum\mathbf M_{cm}\right)^{FRD}-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^{FRD}_{b/i}\,\mathbf J^{FRD}\,\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/i}\right]}
$$

This is the **angular-velocity state equation** or [[Rotational Dynamic Equation]]. Given the current $\boldsymbol\omega$, the inertia and the applied moment, it returns $\dot p,\dot q,\dot r$, ready for numerical integration.

- **First term:** the applied external moment (aerodynamic, thrust, control surfaces).
- **Second term:** the change of angular momentum associated with the rotating body frame. It is purely inertial: it exists with no applied moment at all.

### Step 5: principal axes, Euler's equations (extension)

If the FRD axes are principal, $\mathbf J=\mathrm{diag}(J_x,J_y,J_z)$, then $\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega=[J_xp,J_yq,J_zr]^T$ and

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega=\begin{bmatrix}q\,J_zr-r\,J_yq\\r\,J_xp-p\,J_zr\\p\,J_yq-q\,J_xp\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(J_z-J_y)\,qr\\(J_x-J_z)\,rp\\(J_y-J_x)\,pq\end{bmatrix}.
$$

With $\sum\mathbf M_{cm}=[L,M,N]^T$, each row of (3.70) reads

$$
\boxed{J_x\dot p=L+(J_y-J_z)\,qr,\qquad J_y\dot q=M+(J_z-J_x)\,rp,\qquad J_z\dot r=N+(J_x-J_y)\,pq.}
$$

The pattern is cyclic, $x\to y\to z\to x$: each rate is driven by the **product of the other two**.

### Step 6: the aircraft case with J_xz ≠ 0 (extension)

With $x$–$z$ symmetry ($J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$),

$$
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}J_x&0&-J_{xz}\\0&J_y&0\\-J_{xz}&0&J_z\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}J_x\dot p-J_{xz}\dot r\\J_y\dot q\\-J_{xz}\dot p+J_z\dot r\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiplying out $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ (checked in SymPy) gives the three moment equations:

$$
L=J_x\dot p-J_{xz}\dot r+(J_z-J_y)\,qr-J_{xz}\,pq,
$$

$$
M=J_y\dot q+(J_x-J_z)\,pr+J_{xz}\left(p^2-r^2\right),
$$

$$
N=J_z\dot r-J_{xz}\dot p+(J_y-J_x)\,pq+J_{xz}\,qr.
$$

- **Roll and yaw are coupled** through $J_{xz}$ even with no gyroscopic terms: an aileron rolling moment also produces some yaw acceleration.
- The $L$ equation is exactly the SESA2027 one with $I_{xy}=I_{yz}=0$ ([[SESA2027 A1 - Dynamic Systems and Aircraft Equations of Motion#3. Inertia tensor and full equations (L1.03)|SESA2027 A1 §3]]).
- Solving the $L$ and $N$ equations together for $\dot p$ and $\dot r$ involves $\Gamma=J_xJ_z-J_{xz}^2$, the determinant of the $x$–$z$ block. This is how simulation codes (e.g. Stevens and Lewis) write it.

## 5. Worked examples

### 5.1 Gyroscopic coupling in a light aircraft (suggested)

Representative inertias of a light four-seat aircraft, principal axes assumed ($J_{xz}\approx0$): $J_x=1285$, $J_y=1825$, $J_z=2667$ kg m². Body rates $\boldsymbol\omega=[0.5,\ 0.1,\ 0.05]^T$ rad/s (a brisk roll with some pitch and yaw).

**Gyroscopic term**, row by row:

$$
(J_z-J_y)\,qr=(2667-1825)(0.1)(0.05)=4.21\ \text{N m},
$$

$$
(J_x-J_z)\,rp=(1285-2667)(0.05)(0.5)=-34.55\ \text{N m},
$$

$$
(J_y-J_x)\,pq=(1825-1285)(0.5)(0.1)=27.0\ \text{N m}.
$$

**With no applied moment**, $\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=-\mathbf J^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$:

$$
\dot p=\frac{-4.21}{1285}=-0.0033,\qquad
\dot q=\frac{34.55}{1825}=+0.0189,\qquad
\dot r=\frac{-27.0}{2667}=-0.0101\ \text{rad/s}^2.
$$

The rolling aircraft **pitches up by itself** at $\approx1^\circ$/s². That is inertial (roll) coupling. It is small here, but it grows with $p^2$ and with the inertia differences. In the 1950s it sent long, heavy-fuselage jets such as the X-3 and F-100 out of control in rapid rolls.

**With an aileron moment** $L=500$ N m: $\dot p=(500-4.21)/1285=0.386$ rad/s², with $\dot q$ and $\dot r$ unchanged. (Verified numerically.)

### 5.2 The dumbbell again: a bearing load from imbalance (suggested)

Keep the dumbbell of §3 spinning steadily about $x$ ($\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\mathbf 0$). From (3.70) the required moment is just the gyroscopic term:

$$
\mathbf M=\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h=[1,0,0]^T\times[2,-2,0]^T=[0,0,-2]^T\ \text{N m}.
$$

A steady spin about a **non-principal** axis needs a continual moment, which rotates with the body. This is the dynamic imbalance that the bearings of a badly balanced wheel, propeller or turbine disc have to supply: it is why they are balanced on spinning rigs.

### 5.3 Torque-free motion and the intermediate-axis theorem (suggested)

![[amc_intermediate_axis.png|900]]

With $\mathbf M=\mathbf 0$ and $\mathbf J=\mathrm{diag}(1,2,3)$, integrate (3.71) from a spin of 1 rad/s about each axis in turn, plus a 1% disturbance:

- about the **minor** ($x$) and **major** ($z$) axes the disturbance just wobbles: **stable**;
- about the **intermediate** ($y$) axis it grows until the body **flips** over, again and again (the tennis-racket or Dzhanibekov effect).

Kinetic energy $\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ and $|\mathbf h|$ stay constant to $10^{-10}$ in the simulation, as they must with no moment. Linearising Euler's equations about the spin shows why: the perturbation equation for spin about $y$ has a positive real eigenvalue when $J_y$ lies between $J_x$ and $J_z$. Spacecraft designers use this: a spinning satellite must spin about its major axis (energy dissipation makes even the minor axis unstable; see [[Momentum Bias and Gyroscopic Rigidity]]).

## 6. Connecting kinematics and dynamics (§3.4)

![[amc_6dof_structure.png|900]]

| Equation (compact form) | Purpose | States |
|---|---|---|
| $\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\mathbf J^{-1}(\mathbf M-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)$ | **rotational dynamic equation**: moments and inertia give the angular acceleration | $p,q,r$ |
| $\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}=\mathbf H(\boldsymbol\Phi)\boldsymbol\omega$ | **rotational kinematic equation**: body rates give the Euler-angle rates ([[SESA3047 3.1 - Rotational Kinematics and Euler-Angle Rates|3.1]]) | $\phi,\theta,\psi$ |
| $\dot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf C^T\mathbf v$ | **translational kinematic equation**: body velocity gives the NED velocity ([[SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames|3.2]]) | $x,y,z$ |

> [!important] Key idea (notes, p. 14)
> **Dynamics** decides how forces and moments change the velocity and angular velocity. **Kinematics** then uses those velocities to propagate position and attitude. Together they form the nonlinear 6-DOF model. Nine of its twelve states are now covered; the translational dynamics ($u,v,w$) follow in Chapter 4.

The diagram also shows the couplings that make the model nonlinear: $p,q,r$ enter the translational dynamics through $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$; the attitude sets $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$ for the position equation; and the attitude feeds back into the forces through gravity in body axes, $g[-s_\theta,\ s_\phi c_\theta,\ c_\phi c_\theta]^T$.

## Common mistakes

1. Writing $\mathbf h\parallel\boldsymbol\omega$, i.e. using a scalar inertia, for a body with products of inertia.
2. Applying $\sum\mathbf M=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$ and forgetting the gyroscopic term $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$.
3. Taking moments about a point other than the cm (or a fixed point) without the extra $m\,\mathbf r_{cm}\times\mathbf a$ term.
4. Mixing sign conventions for products of inertia.
5. Writing $\mathbf J$ in NED axes and then differentiating as if it were constant.
