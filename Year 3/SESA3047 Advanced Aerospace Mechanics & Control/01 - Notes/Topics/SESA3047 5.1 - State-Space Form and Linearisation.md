# SESA3047 5.1 - State-Space Form and Linearisation

> [!abstract] Summary
> A state-space model writes a dynamic system as first-order matrix ODEs; the linear form is $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf y=\mathbf C\mathbf x+\mathbf D\mathbf u$. The rigid-body equations of Chapter 4 are nonlinear, so they are first linearised about an equilibrium: every variable becomes its equilibrium value plus $\epsilon$ times a perturbation, and only terms of order $\epsilon^1$ are kept. About straight, steady flight with no rotation, the force equations reduce to $X_1=m\dot u_1$, $Y_1=m(\dot v_1+r_1U_\infty)$, $Z_1=m(\dot w_1-q_1U_\infty)$, and the moment equations to Eq. 5.8.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a question gives nonlinear equations of motion (aircraft or made up) and asks for the equilibrium points, the linearised equations, or the state-space form.
> - **Assumptions:** linearise about an equilibrium (all derivatives zero there); perturbations small ($\epsilon\ll1$), angles in radians; for the aircraft, trim $(U,V,W)=(U_\infty,0,0)$ and $(p,q,r)=(0,0,0)$, constant $m$ and $g$, $x$–$z$ symmetry ($J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$), no internal rotors.
> - **Method:** 1. Find the equilibrium (set every time derivative to zero). 2. Write each variable as equilibrium $+\,\epsilon\times$ perturbation, and each derivative as $\epsilon\times$ perturbation rate. 3. Substitute and expand; use $\sin(A+B)$, $\cos(A+B)$ with $\cos\epsilon x_1\approx1$, $\sin\epsilon x_1\approx\epsilon x_1$. 4. Check that the $\epsilon^0$ terms are the equilibrium and cancel; drop $\epsilon^2$ and higher. 5. Divide by $\epsilon$ and arrange as $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$.
> - **Common mistakes:** dropping $\epsilon r_1U_\infty$ along with the $\epsilon^2$ terms (a product survives when one factor has a non-zero equilibrium value); quoting a linearisation without saying which point it is about.
> - **Practise:** [[#3. Linearising the force equations (5.3–5.7)]] · [[SESA3047 Chapter 5 Practice Problems - Solutions#Problem 1. Equilibria and linearisation of a made-up system|Practice problem 1]]

The Chapter 4 model is twelve coupled nonlinear ODEs. They are fine for simulation, but almost nothing can be said about them on paper: there is no general way to tell whether an aircraft disturbed from steady flight will return to it. Restricting attention to small departures from one steady condition makes the products of small quantities negligible and the equations linear, and linear equations have a complete theory: their stability is decided by the eigenvalues of one matrix ([[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability|5.4]]).

A linear model belongs to one operating point. Linearising somewhere else gives a different model, possibly with opposite stability (the $\sin x$ example of §2 is unstable at one equilibrium and stable at another), so any answer should begin by stating the equilibrium and what the perturbations are measured from.

## 1. What state-space form is

The linear state-space form is

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u,\qquad(5.1\text{a})
$$

$$
\mathbf y=\mathbf C\mathbf x+\mathbf D\mathbf u.\qquad(5.1\text{b})
$$

| Symbol | Course name | Size | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf x$ | state vector | $n\times1$ | the $n$ states; for a car on a line, position $x_1$ and velocity $x_2$ |
| $\mathbf u$ | input vector | $m\times1$ | what can be manipulated, e.g. control surfaces |
| $\mathbf y$ | output vector | $p\times1$ | what sensors measure, e.g. a Pitot tube |
| $\mathbf A$ | state matrix | $n\times n$ | the linearised dynamics; set by the physics, the operating point and the design of the vehicle |
| $\mathbf B$ | control matrix | $n\times m$ | how the $m$ actuators push the states; usually $m\le n$ |
| $\mathbf C$ | output matrix | $p\times n$ | converts states into measurements; usually $p\le n$ |
| $\mathbf D$ | feedforward matrix | $p\times m$ | inputs reaching the output directly, before the system responds |

$\mathbf A$ has to be square for two reasons. It left-multiplies an $n\times1$ vector and must return an $n\times1$ vector (the left-hand side is $\dot{\mathbf x}$), and only a square matrix has eigenvalues, which is where the stability will come from.

$\mathbf B$ is needed for control design (Chapters 7–8), $\mathbf D$ is not used at all, and $\mathbf C$ is almost always the identity, so $\mathbf y=\mathbf x$. The exceptions are the satellite and transfer-function examples of [[SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams|5.3]] have a single sensor and a $1\times4$ or $1\times3$ $\mathbf C$.

The form is linear, unlike almost everything in Chapters 2–4, and it is first order. Newton's second law is second order in position, because force sets the rate of change of momentum, so the second derivative appears; each second-order equation is converted to two first-order states ([[SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams#1. Second-order equations as first-order states (5.19–5.24)|5.3 §1]]). The aircraft equations in §3 already contain only first derivatives of $u,v,w,p,q,r$, so they fit $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ without that step.

## 2. Linearising about an equilibrium: the sine example

Take the one-variable system $\dot x=\sin x$. Its equilibrium points are where $\dot x=0$, that is $\sin x_0=0$, so $x_0=0,\pm\pi,\pm2\pi,\dots$: infinitely many, because $\sin$ is periodic. $\sin x$ is nonlinear: its Taylor series

$$
\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots
$$

has powers above one. The exam formula sheet will give these series; you will not need to memorise them.

Write the state as equilibrium plus a small perturbation,

$$
x=x_0+\epsilon x_1,\qquad\epsilon\ll1.
$$

Expand with the compound-angle formula:

$$
\sin(x_0+\epsilon x_1)=\sin x_0\cos(\epsilon x_1)+\cos x_0\sin(\epsilon x_1).
$$

Use $\cos(\epsilon x_1)=1-\tfrac12\epsilon^2x_1^2+\cdots$ and $\sin(\epsilon x_1)=\epsilon x_1-\tfrac16\epsilon^3x_1^3+\cdots$:

$$
\sin(x_0+\epsilon x_1)=\sin x_0+\epsilon x_1\cos x_0+O(\epsilon^2).
$$

The first term is zero because $x_0$ is an equilibrium. Drop $O(\epsilon^2)$:

$$
\sin(x_0+\epsilon x_1)\approx\epsilon x_1\cos x_0.
$$

About $x_0=0$, $\cos x_0=1$, so $\sin x\approx x$: the tangent $y=x$, which is the small-angle approximation. About $x_0=\pi$, $\cos x_0=-1$, so $\sin x\approx-\epsilon x_1=-(x-\pi)$. The tangent there is $y=\pi-x$; "$y=-x$" gives only the slope, and holds as a line only with $x$ measured from $\pi$.

![[amc_sin_linearisation.png|900]]

The two linear models predict opposite behaviour. For $\dot x=\sin x$, the model about $0$ is $\dot x_1=x_1$, which grows: the equilibrium is unstable. The model about $\pi$ is $\dot x_1=-x_1$, which decays: stable. The pendulum is the physical version: hanging down and inverted are both equilibria, one stable and one not, and the linearisation is a different model at each.

The right-hand plot shows how the error of the linear model grows with the size of the perturbation. Dropping the cubic term costs 0.51 % at a 10° perturbation and 2.06 % at 20°. The dropped term itself is cubic, $-(\epsilon x_1)^3/6$, so the relative error $(\epsilon x_1-\sin\epsilon x_1)/\sin\epsilon x_1\approx(\epsilon x_1)^2/6$ is second order: $(0.1745)^2/6=0.51\,\%$ at 10°. The linear model degrades smoothly rather than failing at a sharp limit.

The $\epsilon$ expansion does this for many variables at once:

- terms in $\epsilon^1$ are kept;
- terms in $\epsilon^2$ or higher are second-order small and dropped (expansions are to first order only);
- terms in $\epsilon^0$ must reproduce the equilibrium condition and cancel. If they do not, there is an algebra mistake.

This is the same Jacobian linearisation as [[SESA3047 1.2 - Dynamic Models, Frames and Earth Models#5. Linear and nonlinear models|1.2 §5]], $\mathbf A=\partial\mathbf f/\partial\mathbf x|_0$; the $\epsilon$ method just reaches it by expansion instead of by differentiation. Both methods are applied in [[SESA3047 Chapter 5 Practice Problems - Solutions#Problem 1. Equilibria and linearisation of a made-up system|Practice problem 1]].

## 3. Linearising the force equations (5.3–5.7)

Start from the Chapter 4 equations in body axes (Eq. 5.2, from [[SESA3047 4.2 - Translational Dynamics|4.2]] and [[SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics|3.3]]):

$$
\mathbf F_B=m(\dot{\mathbf v}_B+\boldsymbol\omega_B\times\mathbf v_B),\qquad(5.2\text{a})
$$

$$
\mathbf M_B=\dot{\mathbf h}_B+\boldsymbol\omega_B\times\mathbf h_B.\qquad(5.2\text{b})
$$

Here $\mathbf F_B=[X,Y,Z]^T$ includes the weight, the $X,Y,Z$ convention of [[SESA3047 4.2 - Translational Dynamics#Forces including gravity|4.2 §4]]. Written out (the cross product $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v=[qW-rV,\ rU-pW,\ pV-qU]^T$):

$$
X=m\left(\frac{dU}{dt}+qW-rV\right),\qquad(5.3\text{a})
$$

$$
Y=m\left(\frac{dV}{dt}+rU-pW\right),\qquad(5.3\text{b})
$$

$$
Z=m\left(\frac{dW}{dt}+pV-qU\right).\qquad(5.3\text{c})
$$

Each has a product of two unknowns, which is what makes them nonlinear.

### The equilibrium

The equilibrium is straight, steady flight with no rotation:

$$
(U,V,W)=(U_\infty,0,0),\qquad(p,q,r)=(0,0,0).
$$

$U_\infty$ lies along $x$, so these are axes aligned with the trim velocity (textbooks call them stability axes). With constant velocity and no rotation every acceleration term in Eq. 5.3 is zero, so this is an equilibrium when the trim forces are $X_0=Y_0=Z_0=0$: thrust, drag, lift and weight balance. The only simpler equilibrium has everything zero.

### The perturbations (5.4–5.5)

$$
U=U_\infty+\epsilon u_1,\qquad\dot U=\epsilon\dot u_1,
$$

$$
V=\epsilon v_1,\qquad\dot V=\epsilon\dot v_1,
$$

$$
W=\epsilon w_1,\qquad\dot W=\epsilon\dot w_1,
$$

$$
p=\epsilon p_1,\quad q=\epsilon q_1,\quad r=\epsilon r_1,\qquad\dot p=\epsilon\dot p_1,\ \text{etc.}
$$

$\dot U=\epsilon\dot u_1$ because $U_\infty$ is constant. The forces are perturbed the same way, $X=X_0+\epsilon X_1$ with $X_0=0$ here, so the left-hand side is $\epsilon X_1$.

### The X equation

Substitute into Eq. 5.3a:

$$
\epsilon X_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot u_1+(\epsilon q_1)(\epsilon w_1)-(\epsilon r_1)(\epsilon v_1)\big).
$$

Multiply out:

$$
\epsilon X_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot u_1+\epsilon^2q_1w_1-\epsilon^2r_1v_1\big).\qquad(5.6\text{a})
$$

Drop the $\epsilon^2$ terms:

$$
\epsilon X_1=m\epsilon\dot u_1.
$$

Divide by $\epsilon$:

$$
\boxed{X_1=m\dot u_1.}\qquad(5.7\text{a})
$$

Only one term is left. $U$ itself does not appear in Eq. 5.3a, only its derivative, so the non-zero $U_\infty$ never enters.

### The Y equation

Substitute into Eq. 5.3b:

$$
\epsilon Y_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot v_1+(\epsilon r_1)(U_\infty+\epsilon u_1)-(\epsilon p_1)(\epsilon w_1)\big).
$$

Multiply out:

$$
\epsilon Y_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot v_1+\epsilon r_1U_\infty+\epsilon^2r_1u_1-\epsilon^2p_1w_1\big).\qquad(5.6\text{b})
$$

The middle term is first order: it is a product of a perturbation with the equilibrium $U_\infty$. Keep it and drop the last two:

$$
\epsilon Y_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot v_1+\epsilon r_1U_\infty\big).
$$

Divide by $\epsilon$:

$$
\boxed{Y_1=m(\dot v_1+r_1U_\infty).}\qquad(5.7\text{b})
$$

### The Z equation

Substitute into Eq. 5.3c:

$$
\epsilon Z_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot w_1+(\epsilon p_1)(\epsilon v_1)-(\epsilon q_1)(U_\infty+\epsilon u_1)\big).
$$

Multiply out:

$$
\epsilon Z_1=m\big(\epsilon\dot w_1+\epsilon^2p_1v_1-\epsilon q_1U_\infty-\epsilon^2q_1u_1\big).\qquad(5.6\text{c})
$$

Drop the $\epsilon^2$ terms and divide by $\epsilon$:

$$
\boxed{Z_1=m(\dot w_1-q_1U_\infty).}\qquad(5.7\text{c})
$$

All six product terms are nonlinear, but a product of two perturbations is second order and disappears, while a product with a non-zero equilibrium value ($rU$, $qU$) leaves a first-order term. The choice of equilibrium therefore decides which terms survive. The $\epsilon$ on both sides cancels in the end.

The surviving terms have a clear meaning. $mU_\infty r_1$ in the side-force equation is the centripetal force needed to turn the velocity vector: yawing at $r_1$ while moving at $U_\infty$ swings the velocity sideways. $-mU_\infty q_1$ is the same thing in pitch, the normal force needed to curve the flight path.

## 4. Linearising the moment equations (5.8)

The equilibrium value $U_\infty$ does not appear, because these equations involve only angular rates. The result is Eq. 5.8; the algebra follows.

For a rigid body with no internal rotor, $\mathbf h_B=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega_B$ and $\dot{\mathbf h}_B=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_B$ ([[SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics|3.3]]). With $x$–$z$ symmetry, $J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$ ([[SESA3047 4.1 - Inertia Models and Rotor Dynamics|4.1]]):

$$
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}&0&-J_{xz}\\0&J_{yy}&0\\-J_{xz}&0&J_{zz}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiply out $\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$:

$$
\mathbf h=\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}p-J_{xz}r\\J_{yy}q\\-J_{xz}p+J_{zz}r\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ gives Eq. 5.2b in components:

$$
L=J_{xx}\dot p-J_{xz}\dot r+(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr-J_{xz}pq,
$$

$$
M=J_{yy}\dot q+(J_{xx}-J_{zz})pr+J_{xz}(p^2-r^2),
$$

$$
N=-J_{xz}\dot p+J_{zz}\dot r+(J_{yy}-J_{xx})pq+J_{xz}qr.
$$

Substitute $p=\epsilon p_1$, $q=\epsilon q_1$, $r=\epsilon r_1$ and $L=\epsilon L_1$ (trim moments zero). Every gyroscopic term is a product of two rates, so each is $O(\epsilon^2)$, for example $(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr=\epsilon^2(J_{zz}-J_{yy})q_1r_1$. The rate terms are $O(\epsilon)$. Drop $\epsilon^2$ and divide by $\epsilon$:

$$
\boxed{L_1=J_{xx}\dot p_1-J_{xz}\dot r_1,\qquad M_1=J_{yy}\dot q_1,\qquad N_1=-J_{xz}\dot p_1+J_{zz}\dot r_1.}\qquad(5.8)
$$

$M_1$ has a single rate term, and $L_1$ and $N_1$ have two. The $J_{xz}$ coupling between roll and yaw survives because it multiplies a single rate, not a product.

> [!warning] When the moment terms do not all vanish
> The gyroscopic terms disappear only because every angular rate has zero equilibrium value. Two common situations break this. An internal rotor with constant body-frame momentum $\mathbf h_r$ ([[SESA3047 4.1 - Inertia Models and Rotor Dynamics#5. Spinning internal rotors (4.15–4.25)|4.1 §5]]) adds $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_r=\epsilon\,\boldsymbol\omega_1\times\mathbf h_r$, which is first order and stays: a propeller or engine spool couples pitch and yaw in the linear model. And linearising about a steady turn or spin (non-zero trim rates) keeps products like $q_0r_1$. Eq. 5.8 holds for the no-rotation trim of a rigid, symmetric aircraft.

## 5. Assumptions behind Eqs. 5.7–5.8

| Assumption | Where it is used | What breaks without it |
|---|---|---|
| equilibrium $U=U_\infty$, $V=W=p=q=r=0$, trim forces and moments zero | every row | other trims keep extra products |
| $\epsilon\ll1$, first order only | dropping $\epsilon^2$ terms | large disturbances need the nonlinear model |
| constant mass | $\dot{\mathbf p}=m\dot{\mathbf v}$ | a water-bottle rocket (mass not constant) |
| rigid body, constant $\mathbf J$, no rotors | $\dot{\mathbf h}=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$ | rotor gyroscopic terms (warning above) |
| $x$–$z$ symmetry ($J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$) | the form of Eq. 5.8 | pitch couples to roll and yaw |
| flat Earth, axes as in Chapter 4 | Eq. 5.2 | Coriolis and transport terms from [[SESA3047 4.2 - Translational Dynamics\|4.2]] |

Under these assumptions $u,w,q$ appear only in $X_1,Z_1,M_1$, and $v,p,r$ only in $Y_1,L_1,N_1$. This split into longitudinal and lateral sets is the starting point of [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model#1. The longitudinal–lateral split|5.2]].
