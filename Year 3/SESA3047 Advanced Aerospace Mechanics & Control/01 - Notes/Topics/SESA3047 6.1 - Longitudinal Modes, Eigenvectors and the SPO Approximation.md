# SESA3047 6.1 - Longitudinal Modes, Eigenvectors and the SPO Approximation

> [!abstract] Summary
> The longitudinal state matrix $\mathbf A=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf A'$ has two pairs of complex conjugate eigenvalues: a fast, well-damped short-period oscillation (SPO) and a slow, lightly damped phugoid. Each pair $\sigma\pm j\omega$ gives a natural frequency $\omega_n=|\lambda|$, a damping ratio $\zeta=-\sigma/\omega_n$, a half-life $\ln0.5/\sigma$ and a period $2\pi/\omega$. The eigenvectors describe the motion. Their largest components show which states take part in each mode, their phases show how those states move relative to one another, and an initial condition along one eigenvector excites that mode alone. Keeping only $w$ and $q$ gives the $2\times2$ SPO approximation, which matches the F-4C's full-model SPO to within 0.5 %.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** given a longitudinal $\mathbf A$ (or $\mathbf M$ and $\mathbf A'$) and asked for the modes, their frequency, damping, half-life or period; asked which states a mode involves; or asked to derive or apply the SPO approximation.
> - **Assumptions:** linear, time-invariant model about a trim point (level flight, $\gamma_0=0$, for the F-4C); states $[u,w,q,\theta]$ in m/s, m/s, rad/s, rad; half-life only for $\sigma<0$; SPO approximation needs $u,\theta$ slow, $\mathring Z_{\dot w}\ll m$ and $\mathring Z_q\ll mU_\infty$.
> - **Method:** 1. Form $\mathbf A=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf A'$. 2. Eigenvalues: by hand only for $2\times2$, from $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A)\lambda+\det\mathbf A=0$; otherwise `eig`. 3. For each pair $\sigma\pm j\omega$: $\omega_n=|\lambda|$, $\zeta=-\sigma/\omega_n$, $t_{1/2}=\ln0.5/\sigma$, $T=2\pi/\omega$; the fast, better-damped pair is the SPO, the slow one the phugoid. 4. Read the eigenvector: the largest entry (in comparable units) names the active states, the argument of each entry its phase. 5. SPO approximation: $\omega_n^2=(\mathring M_q\mathring Z_w-mU_\infty\mathring M_w)/(mJ_{yy})$, $2\zeta\omega_n=-[m(\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w})+\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}]/(mJ_{yy})$.
> - **Common mistakes:** taking the imaginary part $\omega$ as the natural frequency (it is $\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$); ranking eigenvector entries in different units (m/s against rad) without scaling.
> - **Practise:** [[#Worked example: the F-4C SPO approximation]] · [[#Worked example: the modes of the Eq. 6.4 model]]

A disturbed aircraft does not move at random. Its free motion is a sum of a few fixed patterns, the modes, and each mode has its own frequency and its own rate of decay. Longitudinally there are two. In the short-period oscillation (SPO) the aircraft pitches about its centre of gravity: angle of attack, pitch rate and pitch attitude change together over a few seconds while the airspeed hardly moves, and a restoring moment from the tail brings the nose back with some overshoot. In the phugoid the aircraft follows a gentle wave over a minute or more, trading speed for height at almost constant angle of attack.

Both modes are already in the state matrix of [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model|5.2]]. Each eigenvalue gives how its mode evolves in time, and each eigenvector gives what moves and in what proportion and phase. The free response is a sum of terms $c_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i$ ([[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability|5.4]]), and each term is one mode. The eigenvalue treatment of the SPO and phugoid is in [[SESA2027 A3 - Longitudinal Dynamic Modes - SPO and Phugoid|SESA2027 A3]]. The lateral modes (roll subsidence, spiral and Dutch roll; handout §§6.7–6.10) are not lectured.

## 1. The longitudinal model as a state equation (6.1–6.3)

The perturbation equations of Chapter 5 (Eq. 5.18), with states $\mathbf x=[u,\ w,\ q,\ \theta]^T$, are

$$
\begin{bmatrix}m&0&0&0\\0&m-\mathring Z_{\dot w}&0&0\\0&-\mathring M_{\dot w}&J_{yy}&0\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot u\\\dot w\\\dot q\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring X_u&\mathring X_w&0&-mg\cos\gamma_0\\\mathring Z_u&\mathring Z_w&\mathring Z_q+mU_\infty&-mg\sin\gamma_0\\\mathring M_u&\mathring M_w&\mathring M_q&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}u\\w\\q\\\theta\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(6.1)
$$

Here $u,w$ are perturbations in the body-axis velocity components (FRD axes, so $w$ is positive downwards), $q$ the pitch rate, $\theta$ the pitch attitude in radians, and $\gamma_0$ the trim climb angle. In compact form,

$$
\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A'\mathbf x.\qquad(6.2)
$$

This is not yet state-space form, because $\dot{\mathbf x}$ is multiplied by a matrix. $\mathbf M$ is invertible (its determinant is $m(m-\mathring Z_{\dot w})J_{yy}\neq0$), so multiplying both sides on the left by $\mathbf M^{-1}$ gives

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf A'\mathbf x=\mathbf A\mathbf x,\qquad\mathbf A=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf A'.\qquad(6.3)
$$

The matrix $\mathbf A'$ is the one written $\mathbf A^\dagger$ in Chapter 5. The eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ decide stability; the eigenvectors describe the motion.

## 2. The numerical example and the longitudinal stability quartic (6.4–6.12)

The chapter's example uses a state matrix based on real aircraft data (SI units, angles in radians):

$$
\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0.16&0&-9.81\\-0.125&-0.5&212&0\\0&-0.0082&-0.5&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(6.4)
$$

The $-9.81$ is $-g\cos\gamma_0$ at level trim and the 212 is close to the trim speed in m/s (the $mU_\infty$ term divided by $m$).

### The characteristic polynomial by cofactors

The eigenvalues solve $\det(\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I)=0$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix}-\lambda&0.16&0&-9.81\\-0.125&-0.5-\lambda&212&0\\0&-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda&0\\0&0&1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=0.\qquad(6.5)
$$

Expand along the first column, which has only two non-zero entries. The cofactor signs are $+$ for row 1 and $-$ for row 2:

$$
(-\lambda)\begin{vmatrix}-0.5-\lambda&212&0\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda&0\\0&1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}-(-0.125)\begin{vmatrix}0.16&0&-9.81\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda&0\\0&1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=0.\qquad(6.6)
$$

The first $3\times3$ has only one non-zero entry in its last column, $-\lambda$ in position (3,3) with sign $+$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix}-0.5-\lambda&212&0\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda&0\\0&1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=-\lambda\begin{vmatrix}-0.5-\lambda&212\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda\end{vmatrix}.
$$

So the first term of Eq. 6.6 is

$$
(-\lambda)(-\lambda)\begin{vmatrix}-0.5-\lambda&212\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=\lambda^2\left[(0.5+\lambda)^2+212\times0.0082\right].
$$

Expanding the square and the product,

$$
\lambda^2\left[\lambda^2+\lambda+0.25+1.7384\right]=\lambda^2\left(\lambda^2+\lambda+1.9884\right).
$$

The second $3\times3$ has no convenient column, so expand along its third row, $[0,\ 1,\ -\lambda]$, with signs $-$ for (3,2) and $+$ for (3,3):

$$
\begin{vmatrix}0.16&0&-9.81\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda&0\\0&1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=-1\cdot\begin{vmatrix}0.16&-9.81\\-0.0082&0\end{vmatrix}+(-\lambda)\begin{vmatrix}0.16&0\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda\end{vmatrix}.
$$

The two $2\times2$ determinants are

$$
\begin{vmatrix}0.16&-9.81\\-0.0082&0\end{vmatrix}=0.16\cdot0-(-9.81)(-0.0082)=-0.080442,
$$

$$
\begin{vmatrix}0.16&0\\-0.0082&-0.5-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=0.16(-0.5-\lambda)-0=-0.08-0.16\lambda.
$$

Multiplying by the $+0.125$ in front, the second term of Eq. 6.6 is

$$
-0.125(-0.080442)-0.125\lambda(-0.08-0.16\lambda),\qquad(6.7\text{–}6.8)
$$

which is

$$
0.0100553+0.01\lambda+0.02\lambda^2.
$$

Adding the two terms:

$$
\lambda^4+\lambda^3+1.9884\lambda^2+0.02\lambda^2+0.01\lambda+0.0100553=0,
$$

$$
\boxed{\lambda^4+\lambda^3+2.0084\lambda^2+0.01\lambda+0.0100553=0.}\qquad(6.9)
$$

Rounded to two decimal places it reads $\lambda^4+\lambda^3+2.01\lambda^2+0.01\lambda+0.01=0$. A quartic like this, the characteristic equation of the longitudinal $\mathbf A$, is the longitudinal stability quartic. Doing the cofactor expansion by hand is optional; eigenvalues by hand are examinable for $2\times2$ matrices only, and anything larger is left to MATLAB (L17 ll. 132–142, 339–344). The useful trick for hand expansion is to expand along the row or column with the most zeros.

### Two quadratics, two modes

A longitudinal quartic factors approximately into two quadratics, one per mode:

$$
\underbrace{(\lambda^2+\lambda+2)}_{\text{mode 1}}\underbrace{(\lambda^2+0.0025\lambda+0.005)}_{\text{mode 2}}=0.\qquad(6.10)
$$

Multiplying out gives $\lambda^4+1.0025\lambda^3+2.0075\lambda^2+0.01\lambda+0.01$, close to Eq. 6.9 but not equal; the exact factors are $(\lambda^2+0.9975\lambda+2.0009)(\lambda^2+0.00249\lambda+0.00503)$. The factorisation is approximate because the two modes have very different speeds, so each quadratic is nearly decoupled from the other. Mode 1 by the quadratic formula:

$$
\lambda=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-8}}{2}=\frac{-1\pm j\sqrt7}{2}=-0.5\pm1.323j=\sigma\pm j\omega,\qquad(6.11)
$$

with $j=\sqrt{-1}$. The envelope of a mode is $e^{\sigma t}$, so its amplitude halves when $e^{\sigma t_{1/2}}=0.5$:

$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln0.5}{\sigma}=\frac{-0.6931}{-0.5}=1.39\text{ s},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{2\pi}{1.323}=4.75\text{ s}.
$$

The half-life is positive only for $\sigma<0$; an unstable mode ($\sigma>0$) has a time to double, $\ln2/\sigma$, instead. A period of a few seconds and a decay within a cycle or two make mode 1 the SPO, which is strongly damped. Mode 2:

$$
\lambda=\frac{-0.0025\pm\sqrt{0.0025^2-4\times0.005}}{2}=-0.00125\pm0.0707j,\qquad(6.12)
$$

$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln0.5}{-0.00125}=554.5\text{ s},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{0.0707}=88.9\text{ s}.
$$

A period of a minute and a half and a half-life of nine minutes make mode 2 the phugoid: very lightly damped, long period. MATLAB's `eig` gives the exact values, $-0.4988\pm1.3237j$ and $-0.00125\pm0.0709j$ (L17 ll. 92–96), which change $t_{1/2}$ and $T$ only in the third figure ($1.39$ s, $4.75$ s; $556$ s, $88.6$ s).

### Why the eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs

$\mathbf A$ has real entries. Take the complex conjugate of $\mathbf A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$; since $\bar{\mathbf A}=\mathbf A$,

$$
\mathbf A\bar{\mathbf v}=\bar\lambda\bar{\mathbf v}.
$$

So whenever $\lambda=a+bj$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\mathbf v$, $\bar\lambda=a-bj$ is also one, with eigenvector $\bar{\mathbf v}$. A real matrix therefore has eigenvalues that are real or come in conjugate pairs, and the eigenvectors of a pair are conjugates too (L17 ll. 107–129). A complex eigenvalue without its partner means an arithmetic error. The argument needs $\mathbf A$ real: a matrix with complex entries can have unpaired complex eigenvalues, but every state matrix in this module is real.

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ are the open-loop poles of SESA2027, and the quartic is the denominator of every transfer function built from this $\mathbf A$ (L17 ll. 144–147).

## 3. Damping ratio and natural frequency (6.13–6.16)

Each quadratic in Eq. 6.10 has the form of a mass–spring–damper's characteristic equation, which gives meaning to its coefficients. Start from

$$
m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0,\qquad(6.13)
$$

with mass $m$, damping constant $c$ and stiffness $k$ (all positive). Try the ansatz $x=x_0e^{\lambda t}$. Its derivatives are

$$
\dot x=\lambda x_0e^{\lambda t},\qquad\ddot x=\lambda^2x_0e^{\lambda t}.
$$

Substitute into Eq. 6.13:

$$
\left(m\lambda^2+c\lambda+k\right)x_0e^{\lambda t}=0.
$$

$x_0e^{\lambda t}$ is never zero for a non-trivial motion, so the bracket vanishes. Divide by $m$:

$$
\lambda^2+\frac cm\lambda+\frac km=0.\qquad(6.14)
$$

By the quadratic formula,

$$
\lambda=\frac{-\dfrac cm\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac cm\right)^2-4\dfrac km}}{2}=\sigma\pm j\omega,\qquad(6.15)
$$

where, when $(c/m)^2<4k/m$, the root is complex with

$$
\sigma=-\frac{c}{2m},\qquad\omega=\tfrac12\sqrt{4\frac km-\left(\frac cm\right)^2}.
$$

With no damping ($c=0$) the roots are $\pm j\sqrt{k/m}$, which defines the natural frequency

$$
\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac km}.
$$

The damping ratio $\zeta$, also called the damping factor (§6.3), is defined from the real part:

$$
\zeta=-\frac{\sigma}{\omega_n}=\frac{c/m}{2\omega_n}.
$$

Rearranged, $c/m=2\zeta\omega_n$ and $k/m=\omega_n^2$, so Eq. 6.14 becomes

$$
\boxed{\lambda^2+2\zeta\omega_n\lambda+\omega_n^2=0.}\qquad(6.16)
$$

This is the form to remember (L17 ll. 346–367). Its roots, for $0\le\zeta<1$, are

$$
\lambda=-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2},
$$

and the modulus of either root is

$$
|\lambda|^2=\zeta^2\omega_n^2+\omega_n^2(1-\zeta^2)=\omega_n^2.
$$

So for any complex pair, read straight off the eigenvalue,

$$
\omega_n=|\lambda|=\sqrt{\sigma^2+\omega^2},\qquad\zeta=-\frac{\sigma}{|\lambda|},\qquad\omega=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.
$$

![[amc_ch6_eigenvalue_geometry.png|900]]

In the plane of the eigenvalue (left panel), $\omega_n$ is the distance from the origin and $\zeta$ is the cosine of the angle $\beta$ between that radius and the negative real axis. Eigenvalues on a ray from the origin share a damping ratio; eigenvalues on a circle share a natural frequency.

> [!warning] The imaginary part is not the natural frequency
> $\omega$ is the damped frequency, $\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, and equals $\omega_n$ only when $\zeta=0$. For the F-4C SPO, $\omega=1.356$ rad/s but $\omega_n=1.405$ rad/s (§7). The gap grows with damping: at $\zeta=0.35$ it is 6 %.

The value of $\zeta$ sorts the behaviour (L17 ll. 381–391). With $\zeta>1$ the two roots are real and negative and the motion creeps back without oscillating (over-damped). At $\zeta=1$ the roots coincide (critically damped). For $0<\zeta<1$ the roots are a complex pair and the motion is a decaying oscillation that overshoots a step input (under-damped). At $\zeta=0$ the roots are $\pm j\omega_n$ and the oscillation never decays, and for $\zeta<0$ it grows. Both longitudinal modes of a conventional aircraft are normally under-damped, the SPO much more heavily than the phugoid. The Year 2 treatment, including percentage overshoot, is in [[Damping Ratio and Natural Frequency]].

### Worked example: the modes of the Eq. 6.4 model

From the exact factors, mode 1 is $\lambda^2+0.9975\lambda+2.0009$:

$$
\omega_n=\sqrt{2.0009}=1.4145\text{ rad/s},\qquad\zeta=\frac{0.9975}{2\times1.4145}=0.353.
$$

The same from the eigenvalue: $|{-0.4988}+1.3237j|=\sqrt{0.2488+1.7521}=1.4145$ and $\zeta=0.4988/1.4145=0.353$. With the rounded factor of Eq. 6.10, $\lambda^2+\lambda+2$, $\omega_n=\sqrt2=1.414$ and $\zeta=1/(2\sqrt2)=0.354$.

Mode 2 is $\lambda^2+0.0025\lambda+0.005$:

$$
\omega_n=\sqrt{0.005}=0.0707\text{ rad/s},\qquad\zeta=\frac{0.0025}{2\times0.0707}=0.0177.
$$

The SPO is twenty times faster and twenty times better damped than the phugoid. That separation of time scales is what makes the two-mode factorisation of Eq. 6.10, and the SPO approximation of §7, work.

## 4. Eigenvectors and modes (6.17–6.18)

The eigenvalues say whether the system is stable: one eigenvalue with a positive real part makes the whole system unstable, so every real part must be negative for stability ([[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability#4. Stability from the eigenvalues|5.4 §4]]). They do not say what the aircraft does. A diverging mode could be a climb that runs away or a spiral of growing radius. The eigenvectors answer that. An eigenvector $\mathbf v$ of $\mathbf A$ satisfies $\mathbf A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$: multiplying it by $\mathbf A$ only stretches it. Moving $\lambda\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf I\mathbf v$ to the left,

$$
(\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0.\qquad(6.17)
$$

In MATLAB, `[V,D] = eig(A)` returns both at once: the columns of `V` are the eigenvectors and `D` is diagonal with the eigenvalues on its diagonal, which `diag(D)` extracts (L17 ll. 73–90). This is the $\mathbf V$ and $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ of the decomposition $\mathbf A=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}$ in [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability#2. Why diagonalise (5.43–5.47)|5.4 §2]]. For Eq. 6.4, in the state order $[u,w,q,\theta]$,

$$
\mathbf v_{1,2}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.020\mp0.083j\\0.996\\\pm0.0062j\\0.004\mp0.002j\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf v_{3,4}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-0.03\mp0.003j\\0.0005\\-0.0004\mp0.007j\end{bmatrix},\qquad(6.18)
$$

where the upper signs go with $\lambda_1=-0.4988+1.3237j$ and $\lambda_3=-0.00125+0.0709j$. The $q$ entry of $\mathbf v_{3,4}$ can be checked from the last row: $\theta=q/\lambda$ gives $|q|=0.0709\times0.0073=0.0005$.

Three things can be read from these. First, $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf v_3,\mathbf v_4$ are conjugate pairs, as §2 requires; a purely imaginary entry such as $0.0062j$ also changes sign between the pair. Second, MATLAB scales each eigenvector to unit length,

$$
\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{|v_1|^2+|v_2|^2+|v_3|^2+|v_4|^2}=1,
$$

using the modulus of each complex entry. The 1 in $\mathbf v_{3,4}$ is 0.9995 rounded. Any non-zero complex multiple of an eigenvector is still an eigenvector, so the length and the overall phase are conventions; only the ratios between entries carry information. Third, the largest entry of $\mathbf v_{1,2}$ is $w$ and the largest of $\mathbf v_{3,4}$ is $u$. The pair dominated by $w$ (with $q$) belongs to the SPO and the pair dominated by $u$ to the phugoid, which matches the eigenvalues of §2.

> [!warning] Compare entries in the same units
> $u$ and $w$ are in m/s, $q$ in rad/s and $\theta$ in rad, so "largest entry" is fair only between like quantities. Dividing the velocities by $U_\infty=212$ m/s turns them into angles: $\alpha\approx w/U_\infty$ and $u/U_\infty$. For the SPO eigenvector this gives $\alpha=0.0047$ rad, $\theta=0.0044$ rad, $q=0.0062$ rad/s but $u/U_\infty=0.0004$: angle of attack, attitude and pitch rate all move, and the speed hardly at all. For the phugoid it gives $u/U_\infty=0.0047$ and $\theta=0.0073$ rad but $\alpha=0.00015$ rad and $q=0.0005$ rad/s: speed and attitude change while the angle of attack stays almost fixed.

## 5. Starting the system on an eigenvector (6.19–6.21)

Suppose the initial condition is one of the eigenvectors, $\mathbf x_0=\mathbf v_k$ for some $k=1,\dots,4$. The free response is

$$
\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf v_k,\qquad(6.19)
$$

with the matrix in front of the vector, since $e^{\mathbf At}$ is $n\times n$ and $\mathbf v_k$ is $n\times1$ (L17 ll. 216–219). Expand the matrix exponential as its series:

$$
\mathbf x(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^n}{n!}\mathbf A^n\mathbf v_k.\qquad(6.20)
$$

Powers of $\mathbf A$ acting on an eigenvector reduce to powers of $\lambda_k$. Starting from $\mathbf A\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k\mathbf v_k$ and multiplying by $\mathbf A$ on the left,

$$
\mathbf A^2\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k\mathbf A\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k^2\mathbf v_k,
$$

and once more,

$$
\mathbf A^3\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k^2\mathbf A\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k^3\mathbf v_k,
$$

so by induction $\mathbf A^n\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k^n\mathbf v_k$ (with $\mathbf A^0\mathbf v_k=\mathbf v_k$). Substitute into Eq. 6.20; every term now has the same vector, which comes out of the sum:

$$
\mathbf x(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^n}{n!}\lambda_k^n\mathbf v_k=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda_kt)^n}{n!}\right)\mathbf v_k.
$$

The bracket is the scalar series for $e^{\lambda_kt}$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x(t)=e^{\lambda_kt}\mathbf v_k.}\qquad(6.21)
$$

The state keeps the shape $\mathbf v_k$ for all time and only its size and phase change, as $e^{\lambda_kt}$. No other eigenvalue appears. Started on an SPO eigenvector, the aircraft shows no phugoid at all, and the reverse (L17 ll. 248–258, 335–336). For a general start, $\mathbf x_0=\sum_kc_k\mathbf v_k$ with $\mathbf c=\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0$, and the same argument applied term by term gives $\mathbf x(t)=\sum_kc_ke^{\lambda_kt}\mathbf v_k$: each mode is excited in proportion to how much of its eigenvector the initial condition contains.

A physical initial condition must be real, and $\mathbf v_1$ is complex. Its real part is

$$
\mathcal R(\mathbf v_1)=\tfrac12\left(\mathbf v_1+\bar{\mathbf v}_1\right)=\tfrac12\left(\mathbf v_1+\mathbf v_2\right),
$$

a combination of the two eigenvectors of the same pair. By linearity and Eq. 6.21,

$$
\mathbf x(t)=\tfrac12\left(e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+e^{\bar\lambda_1t}\bar{\mathbf v}_1\right)=\mathcal R\left(e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1\right),
$$

and writing $\lambda_1=\sigma+j\omega$,

$$
\mathbf x(t)=e^{\sigma t}\left[\mathcal R(\mathbf v_1)\cos\omega t-\mathcal I(\mathbf v_1)\sin\omega t\right].
$$

So a real start on $\mathcal R(\mathbf v_1)$ still excites only the SPO, as a real decaying oscillation. The imaginary part of the eigenvector fixes where in its cycle the mode starts.

![[amc_ch6_eigenvector_responses.png|900]]

The figure is Fig. 6.1 recomputed for the Eq. 6.4 model over 100 s. Started on $\mathcal R(\mathbf v_1)$ (top row) only the SPO appears: $w$ starts at 1 m/s and, with $q$ and $\theta$, rings down within about 10 s, while $u$ barely moves. Started on $\mathcal R(\mathbf v_3)$ (middle) only the phugoid appears: $u$ and $\theta$ swing with an 89 s period and hardly decay over 100 s, and $w$ stays small. The start $\mathcal R(\mathbf v_2+\mathbf v_4)=\mathcal R(\mathbf v_1)+\mathcal R(\mathbf v_3)$ (bottom) contains both. The SPO is visible for the first few seconds and then gone, and the phugoid carries on: the long-term behaviour is set by the least stable mode, the one whose eigenvalue has the largest real part, here the phugoid with $\sigma=-0.00125$ s⁻¹.

## 6. Phase relationships in a mode

For an oscillating mode, component $i$ of $\mathcal R(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v)$ is

$$
x_i(t)=|v_i|\,e^{\sigma t}\cos\left(\omega t+\arg v_i\right).
$$

So $|v_i|$ is the relative amplitude of state $i$ and $\arg v_i$ its phase: two states whose entries have the same argument peak together. For the phugoid eigenvector $\mathbf v_3$, scaled so that $u$ is real and positive, the arguments are $0^\circ$ for $u$, $-2^\circ$ for $q$, $-174^\circ$ for $w$ and $-93^\circ$ for $\theta$.

![[amc_ch6_eigenvector_phasors.png|900]]

In the phugoid, $u$ and $q$ are in phase (handout p. 4). The aircraft is fastest when the pitch rate is most positive, at the bottom of the wave where the flight path curves upwards, and slowest when the pitch rate is most negative, at the top where it curves over. $w$ is almost exactly out of phase with both: when the aircraft is fast, $w$ is slightly negative, a small drop in angle of attack ($\alpha\approx w/U_\infty$), and when it is slow the angle of attack rises slightly. The changes are tiny, about $1.5\times10^{-4}$ rad per m/s of $u$.

The pitch attitude lags the pitch rate. The fourth row of $\mathbf A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ is the kinematic equation $\dot\theta=q$, which for the eigenvector reads $q=\lambda\theta$. So

$$
\theta=\frac{q}{\lambda},\qquad\arg\theta=\arg q-\arg\lambda.
$$

For a decaying oscillation in the upper half-plane, $\lambda=-\zeta\omega_n+j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, and from the geometry of §3

$$
\arg\lambda=180^\circ-\arccos\zeta=90^\circ+\arcsin\zeta.
$$

So $\theta$ lags $q$ by $90^\circ+\arcsin\zeta$: $91.0^\circ$ for the phugoid ($\zeta=0.0176$) and $110.6^\circ$ for the SPO ($\zeta=0.353$). A quarter cycle, the $-90^\circ$ phase of the integrator $\Theta/Q=1/s$ on the imaginary axis, is the undamped limit of this lag and a close approximation for the lightly damped phugoid.

The height follows from the flat-Earth kinematics of [[SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames|3.2]]. With wings level, the downward velocity in Earth axes is $-U\sin\theta+W\cos\theta$, and linearised about level trim ($\gamma_0=0$, $U=U_\infty+u$, $W=w$, small $\theta$) the rate of climb is

$$
\dot h=U_\infty\theta-w.
$$

For the phugoid eigenvector $\dot h=\lambda h$, so

$$
h=\frac{U_\infty\theta-w}{\lambda}.
$$

With the entries of $\mathbf v_3$ this is $|h|=21.7$ m per 1 m/s of $u$, at a phase of $177^\circ$ from $u$: the aircraft is highest when slowest and lowest when fastest. The height is set by $\theta$, not by $w$, since $U_\infty\theta$ is about 50 times larger than $w$ here.

> [!check] The phugoid trades kinetic and potential energy
> Per unit mass, the first-order change in kinetic energy is $U_\infty u=212\times1=212$ m²/s², and the change in potential energy is $gh=9.81\times21.7=213$ m²/s², $177^\circ$ out of phase. The two amplitudes agree to 0.6 %, and the $3^\circ$ offset from exact antiphase leaves a residual of about 5 % of either term, so the total energy is nearly constant through the cycle, which is the picture of Fig. 6.3 and the starting assumption of the phugoid approximation (§6.6).

## 7. The SPO approximation (6.22–6.27)

The SPO approximation is the SESA2027 derivation ([[SESA2027 A3 - Longitudinal Dynamic Modes - SPO and Phugoid#5. The SPO approximation (L1.06)|SESA2027 A3 §5]]), set as reading in §6.5 of Chapter 6 (L17 ll. 397–400). The example is the McDonnell Douglas F-4C Phantom II, Table 6.1, in level flight ($\gamma_0=0$) at $U_\infty=178$ m/s and $\rho=0.3809$ kg/m³, with $m=17642$ kg, $J_{yy}=165669$ kg m², $S=49.239$ m² and $c=4.889$ m.

### Dimensional derivatives and the full model

Table 6.1 gives the derivatives in dimensionless form. They are converted as in [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model#6. Dimensional and dimensionless derivatives (appendix, 5.51–5.56)|5.2 §6]]: a force derivative is multiplied by $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2S$, a moment derivative by $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2Sc$, and each is divided by the reference for its variable ($U_\infty$ for $u$ and $w$, $U_\infty/c$ for $q$, $U_\infty^2/c$ for $\dot w$). The resulting factors are

$$
\tfrac12\rho U_\infty S=1669.2\text{ kg/s},\qquad\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc=8160.8\text{ kg m/s},\qquad\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc^2=39898\text{ kg m}^2\text{/s},
$$

$$
\tfrac12\rho Sc=45.85\text{ kg},\qquad\tfrac12\rho Sc^2=224.1\text{ kg m}.
$$

For example,

$$
\mathring X_u=X_u\cdot\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2S\cdot\frac{1}{U_\infty}=-0.0760\times1669.2=-127,
$$

$$
\mathring M_{\dot w}=M_{\dot w}\cdot\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2Sc\cdot\frac{c}{U_\infty^2}=-0.5910\times224.1=-132.
$$

| Derivative | Dimensionless | Factor | Dimensional |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathring X_u$ | $-0.0760$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty S$ | $-127$ kg/s |
| $\mathring X_w$ | $0.0483$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty S$ | $81$ kg/s |
| $\mathring Z_u$ | $-0.7273$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty S$ | $-1214$ kg/s |
| $\mathring Z_w$ | $-3.1245$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty S$ | $-5215$ kg/s |
| $\mathring Z_{\dot w}$ | $-0.3997$ | $\tfrac12\rho Sc$ | $-18.3$ kg |
| $\mathring Z_q$ | $-1.2109$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc$ | $-9882$ kg m/s |
| $\mathring M_u$ | $0.0340$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc$ | $277$ kg m/s |
| $\mathring M_w$ | $-0.2169$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc$ | $-1770$ kg m/s |
| $\mathring M_{\dot w}$ | $-0.5910$ | $\tfrac12\rho Sc^2$ | $-132$ kg m |
| $\mathring M_q$ | $-1.2732$ | $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty Sc^2$ | $-50798$ kg m²/s |

With $mU_\infty=3\,140\,276$ kg m/s, $\mathring Z_q+mU_\infty=3\,130\,394$, and $mg=173\,068$ N ($\cos\gamma_0=1$, $\sin\gamma_0=0$), Eq. 6.1 becomes

$$
\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}17642&0&0&0\\0&17660&0&0\\0&132&165669&0\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf A'=\begin{bmatrix}-127&81&0&-173068\\-1214&-5215&3130394&0\\277&-1770&-50798&0\\0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(6.22)
$$

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf A=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf A'$ are

$$
\lambda_{1,2}=-0.3690\pm1.3558j,\qquad\lambda_{3,4}=-0.0065\pm0.0779j.
$$

For the SPO pair, $\omega_n=\sqrt{0.3690^2+1.3558^2}=1.405$ rad/s and $\zeta=0.3690/1.405=0.2626$. For the phugoid pair, $\omega_n=0.0781$ rad/s and $\zeta=0.0825$. Both are under-damped, the SPO much more heavily. Physically, a pitch disturbance meets a restoring moment from extra tail lift, which corrects it but overshoots, so the attitude oscillates about the undisturbed one (Fig. 6.2).

### Assumptions

Two assumptions reduce the $4\times4$ model to $2\times2$ (handout p. 6):

- $u$ and $\theta$ are dominated by the slow phugoid time scale, so over an SPO cycle their influence on the $w$ and $q$ equations is negligible and they are set to zero (the SPO mode itself still has a $\theta$ component, since $\dot\theta=q$). The $X$ equation and $\dot\theta=q$ are dropped, and so are the $u$ and $\theta$ columns of the remaining rows. Level trim makes the $-mg\sin\gamma_0\,\theta$ term zero in any case.
- $\mathring Z_{\dot w}\ll m$ and $\mathring Z_q\ll mU_\infty$, so both are neglected. For the F-4C, $18.3/17642=0.1\,\%$ and $9882/3\,140\,276=0.3\,\%$.

### Derivation

Rows 2 and 3 of Eq. 6.1 with $u=\theta=0$ and the two small derivatives dropped are

$$
m\dot w=\mathring Z_ww+mU_\infty q,
$$

$$
-\mathring M_{\dot w}\dot w+J_{yy}\dot q=\mathring M_ww+\mathring M_qq,
$$

or in matrix form

$$
\begin{bmatrix}m&0\\-\mathring M_{\dot w}&J_{yy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot w\\\dot q\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring Z_w&mU_\infty\\\mathring M_w&\mathring M_q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w\\q\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(6.23)
$$

Set $w=w_0e^{\lambda t}$ and $q=q_0e^{\lambda t}$, so $\dot w=\lambda w_0e^{\lambda t}$ and $\dot q=\lambda q_0e^{\lambda t}$. Substituting and cancelling $e^{\lambda t}$:

$$
m\lambda w_0=\mathring Z_ww_0+mU_\infty q_0,
$$

$$
-\mathring M_{\dot w}\lambda w_0+J_{yy}\lambda q_0=\mathring M_ww_0+\mathring M_qq_0.
$$

Collect everything on the left:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}m\lambda-\mathring Z_w&-mU_\infty\\-\mathring M_{\dot w}\lambda-\mathring M_w&J_{yy}\lambda-\mathring M_q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_0\\q_0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(6.24)
$$

A non-zero $[w_0,q_0]^T$ exists only if the determinant is zero:

$$
(m\lambda-\mathring Z_w)(J_{yy}\lambda-\mathring M_q)-(-mU_\infty)(-\mathring M_{\dot w}\lambda-\mathring M_w)=0,
$$

$$
(m\lambda-\mathring Z_w)(J_{yy}\lambda-\mathring M_q)-mU_\infty(\mathring M_{\dot w}\lambda+\mathring M_w)=0.\qquad(6.25)
$$

Expand the first product:

$$
mJ_{yy}\lambda^2-m\mathring M_q\lambda-\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}\lambda+\mathring Z_w\mathring M_q-mU_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w}\lambda-mU_\infty\mathring M_w=0.
$$

Group by powers of $\lambda$:

$$
mJ_{yy}\lambda^2-\left[m(\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w})+\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}\right]\lambda+\left(\mathring M_q\mathring Z_w-mU_\infty\mathring M_w\right)=0.\qquad(6.26)
$$

Divide by $mJ_{yy}$ and match term by term with $\lambda^2+2\zeta\omega_n\lambda+\omega_n^2=0$:

$$
\boxed{\omega_n^2=\frac{\mathring M_q\mathring Z_w-mU_\infty\mathring M_w}{mJ_{yy}},\qquad\zeta=\frac{-\left[m(\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w})+\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}\right]}{2\omega_nmJ_{yy}}.}\qquad(6.27)
$$

### Worked example: the F-4C SPO approximation

The leading coefficient is

$$
mJ_{yy}=17642\times165669=2.9227\times10^9.
$$

For the $\lambda$ coefficient, first

$$
\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w}=-50798+178(-132.47)=-50798-23580=-74378,
$$

so

$$
m(\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w})=17642\times(-74378)=-1.3122\times10^9,
$$

$$
\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}=-5215.4\times165669=-8.6404\times10^8,
$$

$$
-\left[m(\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w})+\mathring Z_wJ_{yy}\right]=1.3122\times10^9+0.8640\times10^9=2.1762\times10^9.
$$

For the constant term,

$$
\mathring M_q\mathring Z_w=(-50798)(-5215.4)=2.6493\times10^8,
$$

$$
-mU_\infty\mathring M_w=-3\,140\,276\times(-1770.07)=5.5585\times10^9,
$$

$$
\mathring M_q\mathring Z_w-mU_\infty\mathring M_w=5.8234\times10^9.
$$

Then

$$
\omega_n^2=\frac{5.8234\times10^9}{2.9227\times10^9}=1.9925\ \text{s}^{-2},\qquad\omega_n=1.41\text{ rad/s},
$$

$$
2\zeta\omega_n=\frac{2.1762\times10^9}{2.9227\times10^9}=0.7446\ \text{s}^{-1},\qquad\zeta=\frac{0.7446}{2\times1.4115}=0.264.
$$

The full model gave $\omega_n=1.405$ and $\zeta=0.2626$, so the approximation is 0.45 % high in $\omega_n$ and 0.44 % high in $\zeta$. The roots are $\lambda=-0.372\pm1.362j$ against $-0.369\pm1.356j$ (the crosses and squares on the right of the eigenvalue figure in §3).

The same answer comes from a $2\times2$ state matrix by trace and determinant, which is the by-hand route the exam expects. Inverting the mass matrix of Eq. 6.23,

$$
\mathbf A_{\text{SPO}}=\begin{bmatrix}m&0\\-\mathring M_{\dot w}&J_{yy}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathring Z_w&mU_\infty\\\mathring M_w&\mathring M_q\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\mathring Z_w}{m}&U_\infty\\[2mm]\dfrac{\mathring M_w}{J_{yy}}+\dfrac{\mathring M_{\dot w}\mathring Z_w}{mJ_{yy}}&\dfrac{\mathring M_q+U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w}}{J_{yy}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.2956&178\\-0.01045&-0.4490\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A_{\text{SPO}}=-0.2956-0.4490=-0.7446=-2\zeta\omega_n,
$$

$$
\det\mathbf A_{\text{SPO}}=(-0.2956)(-0.4490)-178(-0.01045)=0.1327+1.8598=1.9925=\omega_n^2.
$$

The terms show where the SPO comes from. The stiffness $\omega_n^2$ is almost all $-U_\infty\mathring M_w/J_{yy}=1.90$ of the 1.99: the static pitch stiffness, $\mathring M_w<0$, set by the static margin. The damping $2\zeta\omega_n=0.745$ splits into $-\mathring M_q/J_{yy}=0.31$ (pitch damping from the tail), $-\mathring Z_w/m=0.30$ (lift-curve slope) and $-U_\infty\mathring M_{\dot w}/J_{yy}=0.14$ (downwash lag at the tail). More on each in [[Short Period Oscillation]].

## 7a. Simulink: feedback around a transfer function

Lab 2 uses Simulink (L17 ll. 407–573; recorded introduction in `Lab 2/Simulink Demo.txt`). A model is built by dragging blocks from the Library Browser and joining output ports to input ports. Sources such as Step and Sine Wave have only outputs, and sinks such as Scope only inputs. The other blocks used are Transfer Fcn, State-Space and PID Controller (in Continuous), and Sum and Gain (in Commonly Used Blocks). Simulink integrates the diagram in time with a variable-step solver, the `ode45` of Lab 1, up to the Stop Time next to Run, which often needs raising.

Three set-up details catch people out. The Step block switches at $t=1$ s by default, not at $t=0$ (L17 ll. 451–453). A Transfer Fcn block takes the numerator and denominator as coefficient vectors in descending powers of $s$, so $1/(s^2+2s+100)$ is `[1]` over `[1 2 100]`. A State-Space block needs consistent dimensions: $\mathbf A$ is $n\times n$, $\mathbf B$ is $n\times m$, $\mathbf C$ is $p\times n$ and $\mathbf D$ is $p\times m$, or Simulink refuses to run.

The plant $1/(s-1)$ has its pole at $s=+1$, in the right half-plane, so its step response grows like $e^t$; $1/(s+1)$ settles at 1. A lightly damped second-order plant with $\omega_n=10$ rad/s, $G(s)=1/(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+100)$, settles at $G(0)=1/100=0.01$ after a step: it does not follow the demand. Closing the loop needs a summing junction (Simulink's Sum block) with signs $+-$, so that it forms the error $e=r-y$; with $++$ the loop is positive feedback.

With a proportional gain $K$ in the forward path, the closed loop is

$$
T(s)=\frac{KG}{1+KG}=\frac{K/(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)}{1+K/(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2)}.
$$

Multiply top and bottom by $s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2$:

$$
T(s)=\frac{K}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2+K}.
$$

Comparing the denominator with $s^2+2\zeta_{cl}\omega_{cl}s+\omega_{cl}^2$,

$$
\omega_{cl}=\sqrt{\omega_n^2+K},\qquad\zeta_{cl}=\frac{\zeta\omega_n}{\sqrt{\omega_n^2+K}},
$$

and for a stable loop the final value of a unit step is $T(0)=K/(\omega_n^2+K)$. A larger $K$ brings the output closer to the demand but raises the frequency and lowers the damping (L17 ll. 532–539). With $\omega_n=10$ rad/s and an assumed $\zeta=0.1$, $K=1$ gives a final value of 0.0099, $K=15$ gives 0.13, and $K=1000$ gives 0.909 at $\zeta_{cl}=0.030$, which rings badly.

![[amc_ch6_feedback_gain_trend.png|900]]

Integral action makes the loop type 1, so the steady-state error to a step goes to zero (for a stable loop) but the response is slow or oscillatory. Derivative action adds damping and removes the oscillation. The initial spike with $K_d=100$ comes from the derivative of the step in the error; Simulink's PID block filters the derivative (filter coefficient $N=100$ by default), which keeps the spike finite. A real aircraft is never asked to follow a step, but the trade-offs are the same ones that appear in the lab and in [[SESA2027 B1 - Control System Fundamentals and PID Control#4. PID control (L2.03)|SESA2027 B1 §4]].
