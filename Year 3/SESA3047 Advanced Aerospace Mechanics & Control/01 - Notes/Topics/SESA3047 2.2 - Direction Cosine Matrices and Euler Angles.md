# SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles

> [!abstract] Summary
> A **direction cosine matrix** (DCM) $\mathbf C_{b/a}$ converts the components of a vector from coordinates $a$ to coordinates $b$: $\mathbf u^b=\mathbf C_{b/a}\mathbf u^a$. The physical vector is unchanged; only its description changes. Each entry is the cosine of the angle between a new axis and an old axis, $C_{ij}=\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j$. A DCM is orthogonal ($\mathbf C^{-1}=\mathbf C^T$), has $\det\mathbf C=+1$, chains by multiplication, and does **not** commute. Any orientation can be built from three successive elementary rotations about coordinate axes; the three angles are **Euler angles**. The aerospace standard is the z–y–x (3-2-1) sequence from NED to body FRD: yaw $\psi$, then pitch $\theta$, then roll $\phi$, giving $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=\mathbf C_x(\phi)\mathbf C_y(\theta)\mathbf C_z(\psi)$. The angles can be read back from the matrix with $\theta=-\arcsin C_{13}$, $\phi=\mathrm{atan2}(C_{23},C_{33})$ and $\psi=\mathrm{atan2}(C_{12},C_{11})$. This fails at $\theta=\pm90^\circ$, where yaw and roll become indistinguishable (**gimbal lock**).

## The big picture

Section 2.1 dealt with how a vector **changes** as seen by different observers. This section deals with how the **same vector at one instant** is described in different coordinate systems.

- Gravity is simple in NED: $[0,0,g]^T$.
- Thrust and aerodynamic forces are simple in body FRD.
- To add them in Newton's law, one of them must be converted. The DCM is the converter.

> [!tip] Intuition: passive rotation
> Nothing physical moves when you apply $\mathbf C_{b/a}$. You keep the arrow and swap the ruler: the same arrow is measured along a new, rotated set of axes. The notes put it as a right-hand rotation of the **coordinate system**, or equivalently a left-hand rotation of the **vector** relative to fixed axes.

## 1. What a DCM is

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u^b=\mathbf C_{b/a}\,\mathbf u^a}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.28})
$$

$\mathbf C_{b/a}$ maps components from coordinate system $a$ to coordinate system $b$. Read the subscript as "$b$ from $a$". The subscripts chain like fractions: $\mathbf C_{c/b}\mathbf C_{b/a}=\mathbf C_{c/a}$.

### Why "direction cosines" (derivation)

Let $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$ and $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\mathbf b_3$ be the unit axes of the two systems. The $i$-th component of $\mathbf u$ in $b$ is its projection on $\mathbf b_i$:

$$
u^b_i=\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf u=\mathbf b_i\cdot\Big(\sum_j u^a_j\,\mathbf a_j\Big)=\sum_j(\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j)\,u^a_j .
$$

Comparing with $u^b_i=\sum_jC_{ij}u^a_j$:

$$
\boxed{C_{ij}=\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf a_j=\cos\big(\angle\,\mathbf b_i,\mathbf a_j\big)}
$$

Every entry is the cosine of the angle between a new axis and an old axis, hence the name.

- **Row $i$** of $\mathbf C_{b/a}$ is the new axis $\mathbf b_i$ written in old ($a$) components.
- **Column $j$** is the old axis $\mathbf a_j$ written in new ($b$) components.

This row reading is the fastest way to draw or check any DCM. The figures in this note are generated exactly that way.

## 2. The elementary rotation about z (derivation)

Rotate system $B$ by $+\theta$ about the common $z$-axis, right-handed (Fig. 2.5). Project $\mathbf u$ on the new axes.

![[amc_dcm_derivation.png|900]]

**Onto $x_B$** (panel a). $\mathbf x_B=\cos\theta\,\mathbf x_A+\sin\theta\,\mathbf y_A$, so

$$
u^B_x=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf x_B=u^A_x\cos\theta+u^A_y\sin\theta .
$$

**Onto $y_B$** (panel b). $\mathbf y_B=-\sin\theta\,\mathbf x_A+\cos\theta\,\mathbf y_A$, which has rotated past the $y_A$ axis towards $-x_A$. So

$$
u^B_y=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf y_B=-u^A_x\sin\theta+u^A_y\cos\theta .
$$

**Along $z$**, nothing changes: $u^B_z=u^A_z$. Collecting,

$$
\boxed{\mathbf C_z(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.29})
$$

*Direction-cosine check:* $C_{11}=\cos\theta$ (angle $\theta$ between $x_B$ and $x_A$); $C_{12}=\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta$; $C_{21}=\cos(90^\circ+\theta)=-\sin\theta$; $C_{22}=\cos\theta$ ✓.

> [!warning] Transcript slips (L5, ll. 373–381)
> - l. 376 reads the matrix as *"cos theta, sin theta, sin theta, cos theta"*. The $(2,1)$ entry is **$-\sin\theta$**. Without the minus sign the matrix is not orthogonal: its rows $[\cos\theta,\sin\theta]$ and $[\sin\theta,\cos\theta]$ have dot product $2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta\neq0$.
> - l. 380 says *"I maintain x in the same position"*. For $\mathbf C_z$ it is **$z$** that is kept (l. 373 says so correctly: *"a Z-axis that is the same, shared between the two reference frames"*). That is where the $0,0,1$ row and column come from.

The lecturer's framing (L5, ll. 357–370): one vector, two coordinate systems rotated by $\theta$. *"The vector, which is our physical quantity, does not change… what is changing is our perspective."*

## 3. Properties of a DCM (§2.3.2), with the reasons

### Orthogonality (Eq. 2.30)

$$
\mathbf C_{b/a}^T\mathbf C_{b/a}=\mathbf I,\qquad\mathbf C_{b/a}^{-1}=\mathbf C_{b/a}^T .
$$

*Why:* the rows of $\mathbf C$ are the unit vectors $\mathbf b_i$ in $a$-components, and they are mutually perpendicular. Entry $(i,k)$ of $\mathbf C\mathbf C^T$ is (row $i$)·(row $k$) $=\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_k=\delta_{ik}$, so $\mathbf C\mathbf C^T=\mathbf I$. For a square matrix this also gives $\mathbf C^T\mathbf C=\mathbf I$.

*Consequence:* lengths are preserved, $|\mathbf u^b|^2=\mathbf u^{aT}\mathbf C^T\mathbf C\,\mathbf u^a=|\mathbf u^a|^2$. The inverse costs nothing: reverse a transformation by transposing.

### Composition (Eq. 2.31)

$$
\mathbf C_{d/a}=\mathbf C_{d/c}\,\mathbf C_{c/b}\,\mathbf C_{b/a}.
$$

*Why:* $\mathbf u^d=\mathbf C_{d/c}\mathbf u^c=\mathbf C_{d/c}(\mathbf C_{c/b}\mathbf u^b)=\mathbf C_{d/c}\mathbf C_{c/b}\mathbf C_{b/a}\mathbf u^a$. The **rightmost** matrix acts first.

The lecture singles out composition and non-commutativity as *"the very important"* properties (L5, ll. 397–398). Composition is what makes attitude representation possible at all: *"a series of elementary rotation can bring us in a general rotation"* (l. 407), so any orientation can be built from rotations about $x$, $y$ and $z$ alone. *"This is very important for determining the aircraft attitude"* (l. 409).

### Unit determinant (Eq. 2.32)

$$
\det\mathbf C_{b/a}=+1 .
$$

*Why:* orthogonality gives $\det(\mathbf C^T\mathbf C)=(\det\mathbf C)^2=1$, so $\det\mathbf C=\pm1$. The determinant of the matrix whose rows are $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\mathbf b_3$ is the scalar triple product $\mathbf b_1\cdot(\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3)$. That is $+1$ for a right-handed set, because $\mathbf b_2\times\mathbf b_3=\mathbf b_1$. A value of $-1$ would mean a reflection (a mirror-image, left-handed frame), which no physical rotation can produce.

### Non-commutativity (Eq. 2.33)

$$
\mathbf C_{c/b}\mathbf C_{b/a}\neq\mathbf C_{b/a}\mathbf C_{c/b}\quad\text{in general.}
$$

The notes' example (Fig. 2.6) rotates by $\alpha$ about $z_A$ and then by $\beta$ about the **new** $y_B$, $\mathbf u^c=\mathbf C_{c/b}(\beta)\mathbf C_{b/a}(\alpha)\mathbf u^a$ (Eq. 2.34), and compares it with the reverse order. With $\alpha=\beta=90^\circ$ the result is easy to check by hand:

$$
\mathbf C_y(90^\circ)\mathbf C_z(90^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\-1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}
\ \neq\
\mathbf C_z(90^\circ)\mathbf C_y(90^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Read the rows. Order 1 ends with $x_C=-z_A$, $y_C=-x_A$, $z_C=y_A$. Order 2 ends with $x_C=y_A$, $y_C=z_A$, $z_C=x_A$. The second rotation acts about an axis that the first has **already moved**.

![[amc_rotation_order.png|900]]

The lecture demonstrates exactly this on the board (L6, ll. 1–64): $\alpha$ about $z$ (red frame $B$), then $\beta$ about the **new** $y_B$ to give $C$; then the same two angles in the opposite order. *"If we apply the same rotation but in a different sequence we obtain a different… final orientation"* (ll. 59–62). The lecturer admits the perspective sketch *"is not the best"* (l. 57); the figure above, and the numbers below, are the reliable version.

> [!example] Numbers make the difference visible (suggested)
> Take $\alpha=40^\circ$ about $z$ and $\beta=30^\circ$ about $y$. Where does the new $x$-axis (row 1) point, in the original axes?
> - $z$ then $y$: $\mathbf C_y(30^\circ)\mathbf C_z(40^\circ)$, row 1 $=[\cos30^\circ\cos40^\circ,\ \cos30^\circ\sin40^\circ,\ -\sin30^\circ]=[0.663,\ 0.557,\ -0.500]$.
> - $y$ then $z$: $\mathbf C_z(40^\circ)\mathbf C_y(30^\circ)$, row 1 $=[\cos40^\circ\cos30^\circ,\ \sin40^\circ,\ -\cos40^\circ\sin30^\circ]=[0.663,\ 0.643,\ -0.383]$.
>
> Same two angles, but the nose ends up $8.3^\circ$ apart ($\arccos$ of the dot product of the two rows, $0.9895$). Both rows have unit length ✓.

> [!tip] Try it with a book
> Hold a book flat. Turn it 90° about the vertical, then 90° about its own spine. Start again and do the two turns in the opposite order. It ends in a different position. Finite rotations are not vectors: they cannot be added in any order.

## 4. The three elementary rotations

For right-handed rotations about $x$, $y$ and $z$ (Eqs. 2.35–2.37):

$$
\mathbf C_x(\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\phi&\sin\phi\\0&-\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf C_y(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&0&-\sin\theta\\0&1&0\\\sin\theta&0&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf C_z(\psi)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi&\sin\psi&0\\-\sin\psi&\cos\psi&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The axis being rotated about keeps a 1 on the diagonal and zeros in its row and column. The other four entries are cosines and sines.

> [!important] Where does the minus sign go? (the $\mathbf C_y$ trap)
> For a rotation about axis $k$, take the other two axes in **cyclic** order $(i,j)$, so that $i\to j\to k$ runs $x\to y\to z\to x$. Then
> $$C_{ij}=+\sin,\qquad C_{ji}=-\sin,\qquad C_{ii}=C_{jj}=\cos,\qquad C_{kk}=1 .$$
> - about $x$: $(i,j)=(y,z)$, so $+\sin$ at $(2,3)$;
> - about $z$: $(i,j)=(x,y)$, so $+\sin$ at $(1,2)$;
> - about $y$: $(i,j)=(z,x)$, so $+\sin$ at $(3,1)$ and $-\sin$ at $(1,3)$.
>
> $\mathbf C_y$ only *looks* transposed compared with the other two. It follows the same rule, because the cyclic successor of $z$ is $x$, not the other way round (panel d of the DCM figure).

## 5. Euler angles (§2.4)

An orientation can be described by three successive rotations about coordinate axes; the three angles are **Euler angles**.

- **How many sequences?** Each of the three rotations can be about $x$, $y$ or $z$, but two consecutive rotations about the same axis would merge into one. That leaves $3\times2\times2=12$ sequences. Six use three different axes (Tait–Bryan: xyz, xzy, yxz, yzx, zxy, zyx). Six repeat the first axis at the end (proper Euler: xyx, xzx, yxy, yzy, zxz, zyz). This is the notes' "twelve possible combinations (six without repetition of one axis)".
- Because rotations do not commute, Euler angles mean something **only together with their sequence**.
- For a given $\mathbf C$, the angles cannot be recovered unless the sequence is specified.

This is the point the lecture makes twice over (L6, ll. 80–89 and 118–131): *"if I specify for example alpha and beta only and I don't tell you… about which axis and in which sequence to apply it, you can end up in a very different configuration."* Given the angles without the sequence, you cannot build the matrix; given the matrix without the sequence, you cannot extract the angles.

> [!warning] Euler angles are not a vector
> $[\phi\ \theta\ \psi]^T$ cannot be added or reordered like the components of a vector (§2.4.1). **Only infinitesimal rotations behave like vectors** (derivation in §9 below). This is why angular velocity *is* a vector, while the Euler angles themselves are not.

## 6. The aerospace z–y–x (3-2-1) sequence (§2.4.2)

Starting from local NED (Chapter 1) and ending in body FRD:

1. right-handed rotation about $z$ (= Down) by the **yaw** $\psi$, giving intermediate axes $(x_1,y_1,z_1)$;
2. about the **new** $y_1$ by the **pitch** $\theta$, giving $(x_2,y_2,z_2)$;
3. about the **new** $x_2$ by the **roll** $\phi$, giving body $(x_b,y_b,z_b)$ = FRD.

$$
\boxed{\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=\mathbf C_x(\phi)\,\mathbf C_y(\theta)\,\mathbf C_z(\psi)}\qquad(\text{Eq. 2.39})
$$

The **rightmost** matrix (yaw) acts first and roll acts last.

> [!tip] Why the order is written backwards (L6, ll. 144–150)
> The lecturer: *"you write the matrices in the opposite order, it's a little bit confusing"*. Read the product as acting on a vector, $\mathbf C_x(\mathbf C_y(\mathbf C_z\,\mathbf u^{NED}))$. The matrix nearest the vector, $\mathbf C_z$, multiplies it first. So "yaw, then pitch, then roll" is written roll · pitch · yaw. Matrix multiplication is associative, so you may multiply $\mathbf C_y\mathbf C_z$ first, as the lecture suggests (l. 176) and as done below, or $\mathbf C_x\mathbf C_y$ first. Only the left-to-right **order** is fixed.

![[amc_euler_321_sequence.png|900]]

### Multiplying it out (the step the notes compress into one line)

Write $c_\psi=\cos\psi$, $s_\psi=\sin\psi$ and so on. (The board uses capital $C$ and $S$ for the same contraction; a student asked, L6 ll. 170–175. Do not confuse $C\theta=\cos\theta$ with the matrix $\mathbf C$.)

**Step 1: pitch after yaw.** $\mathbf C_y(\theta)\mathbf C_z(\psi)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}c_\theta&0&-s_\theta\\0&1&0\\s_\theta&0&c_\theta\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}c_\psi&s_\psi&0\\-s_\psi&c_\psi&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}c_\theta c_\psi&c_\theta s_\psi&-s_\theta\\-s_\psi&c_\psi&0\\s_\theta c_\psi&s_\theta s_\psi&c_\theta\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row 1, for example, is $[c_\theta,0,-s_\theta]$ times each column: $c_\theta c_\psi+0+0$, $c_\theta s_\psi$, $-s_\theta$. The rows are the intermediate axes $x_2,y_2,z_2$ in NED.

**Step 2: roll.** $\mathbf C_x(\phi)$ leaves row 1 alone and mixes rows 2 and 3:

$$
\text{row}_2\leftarrow c_\phi\,\text{row}_2+s_\phi\,\text{row}_3,\qquad
\text{row}_3\leftarrow -s_\phi\,\text{row}_2+c_\phi\,\text{row}_3 .
$$

For example, the new $(2,1)$ entry is $c_\phi(-s_\psi)+s_\phi(s_\theta c_\psi)$. The result is Eq. 2.38:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_\theta c_\psi & c_\theta s_\psi & -s_\theta\\
-c_\phi s_\psi+s_\phi s_\theta c_\psi & c_\phi c_\psi+s_\phi s_\theta s_\psi & s_\phi c_\theta\\
s_\phi s_\psi+c_\phi s_\theta c_\psi & -s_\phi c_\psi+c_\phi s_\theta s_\psi & c_\phi c_\theta
\end{bmatrix}}
$$

> [!check] Sanity checks you can do in an exam
> - $\phi=\theta=0$: reduces to $\mathbf C_z(\psi)$ ✓.
> - Row 1 is the nose direction in NED: $[c_\theta c_\psi,\ c_\theta s_\psi,\ -s_\theta]$. The nose points along heading $\psi$ and upward at $\theta$ (hence $-s_\theta$ in "Down") ✓.
> - Each row and each column has unit length. For example, row 1: $c_\theta^2(c_\psi^2+s_\psi^2)+s_\theta^2=1$ ✓.

### The two directions of transformation (Eqs. 2.40–2.41)

$$
\mathbf u^{FRD}=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}\,\mathbf u^{NED},\qquad
\mathbf u^{NED}=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}^T\,\mathbf u^{FRD}.
$$

### Gravity in body axes (derivation)

Gravity is $[0,0,g]^T$ in NED, so in FRD it is $g$ times the **third column** of $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$:

$$
[\mathbf g]^{FRD}=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\g\end{bmatrix}=g\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\\sin\phi\cos\theta\\\cos\phi\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Yaw drops out, as it should: turning to a new heading cannot change how gravity looks to the pilot. This is exactly the gravity vector of [[SESA3047 1.2 - Dynamic Models, Frames and Earth Models#12. NED and FRD coordinates|Chapter 1]] and of SESA2027.

### Physical interpretation (§2.4.3)

| Angle | Rotation axis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Yaw $\psi$ | Down ($z$) | heading in the local horizontal plane |
| Pitch $\theta$ | Right ($y_1$, after yaw) | nose up or down relative to the horizontal |
| Roll $\phi$ | Forward ($x_2$, after yaw and pitch) | rotation about the longitudinal axis |

Pitch is applied to axes that yaw has already turned, and roll to axes that both have turned. That is why the sequence must always be stated.

## 7. Extracting Euler angles from a DCM (§2.4.4)

A flight computer propagates $\mathbf C$ (or a quaternion) and must display $\psi,\theta,\phi$ to the pilot. Read the boxed matrix:

- $C_{13}=-s_\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\theta=-\arcsin C_{13}}$ (2.43)
- $\dfrac{C_{23}}{C_{33}}=\dfrac{s_\phi c_\theta}{c_\phi c_\theta}=\tan\phi\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\phi=\mathrm{atan2}(C_{23},C_{33})}$ (2.42)
- $\dfrac{C_{12}}{C_{11}}=\dfrac{c_\theta s_\psi}{c_\theta c_\psi}=\tan\psi\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\psi=\mathrm{atan2}(C_{12},C_{11})}$ (2.44)

Principal ranges (Eq. 2.45): $-\pi<\phi\le\pi$, $-\tfrac\pi2\le\theta\le\tfrac\pi2$, $-\pi<\psi\le\pi$.

> [!warning] Transcript slips in the extraction formulas (L6, ll. 199–208)
> - l. 205: *"the pitch can be obtained with arc sign, C 1, 3"*. It is $\theta=\mathbf{-}\arcsin C_{13}$, because $C_{13}=-\sin\theta$. Dropping the minus sign reports a nose-up attitude as nose-down.
> - l. 208: *"C 1, 2, C 1, 2"*. The yaw formula is $\psi=\mathrm{atan2}(C_{12},C_{11})$: the second argument is $C_{11}$.
> - l. 199 (roll from *"the components 2, 3"* and $3,3$) is correct.
>
> Check any version of these formulas with the round trip below before trusting it.

### Why atan2, not arctan (the L6 question)

A student asked what atan2 means (L6, l. 229). The answer in the recording (ll. 230–250) is hesitant: *"I don't remember exactly which is which… it starts with zero degrees at a different point"*, and agrees with *"like the tangent function but shifted"* (ll. 247–248). Here is the precise version.

![[amc_atan2_quadrants.png|900]]

- $\arctan(r)$ takes **one** number, the ratio $r=y/x$, and returns an angle in $(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$. The ratio has lost the individual signs: $(+1)/(+\sqrt3)$ and $(-1)/(-\sqrt3)$ are both $1/\sqrt3$.
- $\mathrm{atan2}(y,x)$ takes **two** numbers, keeps both signs, and returns the angle of the point $(x,y)$ in $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$ (panel c). That is what the lecturer meant by *"you have two elements in atan2… in the other one… just one"* (l. 250).

For roll, $C_{23}=\sin\phi\cos\theta$ and $C_{33}=\cos\phi\cos\theta$. Inside the principal range $\cos\theta>0$, so the signs of $C_{23}$ and $C_{33}$ are the signs of $\sin\phi$ and $\cos\phi$. atan2 then returns the true $\phi$ in all four quadrants. arctan would map an inverted aircraft at $\phi=-150^\circ$ to $+30^\circ$: upright (panel a). The same argument applies to yaw with $C_{12}$ and $C_{11}$. Pitch needs no quadrant information, because $\theta$ is restricted to $[-90^\circ,90^\circ]$, exactly the range of $\arcsin$.

> [!example] Two attitudes that arctan cannot tell apart (verified)
> Level pitch, $\psi=0$:
> - $\phi=30^\circ$: $C_{23}=0.5$, $C_{33}=0.866$. $\arctan(0.5/0.866)=30^\circ$ ✓; $\mathrm{atan2}(0.5,0.866)=30^\circ$ ✓.
> - $\phi=-150^\circ$: $C_{23}=-0.5$, $C_{33}=-0.866$. $\arctan(0.577)=30^\circ$ ✗; $\mathrm{atan2}(-0.5,-0.866)=-150^\circ$ ✓.

### Worked round trip (suggested)

$\psi=-30^\circ$, $\theta=12^\circ$, $\phi=15^\circ$ (the attitude drawn in Chapter 1):

$$
\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=\begin{bmatrix}0.8471&-0.4891&-0.2079\\0.5296&0.8096&0.2532\\0.0445&-0.3246&0.9448\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Back out the angles:

- $\theta=-\arcsin(-0.2079)=12.0^\circ$;
- $\phi=\mathrm{atan2}(0.2532,0.9448)=15.0^\circ$;
- $\psi=\mathrm{atan2}(-0.4891,0.8471)=-30.0^\circ$ ✓.

Also $\mathbf C\mathbf C^T=\mathbf I$ and $\det\mathbf C=1$ to four decimal places. Gravity in body axes is the last column, $g[-0.208,\ 0.253,\ 0.945]^T$.

## 8. The singularity: gimbal lock (§2.4.5)

At $\theta=\pm90^\circ$, $c_\theta=0$, so $C_{11}=C_{12}=C_{23}=C_{33}=0$. Both atan2 extractions become $\mathrm{atan2}(0,0)$, which is undefined.

> [!warning] It fails silently (L6, l. 213)
> The lecturer: *"if you try to apply this function of 0 and 0, you will get 0, which is not the correct result."* That is literally true of the software: Python's `math.atan2(0, 0)`, NumPy and MATLAB all return `0` with **no error**. A flight computer would display $\phi=\psi=0$ for an aircraft that may in fact be rolled. In real arithmetic the entries are not exactly zero near $90^\circ$ but tiny and noisy, so $\phi$ and $\psi$ jump about wildly instead. Either way the output cannot be trusted.

The lecture's framing of the range (L6, ll. 215–222): the method works everywhere except **at** $\theta=\pm90^\circ$ itself (*"at 91 it works again"*, l. 221, meaning the attitude is representable again once you leave the singular point, with $\theta$ folded back into the principal range and $\phi,\psi$ shifted by $180^\circ$).

![[amc_gimbal_lock.png|900]]

**Why only a combination survives (derivation).** Put $s_\theta=1$, $c_\theta=0$ in the boxed matrix and use the compound-angle identities:

$$
\mathbf C\big|_{\theta=90^\circ}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\-c_\phi s_\psi+s_\phi c_\psi&c_\phi c_\psi+s_\phi s_\psi&0\\s_\phi s_\psi+c_\phi c_\psi&-s_\phi c_\psi+c_\phi s_\psi&0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\\sin(\phi-\psi)&\cos(\phi-\psi)&0\\\cos(\phi-\psi)&-\sin(\phi-\psi)&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Every entry depends only on $\phi-\psi$. For example, $(\phi,\psi)=(30^\circ,0^\circ)$ and $(60^\circ,30^\circ)$ give **identical** matrices. At $\theta=-90^\circ$ the same algebra gives dependence on $\phi+\psi$.

**Physically:** with the nose vertical, the body $x$-axis lies along the local vertical. Yaw (about Down) and roll (about the body $x$-axis) turn the aircraft about the **same line**, so the three angles no longer describe three independent rotations. One degree of freedom is lost.

For aerobatic aircraft, missiles and spacecraft that may pass through vertical, use a non-singular representation such as **quaternions**. For conventional aircraft, pitch attitudes near $\pm90^\circ$ are rare, so Euler angles remain practical (the notes' conclusion, repeated in L6, ll. 224–227: *"for most of the applications in aerospace engineering, it's not a problem… when it's a problem… quaternions"*).

## 9. Small rotations do behave like a vector (derivation)

*(Extension, supporting the §2.4.1 remark that "only infinitesimal rotation increments may be treated as vectors".)*

For small angles, $\cos\epsilon\approx1$ and $\sin\epsilon\approx\epsilon$. Then

$$
\mathbf C_x(\delta\phi)\approx\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&\delta\phi\\0&-\delta\phi&1\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf I-\delta\phi\,\tilde{\mathbf e}_x ,
$$

and similarly for $y$ and $z$. The product of three such matrices, **in either order**, is

$$
\mathbf C\approx\mathbf I-\big(\delta\phi\,\tilde{\mathbf e}_x+\delta\theta\,\tilde{\mathbf e}_y+\delta\psi\,\tilde{\mathbf e}_z\big)=\mathbf I-\widetilde{\delta\boldsymbol\Theta},\qquad\delta\boldsymbol\Theta=[\delta\phi,\ \delta\theta,\ \delta\psi]^T,
$$

because the cross terms are second order. So small rotations add and commute like the components of a vector, and they appear through the [[Cross-Product Matrix]] of 2.1. *Numerical check:* with $(\delta\phi,\delta\theta,\delta\psi)=(0.02,0.03,-0.01)$ rad, the exact product differs from $\mathbf I-\widetilde{\delta\boldsymbol\Theta}$ by at most $6\times10^{-4}$, the size of the products of the angles. Reversing the order changes it by the same tiny amount.

## 10. Worked example: level aircraft with a yaw of 30° (§2.4.6)

$\phi=0$, $\theta=0$, $\psi=30^\circ$ (Eq. 2.46). With $\mathbf C_x(0)=\mathbf C_y(0)=\mathbf I$,

$$
\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=\mathbf C_z(30^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos30^\circ&\sin30^\circ&0\\-\sin30^\circ&\cos30^\circ&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3/2&1/2&0\\-1/2&\sqrt3/2&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\qquad(2.47)
$$

A unit vector pointing north, $\mathbf u^{NED}=[1,0,0]^T$ (2.48), has body components equal to the first column:

$$
\mathbf u^{FRD}=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}\mathbf u^{NED}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos30^\circ\\-\sin30^\circ\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.866\\-0.500\\0\end{bmatrix}\qquad(2.49)
$$

![[amc_yaw30_example.png|900]]

- $+0.866$ forward: north lies partly **ahead** of the aircraft.
- $-0.500$ right: north lies to the **left** of the body axis. The aircraft heads $30^\circ$ east of north, so north is off the left wing.
- $0$ down: both lie in the horizontal plane.

The physical vector has not changed; only its description has (the notes' key idea).

### A second example: why components matter (suggested)

Same attitude as §7, $(\psi,\theta,\phi)=(-30^\circ,12^\circ,15^\circ)$, with the aircraft moving horizontally along its heading at 200 m/s: $\mathbf v^{NED}=200[\cos(-30^\circ),\ \sin(-30^\circ),\ 0]^T$. Then

$$
\mathbf v^{FRD}=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}\mathbf v^{NED}=[195.6,\ 10.8,\ 40.2]^T\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$

The large body-$z$ component means the air meets the wing from below. The angle of attack is $\alpha\approx\arctan(40.2/195.6)=11.6^\circ$, close to the pitch angle, as expected for level flight. The sideslip is $\beta\approx\arcsin(10.8/200)\approx3.1^\circ$, caused by the bank. Aerodynamic angles are **body-axis** quantities, so this conversion is needed every time.

## 11. Preview: how the DCM evolves in time

*(Extension joining the two halves of Chapter 2; the equations of motion come later.)*

Take a vector fixed in $F_a$, so ${}^a\dot{\mathbf u}=\mathbf 0$. The transport theorem gives ${}^b\dot{\mathbf u}=-\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf u$. In $b$-components, $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mathbf u^b=-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^b\mathbf u^b$. But $\mathbf u^b=\mathbf C_{b/a}\mathbf u^a$ with $\mathbf u^a$ constant, so $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mathbf u^b=\dot{\mathbf C}_{b/a}\mathbf u^a$. This holds for every $\mathbf u^a$, hence

$$
\dot{\mathbf C}_{b/a}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^b_{b/a}\,\mathbf C_{b/a}.
$$

Integrating this with the body rates from gyros is how an inertial navigation system tracks attitude. Section 7 then converts the result to Euler angles for display.

## 12. Problem-solving workflow

1. Name the two coordinate systems and the direction: **$b$ from $a$**, $\mathbf u^b=\mathbf C_{b/a}\mathbf u^a$.
2. List the elementary rotations **in the order they are applied**. Write the product with the **first rotation on the right**.
3. Build each elementary matrix with the cyclic rule, and watch the $\mathbf C_y$ sign.
4. Multiply two at a time. Check that each row has unit length.
5. For the reverse direction, **transpose**; never invert numerically.
6. To extract angles, use $\theta=-\arcsin C_{13}$ and atan2 for $\phi,\psi$. Check that $|\theta|$ is not near $90^\circ$.
7. Sanity-check with a special case (all angles zero, or only one non-zero).
