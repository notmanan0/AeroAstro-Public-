# SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams

> [!abstract] Summary
> Any linear model can be put in state-space form, whatever it starts as. A second-order ODE gets two states, the variable and its rate; the satellite-and-instrument example (used in Lab 2) becomes a $4\times4$ model with one torque input and one angle sensor. A transfer function is converted by cross-multiplying: the denominator becomes a chain of integrators that fixes $\mathbf A$, the input enters through $\mathbf B$, and the numerator fixes $\mathbf C$. Only the denominator reaches $\mathbf A$, so the poles, and with them stability, live in $\mathbf A$. A block diagram is converted block by block, with each block's input rewritten in terms of states and reference inputs (Chapter 5 only; not examinable).

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** given a set of linear ODEs (possibly second order), a transfer function, or a block diagram, and asked for $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$, $\mathbf C$.
> - **Assumptions:** linear time-invariant; transfer functions strictly proper (numerator degree below denominator degree, so $\mathbf D=0$); zero initial conditions when moving between $s$ and $t$.
> - **Method:** 1. For an $n$th-order ODE, take the variable and its first $n-1$ derivatives as states (as many states as the highest derivative order). 2. For $G=N(s)/D(s)$, introduce $V$ with $D(s)V=R$ and $W=N(s)V$; cross-multiply and inverse Laplace transform. 3. Write $\dot x_i$ for each state; the last row comes from the ODE, and the input goes in $\mathbf B$, not $\mathbf A$. 4. The numerator gives $\mathbf C$. 5. Read off $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$, $\mathbf C$ and check the dimensions $n\times n$, $n\times m$, $p\times n$.
> - **Common mistakes:** forgetting to divide the input torque by $J_1$ (Eq. 5.21d has this slip); putting the input term inside $\mathbf A\mathbf x$ instead of $\mathbf B\mathbf u$.
> - **Practise:** [[#1. Second-order equations as first-order states (5.19–5.24)]] · [[SESA3047 Chapter 5 Practice Problems - Solutions#Problem 2. Transfer functions to state space|Practice problem 2]]

The aircraft model of [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model|5.2]] contains first derivatives only. Most models do not. A spring–mass system has $\ddot x$, a transfer function from the Year 2 control module has powers of $s$, and a controller drawn as a block diagram has signals looping between blocks. Each of them can be rewritten as $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$.

## 1. Second-order equations as first-order states (5.19–5.24)

### The system

A satellite carries an instrument package on a flexible tether. The satellite's attitude relative to a star is $\theta_1$, the instrument's $\theta_2$. Cold-gas jets on the satellite apply the torque $T_c$; a star tracker measures $\theta_2$. The two bodies are modelled as discs joined by a torsional spring $k$ and damper $b$: a mass–spring–damper in rotation, so second order. The aim is to steer the instrument package, but the torque can only be applied to the satellite. Deriving Eq. 5.19 is not expected: dynamics derivations in this module are limited to mass–spring–damper-type systems where the assumptions are given, and the conversion procedure is what is examined.

Where the equations come from, briefly: the spring twists by $\theta_1-\theta_2$ and the damper by $\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2$, and each body feels equal and opposite reaction torques. Newton's second law for rotation on each disc gives

$$
J_1\ddot\theta_1+b(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)+k(\theta_1-\theta_2)=T_c,\qquad(5.19\text{a})
$$

$$
J_2\ddot\theta_2+b(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)+k(\theta_2-\theta_1)=0.\qquad(5.19\text{b})
$$

$J_1$ and $J_2$ are the rotational inertias of satellite and package. The spring and damper torques are equal and opposite on the two discs, which is why the angle differences change sign between the two equations. The control torque appears only in Eq. 5.19a, while the variable to be controlled is $\theta_2$: one input $T_c$, one output $\theta_2$, so a single-input, single-output system. In Lab 2 the system is built in Simulink both as a transfer function and as a state-space block, and the two are equivalent. The equations are already linear; the issue is that they are second order.

### Choosing the states

Higher-order derivatives can be expressed as a chain of first-order derivatives. There are two angles, but each appears up to its second derivative, so two states are not enough: the count is the sum of the derivative orders, $2+2=4$. Give each angle two states, itself and its rate (Eq. 5.20):

$$
x_1=\theta_2,\qquad x_2=\dot\theta_2,\qquad x_3=\theta_1,\qquad x_4=\dot\theta_1.
$$

The state vector is always a column, $n\times1$, here $4\times1$. The measured angle comes first, which will make $\mathbf C$ simple. Differentiating the state vector gives $\dot{\mathbf x}=[\dot\theta_2,\ddot\theta_2,\dot\theta_1,\ddot\theta_1]^T$, so the second derivatives of the original equations now sit inside $\dot{\mathbf x}$; the job is to write each entry as a function of the $x$'s alone, with no $\theta$ left.

### Differentiating the states

Two rows are free, because the derivative of an angle is the next state:

$$
\dot x_1=\dot\theta_2=x_2,\qquad \dot x_3=\dot\theta_1=x_4.
$$

The other two come from Eq. 5.19, solved for the accelerations. From 5.19b, move the damper and spring terms to the right, which flips their signs to $(\theta_1-\theta_2)$ and $(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)$, then divide by $J_2$:

$$
\ddot\theta_2=\frac{b}{J_2}(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)+\frac{k}{J_2}(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

From 5.19a, divide by $J_1$:

$$
\ddot\theta_1=\frac{b}{J_1}(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)+\frac{k}{J_1}(\theta_2-\theta_1)+\frac{T_c}{J_1}.
$$

With $J_1=1$ in the numerical values used below, omitting this $J_1$ would make no numerical difference, so it has to be checked symbolically.

Replace each angle and rate by its state:

$$
\dot x_1=x_2,\qquad(5.22\text{a})
$$

$$
\dot x_2=\frac{b}{J_2}(x_4-x_2)+\frac{k}{J_2}(x_3-x_1),\qquad(5.22\text{b})
$$

$$
\dot x_3=x_4,\qquad(5.22\text{c})
$$

$$
\dot x_4=\frac{b}{J_1}(x_2-x_4)+\frac{k}{J_1}(x_1-x_3)+\frac{T_c}{J_1}.\qquad(5.22\text{d})
$$

### The state-space model

The input term $T_c/J_1$ is not a function of the states, so it cannot go into $\mathbf A\mathbf x$. It belongs in $\mathbf B\mathbf u$. The split has a meaning: $\mathbf A$ holds the natural dynamics, what the system does if left alone, and $\mathbf B\mathbf u$ is how control acts on it. The open-loop poles of the system are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ (shown in [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability#4. Stability from the eigenvalues|5.4]]).

Collect coefficients of $x_1,\dots,x_4$ in each row, with $u=T_c$ and the single sensor $y=\theta_2=x_1$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0\\-\dfrac{k}{J_2}&-\dfrac{b}{J_2}&\dfrac{k}{J_2}&\dfrac{b}{J_2}\\0&0&0&1\\\dfrac{k}{J_1}&\dfrac{b}{J_1}&-\dfrac{k}{J_1}&-\dfrac{b}{J_1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf A}\mathbf x+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\\dfrac{1}{J_1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf B}u,\qquad y=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf C}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.23)
$$

One actuator and one sensor make $\mathbf B$ $4\times1$ and $\mathbf C$ $1\times4$, so this is a single-input, single-output (SISO) system. The other three states are not measured.

With the values of Chapter 5 $J_1=1$, $J_2=0.1$, $k=0.091$, $b=0.0036$: $k/J_2=0.91$, $b/J_2=0.036$, $k/J_1=0.091$, $b/J_1=0.0036$, so

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0\\-0.91&-0.036&0.91&0.036\\0&0&0&1\\0.091&0.0036&-0.091&-0.0036\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}u,\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.24)
$$

### The transfer function by hand

*Not lectured.* Eq. 5.25 comes from MATLAB's `ss2tf`; by hand, Laplace-transform Eq. 5.19 with zero initial conditions:

$$
(J_1s^2+bs+k)\Theta_1-(bs+k)\Theta_2=T_c,
$$

$$
-(bs+k)\Theta_1+(J_2s^2+bs+k)\Theta_2=0.
$$

The second gives $\Theta_1$ in terms of $\Theta_2$:

$$
\Theta_1=\frac{J_2s^2+bs+k}{bs+k}\,\Theta_2.
$$

Substitute into the first:

$$
\Big[(J_1s^2+bs+k)(J_2s^2+bs+k)-(bs+k)^2\Big]\frac{\Theta_2}{bs+k}=T_c.
$$

Expand the bracket. The $(bs+k)^2$ terms cancel:

$$
(J_1s^2+bs+k)(J_2s^2+bs+k)-(bs+k)^2=J_1J_2s^4+(J_1+J_2)(bs+k)s^2.
$$

Factor out $s^2$ and rearrange:

$$
\frac{\Theta_2}{T_c}=\frac{bs+k}{s^2\big[J_1J_2s^2+(J_1+J_2)(bs+k)\big]}.
$$

Divide top and bottom by $J_1J_2=0.1$ and insert numbers:

$$
G(s)=\frac{0.036s+0.91}{s^2(s^2+0.0396s+1.001)}=\frac{0.036(s+25.28)}{s^2(s^2+0.0396s+1.001)}.
$$

Eq. 5.25, $0.036(s+25)/[s^2(s^2+0.04s+1)]$, is this rounded.

![[amc_satellite_response.png|900]]

The poles are $s=0$ (twice) and $s=-0.0198\pm1.0003j$; the zero is $s=-25.28$. The oscillatory pair has natural frequency $\omega_n=\sqrt{1.001}=1.0005$ rad/s and damping ratio $\zeta=0.0396/(2\omega_n)=0.0198$: the tether rings with a period of about 6.3 s and hardly decays. The double pole at the origin is the pair rotating together: nothing resists a change in the absolute attitude. The figure shows the result of a torque impulse giving the satellite 0.1 rad/s: both bodies drift at $0.1/(J_1+J_2)=0.0909$ rad/s, by conservation of angular momentum, with the tether oscillation on top.

> [!warning] Two poles at the origin are not marginally stable
> The satellite model has two integrators (two poles at the origin) and two stable poles. A repeated pole at the origin is not marginally stable. The free response contains a term growing like $t$ (the drift in the figure), and a constant torque gives $\theta\propto t^2$, unbounded. In matrix terms the zero eigenvalue of $\mathbf A$ is repeated but $\mathbf A$ has rank 3, so there is only one eigenvector for it (a Jordan block). A single simple pole at the origin would be marginal. See [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability#4. Stability from the eigenvalues|5.4 §4]].

The poles of $G(s)$ equal the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ in Eq. 5.24 (shown in [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability|5.4]]).

## 2. Transfer functions to state space (5.26–5.34)

### A simple lag

Start with

$$
G(s)=\frac{1}{s-T_1}=\frac{W}{R}.\qquad(5.26)
$$

Cross-multiply:

$$
R=(s-T_1)W=sW-T_1W.\qquad(5.27)
$$

Multiplication by $s$ in the Laplace domain is differentiation in time (zero initial conditions), so

$$
r=\dot w-T_1w.\qquad(5.28)
$$

Take the state $x=w$ and the input $u=r$, and solve for the derivative:

$$
\dot x=T_1x+u,\qquad y=x.\qquad(5.29)
$$

So $\mathbf A=T_1$, $\mathbf B=1$, $\mathbf C=1$: a $1\times1$ "matrix" is just a number. State-space analysis works in the time domain, so the transfer function is taken back there first: cross-multiply to clear the fraction, then use the rule that each factor of $s$ is one time derivative (and each $1/s$ one integral) under zero initial conditions.

For $T_1>0$ the pole is at $s=T_1$, in the right half-plane, so the system is unstable. The time domain gives the same result. With no input ($r=0$), Eq. 5.28 is

$$
\frac{dw}{dt}=T_1w.
$$

Separate the variables:

$$
\frac{dw}{w}=T_1\,dt.
$$

Integrate both sides:

$$
\ln w=T_1t+c.
$$

Exponentiate, and write $w_0=e^c$, the value at $t=0$:

$$
w(t)=w_0e^{T_1t},
$$

which grows without bound when $T_1>0$ (right panel of the figure in [[#Where the poles go]]). The usual way of writing a lag, $1/(s+a)$ with $a>0$, has $T_1=-a$ and decays.

### More poles and some zeros

$$
G(s)=\frac{W}{R}=\frac{V}{R}\frac{W}{V}=\frac{1}{s^3+6s^2+9s+3}\,(5s^2+2s+2).\qquad(5.30)
$$

The split introduces an intermediate signal $V$ so that the denominator and numerator can be handled separately. Denominator first. Cross-multiply $V/R=1/(s^3+6s^2+9s+3)$:

$$
(s^3+6s^2+9s+3)V=R.
$$

Back to the time domain:

$$
\dddot v+6\ddot v+9\dot v+3v=r.\qquad(5.31)
$$

Each $s$ became one time derivative, so $s^3$ is three of them. The rule of thumb is one state per order of the highest derivative: third order, so three states, $x_1=v$, $x_2=\dot v$, $x_3=\ddot v$. Differentiate each:

$$
\dot x_1=\dot v=x_2,
$$

$$
\dot x_2=\ddot v=x_3,
$$

$$
\dot x_3=\dddot v=-3x_1-9x_2-6x_3+r.\qquad(5.32)
$$

The last row comes from Eq. 5.31 solved for $\dddot v=r-3v-9\dot v-6\ddot v$, written in the order $v,\dot v,\ddot v$ so the $x$'s can be substituted directly. It is fine for $\dot x_3$ to contain $x_3$ itself, as long as no derivative appears on the right. The input coefficient in the last row is 1, as in Eq. 5.31.

The input $r$ appears only in the $\dot x_3$ row, so $\mathbf B=[0,0,1]^T$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-3&-9&-6\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}r.\qquad(5.33)
$$

$\mathbf A$ is complete after using only the denominator. The poles of the transfer function therefore sit entirely in $\mathbf A$, and the zeros have nothing to do with it. Since poles, not zeros, decide stability, $\mathbf A$ decides whether the system is stable.

The stability of these poles follows from the Routh–Hurwitz test from Year 2 ([[Routh-Hurwitz Stability Criterion]]), which avoids solving the cubic (the test was not carried out in L16). For $s^3+6s^2+9s+3$ the array is

$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}s^3&1&9\\s^2&6&3\\s^1&\dfrac{6\cdot9-1\cdot3}{6}=8.5&0\\s^0&3&\end{array}
$$

The first column, $1,6,8.5,3$, has no sign change, so all three poles are in the left half-plane. They are $s=-0.468$, $-1.653$ and $-3.879$, which are also the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ in Eq. 5.33 (checked numerically).

For the numerator, cross-multiply $W/V=5s^2+2s+2$:

$$
W=5s^2V+2sV+2V.
$$

Inverse Laplace transform:

$$
w=5\ddot v+2\dot v+2v.
$$

Substitute the states:

$$
w=2x_1+2x_2+5x_3=\begin{bmatrix}2&2&5\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.34)
$$

So $\mathbf C=[2\ \ 2\ \ 5]$: the numerator coefficients in reverse order.

$\mathbf C$ is the observation matrix. In practice not every state can be measured; $\mathbf C$ turns the states into the outputs that sensors can actually observe. Whether all the states can be recovered from those outputs is the question of observability (around week 10). The course spends nine weeks on $\mathbf A$, three on $\mathbf B$, and little on $\mathbf C$.

Every product in the model has to be dimensionally consistent: the inner dimensions of each product match, and the outer ones give the size of the result. Here $\mathbf A\mathbf x$ is $(3\times3)(3\times1)=3\times1$, $\mathbf Br$ is $(3\times1)(1\times1)=3\times1$, matching $\dot{\mathbf x}$, and $\mathbf C\mathbf x$ is $(1\times3)(3\times1)=1\times1$, a scalar output $w$. If the sizes do not match, MATLAB or Python will refuse the multiplication, so this check catches mistakes early.

![[amc_integrator_chain.png|900]]

In the figure the states are the outputs of three integrators in a chain. The denominator coefficients are feedback gains from those outputs back to the input, so they fill the last row of $\mathbf A$; the numerator coefficients are taps from the same signals forward to the output, so they fill $\mathbf C$. $\mathbf C(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B$ recovers $(5s^2+2s+2)/(s^3+6s^2+9s+3)$ (checked with `scipy.signal.ss2tf`).

In this construction the poles are in $\mathbf A$, the input in $\mathbf B$ and the zeros in $\mathbf C$, and the numerator plays no role in $\mathbf A$. The first part is general: the poles are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ in any minimal realisation (one with no cancelled modes). Where the zeros go is a property of this construction, not of state space. The state-space model of a transfer function is not unique: any change of states $\mathbf z=\mathbf T\mathbf x$ gives a different $\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf C$ with the same $G(s)$, and the realisation textbooks call observable canonical form puts the numerator into $\mathbf B$ instead. The construction also needs the numerator degree below the denominator degree; if they were equal, part of the input would reach the output directly and $\mathbf D\neq0$.

### Where the poles go

![[amc_tf_poles_and_lag.png|900]]

The left panel puts the two examples side by side in the $s$-plane. The poles of Eq. 5.30 (crosses) land exactly on the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ from Eq. 5.33 (squares), all three in the left half-plane, while the zeros $-0.2\pm0.6j$ appear nowhere in $\mathbf A$. The right panel is the simple lag: a pole at $s=T_1<0$ decays, $T_1=0$ holds its initial value, and $T_1>0$ grows as $e^{T_1t}$.

## 3. Block diagrams to state space (5.35–5.39)

*Chapter 5 only: not lectured, and not on the exam. Worked here as a reference for Practice problem 4.*

Fig. 5.3 of Chapter 5 is a multi-input, multi-output (MIMO) system: references $r_1,r_2$, outputs $y_1,y_2$, gains $K_1,K_2$ and four first-order blocks. The signals are

- $u_1=K_1(r_1-y_1)$ feeds $1/(s+1)$ (giving $x_1$) and $0.4/(s+0.5)$ (giving $x_3$);
- $u_2=K_2(r_2-y_2)$ feeds $5/(s+5)$ (giving $x_2$) and $4/(s+2)$ (giving $x_4$);
- $y_1=x_1+x_2$ and $y_2=x_3+x_4$, each fed back negatively to its own reference.

Four states, two inputs, two outputs, so $\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{4\times4}$, $\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{4\times2}$, $\mathbf C\in\mathbb R^{2\times4}$.

The output matrix is immediate:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&0\\0&0&1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\\x_4\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(5.35)
$$

### Row 1

From the block,

$$
\frac{X_1}{U_1}=\frac{1}{s+1}.\qquad(5.36)
$$

Cross-multiply and invert:

$$
\dot x_1+x_1=u_1.\qquad(5.37)
$$

$u_1$ is not an input: the inputs are $r_1$ and $r_2$. Substitute $u_1=K_1(r_1-y_1)$, then $y_1=x_1+x_2$:

$$
\dot x_1=-x_1+K_1(r_1-x_1-x_2).\qquad(5.38)
$$

Collect: $\dot x_1=(-1-K_1)x_1-K_1x_2+K_1r_1$.

### Row 2

$X_2/U_2=5/(s+5)$, so $\dot x_2+5x_2=5u_2$. Substitute $u_2=K_2(r_2-x_3-x_4)$:

$$
\dot x_2=-5x_2-5K_2x_3-5K_2x_4+5K_2r_2.
$$

### Row 3

$X_3/U_1=0.4/(s+0.5)$, so $\dot x_3+0.5x_3=0.4u_1$. Substitute $u_1=K_1(r_1-x_1-x_2)$:

$$
\dot x_3=-0.4K_1x_1-0.4K_1x_2-0.5x_3+0.4K_1r_1.
$$

### Row 4

$X_4/U_2=4/(s+2)$, so $\dot x_4+2x_4=4u_2$. Substitute $u_2$:

$$
\dot x_4=-4K_2x_3+(-2-4K_2)x_4+4K_2r_2.
$$

### Assembled

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}-1-K_1&-K_1&0&0\\0&-5&-5K_2&-5K_2\\-0.4K_1&-0.4K_1&-0.5&0\\0&0&-4K_2&-2-4K_2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}K_1&0\\0&5K_2\\0.4K_1&0\\0&4K_2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf r.\qquad(5.39)
$$

(Checked symbolically as the Jacobian of the four rows.) The gains appear inside $\mathbf A$, not only in $\mathbf B$: closing a feedback loop changes the system's own dynamics. The cross-connections ($u_1$ into the $x_3$ block, $u_2$ into the $x_2$ block) couple the two loops through rows 2 and 3.
