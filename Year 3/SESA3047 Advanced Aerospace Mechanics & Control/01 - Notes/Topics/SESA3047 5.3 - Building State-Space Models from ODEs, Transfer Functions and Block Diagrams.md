# SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams

> [!abstract] Summary
> Any linear model can be put in state-space form, whatever it starts as. A second-order ODE gets two states, the variable and its rate; the satellite-and-instrument example (used in Lab 2) becomes a $4\times4$ model with one torque input and one angle sensor. A transfer function is converted by cross-multiplying: the denominator becomes a chain of integrators that fixes $\mathbf A$, the input enters through $\mathbf B$, and the numerator fixes $\mathbf C$. A block diagram is converted block by block, with each block's input rewritten in terms of states and reference inputs.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** given a set of linear ODEs (possibly second order), a transfer function, or a block diagram, and asked for $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$, $\mathbf C$.
> - **Assumptions:** linear time-invariant; transfer functions strictly proper (numerator degree below denominator degree, so $\mathbf D=0$); zero initial conditions when moving between $s$ and $t$.
> - **Method:** 1. For an $n$th-order ODE, take the variable and its first $n-1$ derivatives as states. 2. For $G=N(s)/D(s)$, introduce $V$ with $D(s)V=R$ and $W=N(s)V$. 3. Write $\dot x_i$ for each state; the last row comes from the ODE. 4. For a block diagram, write each block as a first-order ODE, then replace every internal signal ($u_1$, $y_1$, …) by states and references. 5. Read off $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$, $\mathbf C$ and check the dimensions $n\times n$, $n\times m$, $p\times n$.
> - **Common mistakes:** leaving an internal signal such as $u_1=K_1(r_1-y_1)$ as an "input" (only $r_1,r_2$ are inputs); forgetting to divide the input torque by $J_1$ (the handout's own Eq. 5.21d does this).
> - **Practise:** [[#1. Second-order equations as first-order states (5.19–5.24)]] · [[SESA3047 Chapter 5 Practice Problems - Solutions#Problem 2. Transfer functions to state space|Practice problem 2]] · [[SESA3047 Chapter 5 Practice Problems - Solutions#Problem 4. The MISO block diagram|Practice problem 4]]

The aircraft model of [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model|5.2]] happened to arrive with first derivatives only. Most models do not. A spring–mass system has $\ddot x$, a transfer function from the Year 2 control module has powers of $s$, and a controller drawn as a block diagram has signals looping between blocks. This note shows how each becomes $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$. The tricks are mechanical, but each has one place where it is easy to go wrong, and the handout's own equations contain two such slips.

## 1. Second-order equations as first-order states (5.19–5.24)

### The system

A satellite carries an instrument package on a flexible tether (handout Fig. 5.2). The satellite's attitude relative to a star is $\theta_1$, the instrument's $\theta_2$. Cold-gas jets on the satellite apply the torque $T_c$; a star tracker measures $\theta_2$. The two bodies are modelled as discs joined by a torsional spring $k$ and damper $b$. The handout says the derivation of the equations matters less than the procedure.

Where the equations come from, briefly: the spring twists by $\theta_1-\theta_2$ and the damper by $\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2$, and each body feels equal and opposite reaction torques. Newton's second law for rotation on each disc gives

$$
J_1\ddot\theta_1+b(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)+k(\theta_1-\theta_2)=T_c,\qquad(5.19\text{a})
$$

$$
J_2\ddot\theta_2+b(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)+k(\theta_2-\theta_1)=0.\qquad(5.19\text{b})
$$

$J_1$ and $J_2$ are the rotational inertias of satellite and package. These are already linear; the issue is that they are second order.

### Choosing the states

The handout's key idea: higher-order derivatives can be expressed as a sequence of first-order derivatives. Give each angle two states, itself and its rate (Eq. 5.20):

$$
x_1=\theta_2,\qquad x_2=\dot\theta_2,\qquad x_3=\theta_1,\qquad x_4=\dot\theta_1.
$$

The measured angle comes first, which will make $\mathbf C$ simple.

### Differentiating the states

Two rows are free, because the derivative of an angle is the next state:

$$
\dot x_1=\dot\theta_2=x_2,\qquad \dot x_3=\dot\theta_1=x_4.
$$

The other two come from Eq. 5.19, solved for the accelerations. From 5.19b, divide by $J_2$:

$$
\ddot\theta_2=\frac{b}{J_2}(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)+\frac{k}{J_2}(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

From 5.19a, divide by $J_1$:

$$
\ddot\theta_1=\frac{b}{J_1}(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)+\frac{k}{J_1}(\theta_2-\theta_1)+\frac{T_c}{J_1}.
$$

The handout's Eq. 5.21d writes the last term as $+T_c$, without the $J_1$; its own Eq. 5.22d and the $\mathbf B$ matrix have $T_c/J_1$, which is correct. With $J_1=1$ the numbers agree anyway, which is exactly how the slip would survive into a lab.

Replace each angle and rate by its state:

$$
\dot x_1=x_2,\qquad(5.22\text{a})
$$

$$
\dot x_2=\frac{b}{J_2}(x_4-x_2)+\frac{k}{J_2}(x_3-x_1),\qquad(5.22\text{b})
$$

$$
\dot x_3=x_4,\qquad(5.22\text{c})
$$

$$
\dot x_4=\frac{b}{J_1}(x_2-x_4)+\frac{k}{J_1}(x_1-x_3)+\frac{T_c}{J_1}.\qquad(5.22\text{d})
$$

### The state-space model

Collect coefficients of $x_1,\dots,x_4$ in each row, with $u=T_c$ and the single sensor $y=\theta_2=x_1$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0\\-\dfrac{k}{J_2}&-\dfrac{b}{J_2}&\dfrac{k}{J_2}&\dfrac{b}{J_2}\\0&0&0&1\\\dfrac{k}{J_1}&\dfrac{b}{J_1}&-\dfrac{k}{J_1}&-\dfrac{b}{J_1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf A}\mathbf x+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\\dfrac{1}{J_1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf B}u,\qquad y=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf C}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.23)
$$

One actuator and one sensor make $\mathbf B$ $4\times1$ and $\mathbf C$ $1\times4$, so this is a single-input, single-output (SISO) system. The other three states are not measured.

With the handout's values $J_1=1$, $J_2=0.1$, $k=0.091$, $b=0.0036$: $k/J_2=0.91$, $b/J_2=0.036$, $k/J_1=0.091$, $b/J_1=0.0036$, so

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0\\-0.91&-0.036&0.91&0.036\\0&0&0&1\\0.091&0.0036&-0.091&-0.0036\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}u,\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.24)
$$

### The transfer function by hand

The handout gets Eq. 5.25 from MATLAB's `ss2tf`. It is quicker to understand by hand. Laplace-transform Eq. 5.19 with zero initial conditions:

$$
(J_1s^2+bs+k)\Theta_1-(bs+k)\Theta_2=T_c,
$$

$$
-(bs+k)\Theta_1+(J_2s^2+bs+k)\Theta_2=0.
$$

The second gives $\Theta_1$ in terms of $\Theta_2$:

$$
\Theta_1=\frac{J_2s^2+bs+k}{bs+k}\,\Theta_2.
$$

Substitute into the first:

$$
\Big[(J_1s^2+bs+k)(J_2s^2+bs+k)-(bs+k)^2\Big]\frac{\Theta_2}{bs+k}=T_c.
$$

Expand the bracket. The $(bs+k)^2$ terms cancel:

$$
(J_1s^2+bs+k)(J_2s^2+bs+k)-(bs+k)^2=J_1J_2s^4+(J_1+J_2)(bs+k)s^2.
$$

Factor out $s^2$ and rearrange:

$$
\frac{\Theta_2}{T_c}=\frac{bs+k}{s^2\big[J_1J_2s^2+(J_1+J_2)(bs+k)\big]}.
$$

Divide top and bottom by $J_1J_2=0.1$ and insert numbers:

$$
G(s)=\frac{0.036s+0.91}{s^2(s^2+0.0396s+1.001)}=\frac{0.036(s+25.28)}{s^2(s^2+0.0396s+1.001)}.
$$

The handout's Eq. 5.25, $0.036(s+25)/[s^2(s^2+0.04s+1)]$, is this rounded.

![[amc_satellite_response.png|900]]

The poles are $s=0$ (twice) and $s=-0.0198\pm1.0003j$; the zero is $s=-25.28$. The oscillatory pair has natural frequency $\omega_n=\sqrt{1.001}=1.0005$ rad/s and damping ratio $\zeta=0.0396/(2\omega_n)=0.0198$: the tether rings with a period of about 6.3 s and hardly decays. The double pole at the origin is the pair rotating together: nothing resists a change in the absolute attitude. The figure shows the result of a torque impulse giving the satellite 0.1 rad/s: both bodies drift at $0.1/(J_1+J_2)=0.0909$ rad/s, by conservation of angular momentum, with the tether oscillation on top.

> [!warning] "Marginally stable" with two poles at the origin
> The handout calls the system *"marginally stable as there are two integrators (two poles at the origin) and two stable poles"*. Strictly, a repeated pole at the origin is not marginally stable. The free response contains a term growing like $t$ (the drift in the figure), and a constant torque gives $\theta\propto t^2$, unbounded. In matrix terms the zero eigenvalue of $\mathbf A$ is repeated but $\mathbf A$ has rank 3, so there is only one eigenvector for it (a Jordan block). A single simple pole at the origin would be marginal. See [[SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability#4. Stability from the eigenvalues|5.4 §4]].

The handout notes that the poles of $G(s)$ equal the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ in Eq. 5.24; this is shown in 5.4.

## 2. Transfer functions to state space (5.26–5.34)

### A simple lag

Start with

$$
G(s)=\frac{1}{s-T_1}=\frac{W}{R}.\qquad(5.26)
$$

Cross-multiply:

$$
R=(s-T_1)W=sW-T_1W.\qquad(5.27)
$$

Multiplication by $s$ in the Laplace domain is differentiation in time (zero initial conditions), so

$$
r=\dot w-T_1w.\qquad(5.28)
$$

Take the state $x=w$ and the input $u=r$, and solve for the derivative:

$$
\dot x=T_1x+u,\qquad y=x.\qquad(5.29)
$$

So $\mathbf A=T_1$, $\mathbf B=1$, $\mathbf C=1$. The pole is at $s=T_1$, so this lag is stable only for $T_1<0$; the usual way of writing a lag, $1/(s+a)$ with $a>0$, has $T_1=-a$.

### More poles and some zeros

$$
G(s)=\frac{W}{R}=\frac{V}{R}\frac{W}{V}=\frac{1}{s^3+6s^2+9s+3}\,(5s^2+2s+2).\qquad(5.30)
$$

The split introduces an intermediate signal $V$ so that the denominator and numerator can be handled separately. Denominator first:

$$
(s^3+6s^2+9s+3)V=R.
$$

Back to the time domain:

$$
\dddot v+6\ddot v+9\dot v+3v=r.\qquad(5.31)
$$

Third order, so three states: $x_1=v$, $x_2=\dot v$, $x_3=\ddot v$. Differentiate each:

$$
\dot x_1=\dot v=x_2,
$$

$$
\dot x_2=\ddot v=x_3,
$$

$$
\dot x_3=\dddot v=-3x_1-9x_2-6x_3+r.\qquad(5.32)
$$

(The handout's Eq. 5.32 writes the derivatives as $\dot w,\ddot w,\dddot w$ where it means $v$, and ends with $+5r$. From Eq. 5.31 the input coefficient is 1, which is what its own Eq. 5.33 uses.)

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-3&-9&-6\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}r.\qquad(5.33)
$$

Now the numerator. From $W=(5s^2+2s+2)V$:

$$
w=5\ddot v+2\dot v+2v=2x_1+2x_2+5x_3=\begin{bmatrix}2&2&5\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\qquad(5.34)
$$

So $\mathbf C=[2\ \ 2\ \ 5]$: the numerator coefficients in reverse order.

![[amc_integrator_chain.png|900]]

The figure shows why. The states are the outputs of three integrators in a chain. The denominator coefficients are feedback gains from those outputs back to the input, so they fill the last row of $\mathbf A$; the numerator coefficients are taps from the same signals forward to the output, so they fill $\mathbf C$. $\mathbf C(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B$ recovers $(5s^2+2s+2)/(s^3+6s^2+9s+3)$ (checked with `scipy.signal.ss2tf`).

The handout says the numerator playing no role in $\mathbf A$ *"is ALWAYS true"*, with the poles in $\mathbf A$, the input in $\mathbf B$ and the zeros in $\mathbf C$. The first part is general: the poles are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ in any realisation. Where the zeros go is a property of this construction, not of state space. The state-space model of a transfer function is not unique: any change of states $\mathbf z=\mathbf T\mathbf x$ gives a different $\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf C$ with the same $G(s)$, and the realisation textbooks call observable canonical form puts the numerator into $\mathbf B$ instead. The construction also needs the numerator degree below the denominator degree; if they were equal, part of the input would reach the output directly and $\mathbf D\neq0$.

## 3. Block diagrams to state space (5.35–5.39)

The handout's Fig. 5.3 is a multi-input, multi-output (MIMO) system: references $r_1,r_2$, outputs $y_1,y_2$, gains $K_1,K_2$ and four first-order blocks. The signals are

- $u_1=K_1(r_1-y_1)$ feeds $1/(s+1)$ (giving $x_1$) and $0.4/(s+0.5)$ (giving $x_3$);
- $u_2=K_2(r_2-y_2)$ feeds $5/(s+5)$ (giving $x_2$) and $4/(s+2)$ (giving $x_4$);
- $y_1=x_1+x_2$ and $y_2=x_3+x_4$, each fed back negatively to its own reference.

Four states, two inputs, two outputs, so $\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{4\times4}$, $\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{4\times2}$, $\mathbf C\in\mathbb R^{2\times4}$.

The output matrix is immediate:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&0\\0&0&1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\\x_4\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(5.35)
$$

### Row 1

From the block,

$$
\frac{X_1}{U_1}=\frac{1}{s+1}.\qquad(5.36)
$$

Cross-multiply and invert:

$$
\dot x_1+x_1=u_1.\qquad(5.37)
$$

$u_1$ is not an input: the inputs are $r_1$ and $r_2$. Substitute $u_1=K_1(r_1-y_1)$, then $y_1=x_1+x_2$:

$$
\dot x_1=-x_1+K_1(r_1-x_1-x_2).\qquad(5.38)
$$

Collect: $\dot x_1=(-1-K_1)x_1-K_1x_2+K_1r_1$.

### Row 2

$X_2/U_2=5/(s+5)$, so $\dot x_2+5x_2=5u_2$. Substitute $u_2=K_2(r_2-x_3-x_4)$:

$$
\dot x_2=-5x_2-5K_2x_3-5K_2x_4+5K_2r_2.
$$

### Row 3

$X_3/U_1=0.4/(s+0.5)$, so $\dot x_3+0.5x_3=0.4u_1$. Substitute $u_1=K_1(r_1-x_1-x_2)$:

$$
\dot x_3=-0.4K_1x_1-0.4K_1x_2-0.5x_3+0.4K_1r_1.
$$

### Row 4

$X_4/U_2=4/(s+2)$, so $\dot x_4+2x_4=4u_2$. Substitute $u_2$:

$$
\dot x_4=-4K_2x_3+(-2-4K_2)x_4+4K_2r_2.
$$

### Assembled

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}-1-K_1&-K_1&0&0\\0&-5&-5K_2&-5K_2\\-0.4K_1&-0.4K_1&-0.5&0\\0&0&-4K_2&-2-4K_2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}K_1&0\\0&5K_2\\0.4K_1&0\\0&4K_2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf r.\qquad(5.39)
$$

(Checked symbolically as the Jacobian of the four rows.) The gains appear inside $\mathbf A$, not only in $\mathbf B$: closing a feedback loop changes the system's own dynamics, which is the whole point of feedback control. The cross-connections ($u_1$ into the $x_3$ block, $u_2$ into the $x_2$ block) couple the two loops through rows 2 and 3.
