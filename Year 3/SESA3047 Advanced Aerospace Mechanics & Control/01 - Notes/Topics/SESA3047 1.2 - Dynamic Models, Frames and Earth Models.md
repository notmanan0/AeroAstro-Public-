# SESA3047 1.2 - Dynamic Models, Frames and Earth Models

> [!abstract] Summary
> A model is useful only for the question and operating range its assumptions support. High fidelity adds represented physics, inputs and computational cost; it does not guarantee validity. Dynamic models are classified by spatial representation, time dependence and linearity. Aerospace vehicles are nonlinear, but linearisation about a trim condition produces a useful local model. Motion must also be described relative to a named reference frame and resolved in a named coordinate system. For many aircraft problems, the flat-Earth approximation makes a local NED frame effectively inertial with constant gravity, while vehicle forces and angular motion are most convenient in body-fixed FRD coordinates.

## 1. Plant versus model

The **plant** is the physical aircraft, spacecraft or subsystem. The **model** is a mathematical representation of selected behaviour.

One physical aircraft may need different models for:

- rigid-body flight dynamics;
- structural loads and flexible modes;
- control-system design;
- mission analysis;
- high-fidelity simulation.

A rigid-body model assumes that distances between points on the vehicle remain constant. It therefore omits structural deformation. That model is credible only when flexible motion has negligible influence on the dynamics of interest.

> [!quote] Modelling principle
> “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” Usefulness depends on whether the assumptions match the application.

## 2. Variables, parameters, inputs, outputs and states

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Variable | quantity that can change | altitude, velocity, angle, actuator position |
| Parameter | quantity treated as fixed in one model instance | mass, inertia, stiffness |
| Input | imposed quantity that drives the model | elevator command, thrust request, gust |
| Output | selected quantity to observe or use | pitch angle, acceleration, sensor reading |
| State | minimum information that, with the input and model, determines future evolution | displacement and velocity of an oscillator |

For a second-order oscillator, displacement alone is not enough: position and velocity form a possible minimum state set.

## 3. Fidelity, complexity and validity

**High fidelity** represents more physical phenomena. It normally requires more parameters, data and computation. **Low fidelity** retains only the effects needed for a purpose.

Example:

- a rigid six-degree-of-freedom vehicle model is efficient when structural flexibility is negligible;
- a flexible multibody model can capture wing bending, vibration and rigid–flexible coupling, but needs extra degrees of freedom and structural data.

Increasing complexity does not automatically improve a prediction. A detailed model can be worse if its parameters are uncertain, the operating condition violates its assumptions, or its outputs do not answer the engineering question.

$$
\boxed{\text{Best model}=\text{simplest model credible for the stated purpose and range}.}
$$

## 4. Spatial and temporal classifications

### Continuum versus lumped parameter

- **Continuum model:** fields vary continuously in space, as in airflow or structural deformation; typically governed by PDEs.
- **Lumped-parameter model:** properties are concentrated into a finite set of coordinates or degrees of freedom; typically governed by ODEs.

### Static versus dynamic

- **Static model:** relates input and response at equilibrium, with no time evolution.
- **Dynamic model:** describes response as it evolves from initial conditions under inputs and disturbances.

## 5. Linear and nonlinear models

A nonlinear model does not generally satisfy superposition. Aerospace sources of nonlinearity include:

- large rotations;
- translation–rotation coupling;
- nonlinear aerodynamic forces and moments;
- actuator saturation and other physical limits.

The lecture oscillator is

$$
m\ddot x+c\dot x+f(x)=0,
\qquad
f(x)=kx+\mu x^3.
$$

The cubic term makes the model nonlinear. Neglect it near $x=0$:

$$
m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0.
$$

This linear model is a local approximation. It can be accurate for small motion and fail rapidly as displacement grows.

More generally, for $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x,\mathbf u)$, linearise about an operating condition $(\mathbf x_0,\mathbf u_0)$:

$$
\delta\dot{\mathbf x}
\approx
\left.\frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf x}\right|_0\delta\mathbf x
+\left.\frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf u}\right|_0\delta\mathbf u.
$$

The perturbation model is useful near the chosen trim point; it does not make the physical vehicle globally linear.

### Worked example — linearising the cubic oscillator

*(Suggested, not lecturer-set: a preview of the linearisation step flagged at L3, ll. 226–230.)*

Start from $m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx+\mu x^3=0$ and choose states $x_1=x$, $x_2=\dot x$:

$$
\dot x_1=x_2,
\qquad
\dot x_2=-\frac km x_1-\frac cm x_2-\frac\mu m x_1^3 .
$$

This is $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)$ with a nonlinear $\mathbf f$ because of the $x_1^3$ term. Linearise about an operating point $x_1=x_0$, $x_2=0$ with $\delta\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\,\delta\mathbf x$, $\mathbf A=\partial\mathbf f/\partial\mathbf x|_0$:

$$
\mathbf A=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1\\[2pt]
-\dfrac{k+3\mu x_0^2}{m} & -\dfrac cm
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

- About $x_0=0$: $\mathbf A$ reduces to the linear oscillator $m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0$, which is the lecture's small-displacement model.
- About $x_0\neq0$: the local stiffness becomes $k_{\text{eff}}=k+3\mu x_0^2$. The linear model is valid only near the point it was built at, and each operating point has its own linear model. This is exactly why aircraft equations are linearised about a **trim** condition.

> [!note] Where does $x_0\neq0$ come from?
> With no load, the only equilibrium of $m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx+\mu x^3=0$ is $x=0$. A non-zero operating point needs a steady load $F_0=kx_0+\mu x_0^3$ to hold it there. That load is the analogue of trim, where steady lift, thrust and control deflection hold the aircraft at its operating condition. Measure $\delta x$ from that point and $F_0$ cancels, leaving $m\,\delta\ddot x+c\,\delta\dot x+k_{\text{eff}}\,\delta x=0$.

Geometrically, each linear model is the **tangent** to the restoring-force curve at the operating point. Its slope is $\partial f/\partial x|_{x_0}=k+3\mu x_0^2$, the $(2,1)$ entry of $\mathbf A$ times $-m$:

## 6. Time-invariant and time-varying models

An LTI oscillator has constant coefficients:

$$
m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0,
\qquad m,c,k=\text{constant}.
$$

A linear time-varying example is

$$
m\ddot x+c\dot x+k(t)x=0,
\qquad k(t)=k_0+\Delta k(t).
$$

It remains linear in $x,\dot x,\ddot x$, but the model properties change explicitly with time. Aerospace examples include propellant depletion, deployment and scheduled configuration changes.

A slowly varying physical system may still be approximated as time invariant over a short interval.

## 7. Dynamic-model hierarchy

From greater generality and cost toward simpler local models:

$$
\text{nonlinear PDEs}
\rightarrow
\text{linear PDEs}
\rightarrow
\text{nonlinear ODEs}
\rightarrow
\text{linear ODEs}
\rightarrow
\text{static model}.
$$

Each reduction removes represented physics or restricts the range of validity. It should be justified rather than treated as automatically better or worse.

## 8. Kinematics and dynamics

**Kinematics** describes motion without asking what causes it: position, velocity, acceleration, attitude, angular velocity and angular acceleration.

**Dynamics** relates motion to forces and moments. A complete vehicle model combines:

- kinematic equations that evolve position and attitude from velocities;
- dynamic equations that evolve translational and angular velocities from applied force and moment.

## 9. Reference frame, coordinate system and vector

A **reference frame** specifies the observer: its origin, orientation and motion. A **coordinate system** assigns numerical components within a frame, for example Cartesian or spherical coordinates.

A physical vector exists independently of coordinates. Its component array changes when the coordinate system changes.

For example,

$$
\mathbf v=v_1\mathbf e_1+v_2\mathbf e_2+v_3\mathbf e_3,
\qquad
[\mathbf v]^c=\begin{bmatrix}v_1&v_2&v_3\end{bmatrix}^T.
$$

Changing coordinates changes $[\mathbf v]^c$, not the physical vector $\mathbf v$.

An **inertial frame** is one in which Newton's laws apply without apparent forces caused by frame acceleration or rotation. Earth-fixed and body-fixed frames are generally non-inertial, although the flat-Earth approximation treats the local Earth frame as inertial.

## 10. Position, velocity and derivative notation

The notation must identify what is measured and relative to what:

- position of point $A$ relative to point $B$: $\mathbf p_{A/B}$;
- velocity of point $A$ relative to frame $F_i$: $\mathbf v_{A/i}$;
- components expressed in coordinates $c$: $[\mathbf p_{A/B}]^c$ or $[\mathbf v_{A/i}]^c$;
- a derivative observed in frame $F_b$: $\left.\dfrac{\mathrm d\mathbf v}{\mathrm dt}\right|_b$.

The distinction becomes essential when rotating frames are introduced: derivatives of the same physical vector taken by different observers are not generally equal.

### Where each label goes (L4, ll. 44–117)

The Chapter 1 notation was finished at the start of the Week 2 lecture. The rule is positional:

| Position on the symbol | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| right **subscript** | what the vector is *of*, and relative to what (point or frame) | $\mathbf p_{A/B}$: position of point $A$ relative to point $B$ (L4, ll. 61–66); $\mathbf v_{A/i}$: velocity of $A$ relative to frame $F_i$ (ll. 67–74) |
| right **superscript** | the **coordinate system** in which the components are written | $[\mathbf v_{A/i}]^{c}$ (ll. 85–88, 110–111) |
| left **superscript** | the **frame** in which the time derivative is taken | ${}^{b}\dot{\mathbf p}$: derivative observed from $F_b$ (ll. 97–112) |

So a fully labelled quantity such as ${}^{b}[\dot{\mathbf p}_{A/B}]^{c}$ answers three separate questions: *what* is measured, *who* differentiates it, and *in which axes* the numbers are written. The lecturer's reminder (l. 79): *"the reference frame is not the same thing of the coordinate system"*. Position needs a reference **point** (l. 75), velocity and acceleration need a reference **frame** (l. 67).

> [!tip] Why the left superscript is needed
> *"Depending on the reference frame that you use for the derivative, we will have different results"* (L4, ll. 94–96). This is the hook for the transport theorem in [[SESA3047 2.1 - Vector Operations and the Transport Theorem#8. The transport theorem|2.1 §8]].

## 11. Flat-Earth model

The flat-Earth approximation replaces a local region of the curved, rotating Earth by a fixed tangent plane.

Assumptions:

1. neglect Earth rotation, so the local Earth frame is treated as inertial;
2. neglect curvature and measure position in a tangent plane;
3. take gravity as constant in magnitude and normal to the plane.

It is effective for many aircraft dynamics and control problems of limited distance and duration. It becomes inadequate for long-range navigation, high-accuracy trajectories and spacecraft motion.

> [!example] Year 2 numbers: A380 check ([[SESA2027 A1 - Dynamic Systems and Aircraft Equations of Motion|SESA2027 A1]])
> In 10 s at 252 m/s an A380 covers 2.5 km. Over that distance the Earth's surface drops only 0.315 m, about 0.0126 % of the distance travelled. Treating the local plane as flat is therefore well justified for short-period dynamics. The same check also supports constant mass: about 25 kg of fuel is burned, roughly 0.004 % of MTOW.

## 12. NED and FRD coordinates

### Local North–East–Down (NED)

- $x$: north;
- $y$: east;
- $z$: down.

NED is right handed and convenient for local position, ground-relative velocity and gravity:

$$
[\mathbf g]^{NED}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\g\end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Body-fixed Forward–Right–Down (FRD)

- $x$: forward along the vehicle reference direction;
- $y$: right;
- $z$: down.

FRD is right handed and translates and rotates with the vehicle. It is convenient for aerodynamic and propulsive forces, body velocity components and angular velocity.

The vehicle attitude describes the orientation of FRD relative to NED. Direction-cosine matrices and Euler angles for that transformation are covered in [[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles]].

> [!tip] Link to SESA2027
> FRD is the SESA2027 body axis system: $x$ forward, $y$ starboard, $z$ down, with $(U,V,W)$, $(p,q,r)$ and $(L,M,N)$. The Earth-to-body transformation was introduced there as $\mathbf R_{BE}=\mathbf R_x(\phi)\mathbf R_y(\theta)\mathbf R_z(\psi)$ (yaw, then pitch, then roll). See [[Euler Angles and Rotation Matrices]]. The new notation, $[\mathbf v]^c$ and $\left.\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\right|_b$, makes explicit what SESA2027 left implicit in $\mathbf v_B=\mathbf R_{BE}\mathbf v_E$ and in the $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ term.

## 13. Model-choice checklist

Before using an equation set, state:

1. the engineering question and required outputs;
2. the plant and subsystem boundary;
3. the states, inputs, parameters and disturbances;
4. the reference frames and coordinates;
5. rigid or flexible, lumped or continuum;
6. linear or nonlinear, time invariant or time varying;
7. the operating point and validity range;
8. neglected effects and why they are negligible.
