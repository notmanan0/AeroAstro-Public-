# SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability

> [!abstract] Summary
> The free system $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x(0)=\mathbf x_0$ has the solution $\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$, where the matrix exponential is defined by its power series and is not the exponential of each entry. If $\mathbf A=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}$, the series collapses to $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0$: a sum of modes $e^{\lambda_it}$. The system is (asymptotically) stable exactly when every eigenvalue of $\mathbf A$ has a negative real part; one eigenvalue with a positive real part makes it unstable.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** given $\mathbf A$ (or a state-space model) and asked whether the system is stable, or for the free response.
> - **Assumptions:** linear, time-invariant $\mathbf A$; no input ($\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$); the diagonal form needs $\mathbf A$ diagonalisable (true when the eigenvalues are distinct).
> - **Method:** 1. Solve $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=0$; for $2\times2$, $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A)\lambda+\det\mathbf A=0$. 2. Check every $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i$: all $<0$ stable, any $>0$ unstable. 3. If some are zero, check whether they are repeated without enough eigenvectors. 4. For the response, find $\mathbf V$, then $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0$.
> - **Common mistakes:** exponentiating each entry of $\mathbf A$ (Eq. 5.43); calling a repeated eigenvalue at zero "marginally stable" without checking for a Jordan block.
> - **Practise:** [[#5. Worked example: Practice problem 5]] · [[#3. Worked example: e to the A for a 2 by 2 matrix]]

The point of putting a model in state-space form, the handout says, is what it lets us do next. For one first-order equation $\dot x=ax$ we know the answer, $x=x_0e^{at}$: it decays if $a<0$ and grows if $a>0$. The matrix version has the same shape, $\mathbf x=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$, but the matrix exponential mixes all the states together and cannot be read by eye. Diagonalising $\mathbf A$ unmixes it. In eigenvector coordinates each mode evolves on its own as $e^{\lambda_it}$, and the system is stable only if every one of them decays. The handout's analogy: *"There cannot be silence if one out of billions chooses to talk."*

## 1. Solving the free state equation (5.40–5.42)

The handout separates variables as if $\mathbf x$ were a scalar:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf x}{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\,dt,\qquad(5.40)
$$

$$
\log(\mathbf x)=\mathbf At+c,\qquad(5.41)
$$

$$
\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0.\qquad(5.42)
$$

The answer is right, but the steps are a mnemonic: you cannot divide by a vector or take its logarithm. Here is a derivation that holds for matrices. Define the matrix exponential by the same power series as the scalar one,

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf I+\mathbf At+\frac{(\mathbf At)^2}{2!}+\frac{(\mathbf At)^3}{3!}+\cdots.\qquad(5.44)
$$

$\mathbf A$ is constant, so differentiating term by term only touches the powers of $t$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf A+\mathbf A^2t+\frac{\mathbf A^3t^2}{2!}+\cdots.
$$

Factor $\mathbf A$ out on the left:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf A\Big(\mathbf I+\mathbf At+\frac{(\mathbf At)^2}{2!}+\cdots\Big)=\mathbf Ae^{\mathbf At}.
$$

So $\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$ satisfies $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf Ae^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf A\mathbf x$. At $t=0$ every term but $\mathbf I$ vanishes, so $\mathbf x(0)=\mathbf x_0$. A linear ODE with a given initial condition has only one solution, so this is it. ($e^{\mathbf At}$ is also called the state-transition matrix.)

## 2. Why diagonalise (5.43–5.47)

The matrix exponential is not the exponential of each entry (Eq. 5.43):

$$
e^{\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}}=\begin{bmatrix}51.97&74.74\\112.10&164.07\end{bmatrix}\neq\begin{bmatrix}e^1&e^2\\e^3&e^4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2.718&7.389\\20.09&54.60\end{bmatrix}.
$$

(MATLAB's `expm`, SciPy's `scipy.linalg.expm`; MATLAB's `exp` would give the wrong, element-wise answer.) The element-wise rule works only when $\mathbf A$ is diagonal, because the powers of a diagonal matrix are the powers of its entries. That is the reason to diagonalise.

Suppose $\mathbf A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, the columns of $\mathbf V$, with eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\boldsymbol\Lambda$. Then $\mathbf A\mathbf V=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda$, so

$$
\mathbf A=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}.
$$

Powers of $\mathbf A$ telescope, since each inner $\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf V$ is $\mathbf I$:

$$
\mathbf A^2=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^2\mathbf V^{-1},
$$

and by repeating, $\mathbf A^k=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^k\mathbf V^{-1}$. Substitute into the series (Eq. 5.45; the handout's second term is missing its $t$):

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf V\mathbf V^{-1}+\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}t+\frac{\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^2\mathbf V^{-1}t^2}{2!}+\frac{\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^3\mathbf V^{-1}t^3}{3!}+\cdots.
$$

Factor $\mathbf V$ out on the left and $\mathbf V^{-1}$ on the right:

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf V\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^0}{0!}+\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^1}{1!}+\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^2}{2!}+\cdots\right)\mathbf V^{-1}.\qquad(5.46)
$$

The bracket is the series for $e^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}$, and for a diagonal matrix that is $\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t},\dots,e^{\lambda_nt})$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0.}\qquad(5.47)
$$

Read from right to left: $\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf c$ expresses the initial state in eigenvector coordinates; each coordinate is multiplied by its own $e^{\lambda_it}$; $\mathbf V$ converts back. Written as a sum,

$$
\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\mathbf v_n.
$$

Each term is a mode: the eigenvector is its shape (which states move together, and in what proportion), the eigenvalue its time behaviour. For a complex pair $\lambda=\sigma\pm j\omega$ the two terms combine into a real oscillation $e^{\sigma t}(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t)$ with frequency $\omega$ and envelope $e^{\sigma t}$.

## 3. Worked example: e to the A for a 2 by 2 matrix

*(Suggested; the numbers check Eq. 5.43.)* Take $\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$ and $t=1$.

The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2-5\lambda-2=0$ (trace 5, determinant $4-6=-2$):

$$
\lambda=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{33}}{2}=5.3723,\ -0.3723.
$$

From the first row, $(1-\lambda)v_1+2v_2=0$, so $\mathbf v=[2,\ \lambda-1]^T$:

$$
\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\4.3723&-1.3723\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.11944&0.17408\\0.38056&-0.17408\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The exponentials are $e^{5.3723}=215.35$ and $e^{-0.3723}=0.68916$. Then

$$
\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}430.71&1.3783\\941.59&-0.94572\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda}\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}51.97&74.74\\112.10&164.07\end{bmatrix},
$$

which matches `expm` to every digit shown. With one positive eigenvalue this $\mathbf A$ is unstable.

## 4. Stability from the eigenvalues

From the modal sum, $\mathbf x(t)\to\mathbf 0$ for every starting point only if every $e^{\lambda_it}\to0$, that is every $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i<0$. A single $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i>0$ makes its term grow without bound from almost any start. The handout's key idea:

> A system is stable if and only if the real part of all eigenvalues in the state matrix is negative. A system that has one or more eigenvalues whose real part is greater than zero is unstable.

![[amc_eigen_stability.png|900]]

The "if and only if" is about **asymptotic** stability, decaying back to equilibrium. Eigenvalues with zero real part fall between the two sentences, and need care:

- a simple eigenvalue on the imaginary axis (or one repeated with a full set of eigenvectors) gives a mode that neither grows nor decays: $\pm2j$ in the figure oscillates for ever. This is marginal stability;
- a repeated eigenvalue without enough eigenvectors cannot be diagonalised, so Eq. 5.47 does not apply. Its Jordan block makes $e^{\mathbf At}$ contain $te^{\lambda t}$. With $\lambda=0$ that is a term growing like $t$: unstable, though slowly.

The satellite of [[SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams#The transfer function by hand|5.3]] is the second case: two zero eigenvalues, $\mathbf A$ of rank 3, one eigenvector. That is why an impulse leaves it drifting rather than at a new fixed angle, and why the handout's *"marginally stable"* for that example needs qualifying.

The poles of a transfer function are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$, as 5.3 claimed: $\mathbf C(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B$ has $\det(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)$ in its denominator, whose roots are the eigenvalues (unless a pole cancels against a zero). For the aircraft, the four eigenvalues of the longitudinal $\mathbf A$ in [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model#5. The complete model and the state matrix (5.18)|5.2]] are the short-period and phugoid pairs of [[SESA2027 A3 - Longitudinal Dynamic Modes - SPO and Phugoid|SESA2027 A3]].

## 5. Worked example: Practice problem 5

Is $\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x$ stable?

$$
\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=(\lambda-4)(\lambda-2)+5=\lambda^2-6\lambda+13=0,
$$

$$
\lambda=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-52}}{2}=3\pm2j.
$$

Both real parts are $+3>0$, so the system is **unstable**: an oscillation at 2 rad/s (period $\pi$ s) whose amplitude grows as $e^{3t}$, about twentyfold each second. A shortcut for $2\times2$: the eigenvalues have negative real parts exactly when $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A<0$ and $\det\mathbf A>0$; here the trace is $+6$, so it fails at once. This is the red curve in the figure.
