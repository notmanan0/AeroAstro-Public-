# SESA3047 5.4 - Time-Domain Solution and Stability

> [!abstract] Summary
> The free system $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x(0)=\mathbf x_0$ has the solution $\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$, where the matrix exponential is defined by its power series and is not the exponential of each entry. If $\mathbf A=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}$, the series collapses to $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0$: a sum of modes $e^{\lambda_it}$. The system is (asymptotically) stable exactly when every eigenvalue of $\mathbf A$ has a negative real part; one eigenvalue with a positive real part makes it unstable.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** given $\mathbf A$ (or a state-space model) and asked whether the system is stable, or for the free response.
> - **Assumptions:** linear, time-invariant $\mathbf A$; no input ($\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$); the diagonal form needs $\mathbf A$ diagonalisable (true when the eigenvalues are distinct).
> - **Method:** 1. Solve $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=0$; for $2\times2$, $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A)\lambda+\det\mathbf A=0$. 2. Check every $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i$: all $<0$ stable, any $>0$ unstable. 3. If some are zero, check whether they are repeated without enough eigenvectors. 4. For the response, find $\mathbf V$, then $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0$.
> - **Common mistakes:** exponentiating each entry of $\mathbf A$ (Eq. 5.43); calling a repeated eigenvalue at zero "marginally stable" without checking for a Jordan block.
> - **Practise:** [[#6. Worked example: eigenvectors of a triangular matrix]] · [[#5. Worked example: Practice problem 5]]

For one first-order equation $\dot x=ax$ the solution is $x=x_0e^{at}$: it decays if $a<0$ and grows if $a>0$. The matrix version has the same shape, $\mathbf x=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$, but the matrix exponential mixes all the states together and cannot be read by eye. Diagonalising $\mathbf A$ unmixes it. In eigenvector coordinates each mode evolves on its own as $e^{\lambda_it}$, and the system is stable only if every one of them decays. One growing mode is enough to make the whole system unstable (Chapter 5, p. 9; L16 ll. 293–300). The aircraft matrices are $4\times4$ at most, but the same analysis is applied to models with around a million states, where no one could read the behaviour off $\mathbf A$ by eye (L16 ll. 154–158).

## 1. Solving the free state equation (5.40–5.42)

With no control input, $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x$. Separating variables as if $\mathbf x$ were a scalar (L16 ll. 151–177) integrates $\int\mathbf A\,dt=\mathbf At+c$ on one side and $\int d\mathbf x/\mathbf x=\log\mathbf x$ on the other, with the constant fixed by $\mathbf x(0)=\mathbf x_0$:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf x}{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\,dt,\qquad(5.40)
$$

$$
\log(\mathbf x)=\mathbf At+c,\qquad(5.41)
$$

$$
\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0.\qquad(5.42)
$$

The order of the product matters: $e^{\mathbf At}$ is $n\times n$ and $\mathbf x_0$ is $n\times1$, so the matrix comes first; $\mathbf x_0e^{\mathbf At}$ would be an $(n\times1)(n\times n)$ product, which is not defined. The result is right, but the steps are only a mnemonic, because a vector cannot be divided or have its logarithm taken. A valid derivation defines the matrix exponential by the same power series as the scalar one,

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf I+\mathbf At+\frac{(\mathbf At)^2}{2!}+\frac{(\mathbf At)^3}{3!}+\cdots.\qquad(5.44)
$$

$\mathbf A$ is constant, so differentiating term by term only touches the powers of $t$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf A+\mathbf A^2t+\frac{\mathbf A^3t^2}{2!}+\cdots.
$$

Factor $\mathbf A$ out on the left:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf A\Big(\mathbf I+\mathbf At+\frac{(\mathbf At)^2}{2!}+\cdots\Big)=\mathbf Ae^{\mathbf At}.
$$

So $\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$ satisfies $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf Ae^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf A\mathbf x$. At $t=0$ every term but $\mathbf I$ vanishes, so $\mathbf x(0)=\mathbf x_0$. A linear ODE with a given initial condition has only one solution, so this is it. ($e^{\mathbf At}$ is also called the state-transition matrix.)

## 2. Why diagonalise (5.43–5.47)

The matrix exponential is not the exponential of each entry (Eq. 5.43):

$$
e^{\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}}=\begin{bmatrix}51.97&74.74\\112.10&164.07\end{bmatrix}\neq\begin{bmatrix}e^1&e^2\\e^3&e^4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2.718&7.389\\20.09&54.60\end{bmatrix}.
$$

(MATLAB's `expm`, SciPy's `scipy.linalg.expm`; MATLAB's `exp` would give the wrong, element-wise answer.) Exponentiating the entries gives the matrix exponential only for the diagonal entries of a diagonal matrix (the identity being one example), whose off-diagonal zeros stay zero instead of becoming $e^0=1$; this works because the powers of a diagonal matrix are the powers of its entries (L16 ll. 180–197). That is the reason to diagonalise, using the eigenvalue decomposition ([[Eigenvalue Decomposition]]). It exists only for a square, $n\times n$ matrix (L16 ll. 204–206), which $\mathbf A$ always is, and it also needs $n$ independent eigenvectors.

Suppose $\mathbf A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, the columns of $\mathbf V$, with eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\boldsymbol\Lambda$. Then $\mathbf A\mathbf V=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda$, so

$$
\mathbf A=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}.
$$

Powers of $\mathbf A$ telescope, since each inner $\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf V$ is $\mathbf I$:

$$
\mathbf A^2=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^2\mathbf V^{-1},
$$

and by repeating, $\mathbf A^k=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^k\mathbf V^{-1}$. Substitute into the series (Eq. 5.45):

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf V\mathbf V^{-1}+\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}t+\frac{\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^2\mathbf V^{-1}t^2}{2!}+\frac{\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda^3\mathbf V^{-1}t^3}{3!}+\cdots.
$$

The first term is $\mathbf V\mathbf V^{-1}=\mathbf I$, which is the $\boldsymbol\Lambda^0t^0/0!$ term written out. Factor $\mathbf V$ out on the left and $\mathbf V^{-1}$ on the right (L16 ll. 227–266):

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf V\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^0}{0!}+\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^1}{1!}+\frac{(\boldsymbol\Lambda t)^2}{2!}+\cdots\right)\mathbf V^{-1}.\qquad(5.46)
$$

The bracket is the series for $e^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}$, and for a diagonal matrix that is $\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t},\dots,e^{\lambda_nt})$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda t}\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0.}\qquad(5.47)
$$

Read from right to left: $\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf c$ expresses the initial state in eigenvector coordinates; each coordinate is multiplied by its own $e^{\lambda_it}$; $\mathbf V$ converts back. Written as a sum,

$$
\mathbf x(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}\mathbf v_n.
$$

Each term is a mode: the eigenvector is its shape (which states move together, and in what proportion), the eigenvalue its time behaviour. For a complex pair $\lambda=\sigma\pm j\omega$ the two terms combine into a real oscillation $e^{\sigma t}(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t)$ with frequency $\omega$ and envelope $e^{\sigma t}$. So the imaginary part of an eigenvalue sets how fast its mode oscillates, and the real part alone decides whether it grows or decays (L16 ll. 274–287). For an aircraft these modes are the phugoid, short-period, Dutch roll, roll subsidence and spiral modes, and their eigenvectors show which states take part in each (L16 ll. 349–351).

## 3. Worked example: e to the A for a 2 by 2 matrix

*(Suggested; the numbers check Eq. 5.43.)* Take $\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$ and $t=1$.

The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2-5\lambda-2=0$ (trace 5, determinant $4-6=-2$):

$$
\lambda=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{33}}{2}=5.3723,\ -0.3723.
$$

From the first row, $(1-\lambda)v_1+2v_2=0$, so $\mathbf v=[2,\ \lambda-1]^T$:

$$
\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\4.3723&-1.3723\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.11944&0.17408\\0.38056&-0.17408\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The exponentials are $e^{5.3723}=215.35$ and $e^{-0.3723}=0.68916$. Then

$$
\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}430.71&1.3783\\941.59&-0.94572\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\mathbf Ve^{\boldsymbol\Lambda}\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}51.97&74.74\\112.10&164.07\end{bmatrix},
$$

which matches `expm` to every digit shown. With one positive eigenvalue this $\mathbf A$ is unstable.

## 4. Stability from the eigenvalues

From the modal sum, $\mathbf x(t)\to\mathbf 0$ for every starting point only if every $e^{\lambda_it}\to0$, that is every $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i<0$. A single $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i>0$ makes its term grow without bound from almost any start. A system is therefore stable if and only if the real parts of all eigenvalues of the state matrix are negative, and unstable if one or more eigenvalues have a real part greater than zero (Chapter 5, p. 9).

![[amc_eigen_stability.png|900]]

The "if and only if" is about **asymptotic** stability, decaying back to equilibrium. Eigenvalues with zero real part fall between the two cases and need care:

- a simple eigenvalue on the imaginary axis (or one repeated with a full set of eigenvectors) gives a mode that neither grows nor decays: $\pm2j$ in the figure oscillates for ever. This is marginal stability;
- a repeated eigenvalue without enough eigenvectors cannot be diagonalised, so Eq. 5.47 does not apply. Its Jordan block makes $e^{\mathbf At}$ contain $te^{\lambda t}$. With $\lambda=0$ that is a term growing like $t$: unstable, though slowly.

The satellite of [[SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams#The transfer function by hand|5.3]] is the second case: two zero eigenvalues, $\mathbf A$ of rank 3, one eigenvector. That is why an impulse leaves it drifting rather than at a new fixed angle, and why it is not marginally stable.

The poles of a transfer function are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A$ ([[SESA3047 5.3 - Building State-Space Models from ODEs, Transfer Functions and Block Diagrams|5.3]]), so the Year 2 rule that a pole in the right half-plane means instability is the same rule as this one (L16 ll. 301–311): $\mathbf C(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B$ has $\det(s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)$ in its denominator, whose roots are the eigenvalues (unless a pole cancels against a zero). For the aircraft, the four eigenvalues of the longitudinal $\mathbf A$ in [[SESA3047 5.2 - Longitudinal State-Space Model#5. The complete model and the state matrix (5.18)|5.2]] are the short-period and phugoid pairs of [[SESA2027 A3 - Longitudinal Dynamic Modes - SPO and Phugoid|SESA2027 A3]].

## 5. Worked example: Practice problem 5

Is $\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x$ stable?

$$
\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=(\lambda-4)(\lambda-2)+5=\lambda^2-6\lambda+13=0,
$$

$$
\lambda=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-52}}{2}=3\pm2j.
$$

Both real parts are $+3>0$, so the system is **unstable**: an oscillation at 2 rad/s (period $\pi$ s) whose amplitude grows as $e^{3t}$, about twentyfold each second. A shortcut for $2\times2$: the eigenvalues have negative real parts exactly when $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf A<0$ and $\det\mathbf A>0$; here the trace is $+6$, so it fails at once. This is the red curve in the figure.

## 6. Worked example: eigenvectors of a triangular matrix

Take

$$
\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}
$$

and write it as $\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}$ (L16 ll. 316–413; computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors like this is exam material, l. 384).

### Eigenvalues

The eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I)=0$. For a $2\times2$ matrix the determinant is $ad-bc$:

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix}3-\lambda&2\\0&-1-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)-2\cdot0=0.
$$

The $2\cdot0$ term vanishes, leaving

$$
(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)=0,
$$

so

$$
\lambda_1=3,\qquad\lambda_2=-1.
$$

These are the diagonal entries of $\mathbf A$, as they are for any triangular matrix. $\mathbf A$ is upper triangular: every entry below the main diagonal is zero (the entries above it can be anything). For any triangular matrix, upper or lower, $\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I$ is still triangular, and the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (expand along the first column, or row, repeatedly). So $\det(\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I)=\prod_i(a_{ii}-\lambda)$, and the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries (L16 ll. 339–345, 419–421). The rule needs care in two ways. It is the main diagonal, top-left to bottom-right; entries on the other diagonal are not eigenvalues (L16 ll. 423–424). And it does not hold for a full matrix: $[1\ 2;\ 3\ 4]$ has diagonal entries 1 and 4 but eigenvalues 5.372 and $-0.372$ (§3).

### Eigenvectors

An eigenvector satisfies $(\mathbf A-\lambda\mathbf I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0$: multiplying it by $\mathbf A$ only stretches it by $\lambda$ (L16 ll. 352–361).

For $\lambda_1=3$:

$$
\mathbf A-3\mathbf I=\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\0&-4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The two rows give

$$
0\cdot v_1+2v_2=0,\qquad0\cdot v_1-4v_2=0,
$$

so $v_2=0$, and nothing constrains $v_1$. Choose $v_1=1$:

$$
\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check: $\mathbf A\mathbf v_1=[3\cdot1+2\cdot0,\ 0\cdot1-1\cdot0]^T=[3,0]^T=3\mathbf v_1$.

For $\lambda_2=-1$:

$$
\mathbf A+\mathbf I=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The second row is all zeros and gives no condition; the first gives

$$
4v_1+2v_2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad v_2=-2v_1.
$$

Choose $v_1=1$, so $v_2=-2$:

$$
\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check: $\mathbf A\mathbf v_2=[3\cdot1+2\cdot(-2),\ 0\cdot1-1\cdot(-2)]^T=[-1,2]^T=-\mathbf v_2$ (L16 ll. 401–405).

![[amc_eigvec_example.png|900]]

The left panel shows what the two checks mean geometrically: $\mathbf A$ stretches $\mathbf v_1$ to three times its length and flips $\mathbf v_2$ to point the other way, each staying on its own line, while a vector such as $[1,1]^T$, which is not an eigenvector, is turned onto a new direction, $[5,-1]^T$.

### Assembling the decomposition

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of $\mathbf V$, in the same order as the eigenvalues in $\boldsymbol\Lambda$:

$$
\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&-2\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix swaps the diagonal, negates the off-diagonal and divides by the determinant, here $\det\mathbf V=1\cdot(-2)-1\cdot0=-2$:

$$
\mathbf V^{-1}=\frac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix}-2&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\tfrac12\\0&-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check by multiplying back. First

$$
\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\0&2\end{bmatrix},
$$

then

$$
\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}3\cdot1+(-1)\cdot0&3\cdot\tfrac12+(-1)(-\tfrac12)\\0&2\cdot(-\tfrac12)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf A.
$$

One eigenvalue, $\lambda_1=3$, has a positive real part, so the system $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x$ is unstable (L16 ll. 412–413).

### The free response

Eq. 5.47 now gives the solution in closed form:

$$
e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf V\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&0\\0&e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf V^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&\tfrac12(e^{3t}-e^{-t})\\0&e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

*(Suggested extension; numbers checked with `scipy.linalg.expm`.)* Start from $\mathbf x_0=[0,1]^T$. Its eigenvector coordinates are

$$
\mathbf c=\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf x_0=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12\\-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix},
$$

so

$$
\mathbf x(t)=\tfrac12e^{3t}\mathbf v_1-\tfrac12e^{-t}\mathbf v_2=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12(e^{3t}-e^{-t})\\e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $t=1$ s this is $[9.859,\ 0.368]^T$. The $\mathbf v_2$ part dies away and the $\mathbf v_1$ part takes over, so the state swings onto the $x_1$ axis and runs off along it: the red curve in the right panel. Only a start exactly on the $\mathbf v_2$ line, where $c_1=0$, decays to the origin; any start off that line, however close, eventually grows. That is the sense in which one unstable eigenvalue makes the whole system unstable.

### Scaling of eigenvectors

An eigenvector is defined only up to a non-zero factor, so the leading 1 is a choice, not a rule (L16 ll. 432–442). MATLAB and Python normalise each eigenvector to unit length: $\mathbf v_1=[1,0]^T$ already has length 1, and $\mathbf v_2=[1,-2]^T$, of length $\sqrt5$, becomes $[1/\sqrt5,-2/\sqrt5]^T=[0.447,-0.894]^T$. The sign is arbitrary too; NumPy returns $[-0.447,0.894]^T$. A different scaling changes $\mathbf V$ and $\mathbf V^{-1}$ but not the product $\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^{-1}$, and not the response.
