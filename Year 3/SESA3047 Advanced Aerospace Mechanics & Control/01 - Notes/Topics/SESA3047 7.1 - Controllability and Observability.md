# SESA3047 7.1 - Controllability and Observability

> [!abstract] Summary
> With the control matrix $\mathbf B$ and the output matrix $\mathbf C$ added, the state-space model $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$, $\mathbf y=\mathbf C\mathbf x$ raises two questions: can the inputs drive the state anywhere, and can the outputs reveal the whole state? A system with $n$ states is fully controllable if the controllability matrix $\mathcal C=[\mathbf B\ \ \mathbf A\mathbf B\ \cdots\ \mathbf A^{n-1}\mathbf B]$ has rank $n$, and fully observable if the observability matrix $\mathcal O=[\mathbf C;\ \mathbf C\mathbf A;\ \cdots;\ \mathbf C\mathbf A^{n-1}]$ has rank $n$. Full controllability is what allows the closed-loop poles to be placed anywhere ([[SESA3047 7.2 - Pole Placement|7.2]]).

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** asked whether a state-space system is controllable or observable, to assemble $\mathbf B$ for an aircraft, or to explain what rank means.
> - **Assumptions:** linear time-invariant model $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$, $\mathbf y=\mathbf C\mathbf x$ about an equilibrium; $n$ is the number of states; $\mathbf A$ is the state matrix after $\mathbf M^{-1}$ has been applied (and $\mathbf B=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf B'$).
> - **Method:** 1. Check the sizes: $\mathbf A$ is $n\times n$, $\mathbf B$ is $n\times m$, $\mathbf C$ is $p\times n$. 2. Form $\mathbf A\mathbf B,\mathbf A^2\mathbf B,\dots$ up to $\mathbf A^{n-1}\mathbf B$ and place them side by side. 3. For a square $\mathcal C$, rank $n$ if and only if $\det\mathcal C\neq0$; otherwise count independent columns (or rows). 4. For observability stack $\mathbf C,\mathbf C\mathbf A,\dots,\mathbf C\mathbf A^{n-1}$ and do the same. 5. State the conclusion: rank $n$ means fully controllable (observable); rank $<n$ means some state direction cannot be reached (seen).
> - **Common mistakes:** stopping at $\mathbf A\mathbf B$ for a three-state system (the matrix needs $n$ blocks); reading too few inputs or sensors as proof of uncontrollability or unobservability (one input or one sensor is often enough).
> - **Practise:** [[#Worked example: the Eq. 7.11 system]] · [[#Practice problem 2: observability of a phugoid model]] · [[SESA3047 Cambridge Solutions - 7 Controllability, Observability and Pole Placement#3F2 2009 Q4 - Spacecraft rendezvous and thruster failure|Cambridge 3F2 2009 Q4]]

Chapters 5 and 6 were about the state matrix $\mathbf A$: its eigenvalues give the modes, and its eigenvectors give their shapes. Chapter 7 brings in the other two matrices of [[SESA3047 5.1 - State-Space Form and Linearisation|5.1]]. The control matrix $\mathbf B$ says how the actuators (elevator, throttle, ailerons, rudder) and disturbances such as gusts push on the states, and the output matrix $\mathbf C$ says which combinations of the states the sensors measure.

Two properties follow, and neither is specific to aircraft. A system is **controllable** if the inputs can move the state from any initial state to any final state in a finite time: can a spacecraft with a limited number of thrusters be manoeuvred in every direction? It is **observable** if the internal state can be worked out from the outputs over a finite time: do the sensors fitted carry enough information to reconstruct all the states? Both are answered by the rank of a matrix built from $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$, or from $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf C$.

Controllability matters because it is the condition under which feedback can put the closed-loop poles wherever they are wanted. That is a much stronger statement than root locus, in [[SESA2027 B2 - Root Locus Method|SESA2027 B2]], suggests: there, varying one gain moved the closed-loop poles along fairly narrow paths in the complex plane. The difference is that proportional control of the whole state, $\mathbf u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x$, has one gain per state ([[SESA3047 7.2 - Pole Placement#4. One gain against many: root locus and pole placement|7.2 §4]]).

## 1. Inputs to the aircraft model (7.4–7.6)

The longitudinal and lateral models of Chapters 5–6 were free responses, $\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A'\mathbf x$. The actuators and gusts add forcing terms. For the longitudinal motion, the controls act on the axial force $X$, the normal force $Z$ and the pitching moment $M$. The change in axial force from the inputs is a sum of one term per input (Eq. 7.4):

$$
\Delta X_c=\mathring X_\eta\Delta\eta+\mathring X_T\Delta T+\mathring X_uu_g+\mathring X_ww_g,\qquad(7.4)
$$

where $\Delta\eta$ is the change in elevator angle, $\Delta T$ the change in throttle, and $u_g$, $w_g$ the horizontal and vertical gust velocities. The gust terms reuse the speed derivatives $\mathring X_u$ and $\mathring X_w$: a gust changes the relative wind exactly as a change in the aircraft's own velocity does. With the plus signs of Eq. 7.4, $u_g$ and $w_g$ are defined as the gust's contribution to the relative wind seen by the aircraft (a head-on gust is a positive $u_g$). The same holds for $Z$ and $M$, and the four input columns form $\mathbf B'$ (Eq. 7.5):

$$
\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A'\mathbf x+\mathbf B'\mathbf u,\qquad
\mathbf B'=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring X_\eta&\mathring X_T&\mathring X_u&\mathring X_w\\\mathring Z_\eta&\mathring Z_T&\mathring Z_u&\mathring Z_w\\\mathring M_\eta&\mathring M_T&\mathring M_u&\mathring M_w\\0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\eta\\\Delta T\\u_g\\w_g\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.5)
$$

The last row is zero because the fourth equation is the kinematic relation $\dot\theta=q$, on which no input acts. Multiplying through by $\mathbf M^{-1}$ gives the standard form $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf A\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf u$ with $\mathbf B=\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf B'$.

For the lateral motion there are three inputs: the side gust $v_g$, the aileron deflection $\delta_A$ and the rudder deflection $\delta_R$ (Eq. 7.6):

$$
\mathbf B'=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring Y_v&\mathring Y_{\delta_A}&\mathring Y_{\delta_R}\\\mathring L_v&\mathring L_{\delta_A}&\mathring L_{\delta_R}\\\mathring N_v&\mathring N_{\delta_A}&\mathring N_{\delta_R}\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf u=\begin{bmatrix}v_g\\\delta_A\\\delta_R\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.6)
$$

Each $\delta$ is a deviation from the trim setting of that control surface, and in this module the trim setting is taken as zero. $\mathbf B'$ here is $4\times3$, three inputs for four states. Having fewer inputs than states does not make a system uncontrollable; only the rank test of §3 can decide.

Gusts are listed with the controls because they enter the equations in the same way, through $\mathbf B$. They are inputs the controller cannot choose, though: disturbances. In the controllability test only the columns of $\mathbf B$ that the controller commands count.

## 2. Rank (7.2–7.3)

The **rank** of a matrix is the number of its linearly independent columns. A set of columns is linearly independent if none of them can be written as a linear combination of the others. For $\mathbf M\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$, a real matrix with $p$ rows and $q$ columns, the rank can be at most $\min(p,q)$: the columns are vectors in a $p$-dimensional space, and there cannot be more than $p$ independent vectors in it. The number of independent rows always equals the number of independent columns, so the rank can be counted either way ([[Rank and Consistency of Linear Systems]]).

Three examples (Eq. 7.2):

$$
\mathbf M_1=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2&-1\\0&5&-2&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf M_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&4&5\\0&0&6\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf M_3=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.2)
$$

Before looking at any entries, the maximum ranks are 2 for $\mathbf M_1$ (two rows) and 3 for $\mathbf M_2$ and $\mathbf M_3$ ($3\times3$).

$\mathbf M_1$ has rank 2. Its first two columns are independent, since no multiple of $(1,0)$ gives $(0,5)$, and two independent vectors fill the plane, so the other two columns must be combinations of them (Eq. 7.3):

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2\\-2\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}-0.4\begin{bmatrix}0\\5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\begin{bmatrix}-1\\3\end{bmatrix}=-1\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}+0.6\begin{bmatrix}0\\5\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.3)
$$

The coefficients come from matching components: the first component of column 3 is $2$, which fixes the coefficient of $(1,0)$ at 2, and the second is $-2=5c$, so $c=-0.4$.

$\mathbf M_2$ has rank 3. It is upper triangular, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal, $1\times4\times6=24\neq0$, and a square matrix with a non-zero determinant has independent columns. All three rows are independent too, as they must be.

$\mathbf M_3$ has rank 2. The third column is the sum of the first two, and the third row is all zeros, which is a combination of the other rows with zero coefficients. All three columns lie in the plane $z=0$.

![[amc_ch7_rank_geometry.png|900]]

For a square matrix, the quickest test is the determinant: full rank if and only if $\det\neq0$. For a non-square one, count independent columns or row-reduce.

## 3. Controllability (7.1)

### The test

Assemble the **controllability matrix** from $\mathbf A$ ($n\times n$) and $\mathbf B$ ($n\times m$) (Eq. 7.1):

$$
\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf B&\mathbf A\mathbf B&\mathbf A^2\mathbf B&\cdots&\mathbf A^{n-1}\mathbf B\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.1)
$$

It is $n\times nm$. If $\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal C)=n$, the system is fully controllable. For a single input ($m=1$) $\mathcal C$ is square, and the test is $\det\mathcal C\neq0$.

### Why the rank of this matrix decides it

The state at time $t$ is the free response plus the forced response ([[Matrix Exponential and Modal Solution]]):

$$
\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0+\int_0^te^{\mathbf A(t-\tau)}\mathbf B\mathbf u(\tau)\,d\tau .
$$

The inputs can only move the state through the integral. Two steps show that this integral always lies in the column space of $\mathcal C$, and that it can fill all of it.

First, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem says that a matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. If $\det(\lambda\mathbf I-\mathbf A)=\lambda^n+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\dots+a_0$, then

$$
\mathbf A^n=-a_{n-1}\mathbf A^{n-1}-\dots-a_1\mathbf A-a_0\mathbf I .
$$

So $\mathbf A^n$ is a combination of $\mathbf I,\mathbf A,\dots,\mathbf A^{n-1}$, and multiplying by $\mathbf A$ repeatedly shows the same for every higher power. The series $e^{\mathbf As}=\sum_k\mathbf A^ks^k/k!$ can therefore be regrouped as

$$
e^{\mathbf As}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha_k(s)\,\mathbf A^k
$$

for some scalar functions $\alpha_k(s)$. Substituting into the forced response, with $s=t-\tau$:

$$
\int_0^te^{\mathbf A(t-\tau)}\mathbf B\mathbf u(\tau)\,d\tau=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf A^k\mathbf B\int_0^t\alpha_k(t-\tau)\,\mathbf u(\tau)\,d\tau .
$$

Every term is a column of some $\mathbf A^k\mathbf B$ times a number, so the input can only push the state along the column space of $\mathcal C$. If $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal C<n$, there is a direction it cannot push along at all: a non-zero row vector $\mathbf w^T$ with $\mathbf w^T\mathcal C=\mathbf 0$, for which

$$
\mathbf w^T\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf w^Te^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0
$$

whatever the input does. The combination $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x$ evolves as if there were no control.

Second, if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal C=n$, every state can be reached. Define the controllability Gramian

$$
\mathbf W(t_f)=\int_0^{t_f}e^{\mathbf A\tau}\mathbf B\mathbf B^Te^{\mathbf A^T\tau}\,d\tau .
$$

Suppose $\mathbf W$ were singular. Then some $\mathbf w\neq\mathbf 0$ has $\mathbf w^T\mathbf W\mathbf w=0$, that is

$$
\int_0^{t_f}\left\|\mathbf w^Te^{\mathbf A\tau}\mathbf B\right\|^2d\tau=0,
$$

so $\mathbf w^Te^{\mathbf A\tau}\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ for every $\tau$. Differentiating $k$ times and setting $\tau=0$ gives $\mathbf w^T\mathbf A^k\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ for $k=0,\dots,n-1$, so $\mathbf w^T\mathcal C=\mathbf 0$, contradicting rank $n$. So $\mathbf W$ is invertible, and the input

$$
\mathbf u(\tau)=\mathbf B^Te^{\mathbf A^T(t_f-\tau)}\,\mathbf W(t_f)^{-1}\left(\mathbf x_f-e^{\mathbf At_f}\mathbf x_0\right)
$$

drives the state from $\mathbf x_0$ to any $\mathbf x_f$ in any finite time $t_f$. Substituting it into the forced response (with $\sigma=t_f-\tau$) gives exactly $\mathbf W(t_f)\mathbf W(t_f)^{-1}(\mathbf x_f-e^{\mathbf At_f}\mathbf x_0)$, which is the required change of state. The Gramian is not in the course; it is here to show that rank $n$ is enough, not only necessary.

### What an uncontrollable system looks like

The simplest example is two decoupled states with one actuator acting on the first:

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}u,\qquad\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{rank}\mathcal C=1 .
$$

The second state obeys $\dot x_2=2x_2$ whatever $u$ does, and it is unstable. No feedback $u=-\mathbf K\mathbf x$ can change that: the closed-loop matrix keeps a zero in its (2,1) position, so it remains upper triangular with $2$ on the diagonal, and the eigenvalue $+2$ stays put for every $\mathbf K$. An uncontrollable mode is one that cannot be moved.

### Worked example: the Eq. 7.11 system

For

$$
\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},
$$

$n=2$, so $\mathcal C=[\mathbf B\ \ \mathbf A\mathbf B]$. The second column is

$$
\mathbf A\mathbf B=\begin{bmatrix}0\cdot0+1\cdot1\\-2\cdot0-3\cdot1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-3\end{bmatrix},
$$

so (Eq. 7.12)

$$
\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&-3\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det\mathcal C=0\cdot(-3)-1\cdot1=-1\neq0 .
$$

The rank is 2 and the system is fully controllable. The single input reaches $x_1$ through $x_2$: it drives $\dot x_2$ directly, and $x_2$ drives $\dot x_1$.

### Worked example: one actuator for the Navion's lateral motion

Each lateral control surface of the Navion of [[SESA3047 6.3 - Lateral Modes and their Approximations|6.3]] is enough on its own. The control derivatives of Table 6.2 are per radian and are made dimensional with $\tfrac12\rho U_0^2S=30\,242$ N (side force) and $\tfrac12\rho U_0^2Sb=307\,860$ N m (moments). After dividing by $m$, $J_{xx}$ and $J_{zz}$,

$$
\mathbf B=\begin{bmatrix}0&3.783\\-29.29&2.557\\-0.2225&-4.611\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad(\text{columns: }\delta_A,\ \delta_R).
$$

With the $\mathbf A$ of 6.3 §3, the $4\times4$ controllability matrix built from the aileron column alone has rank 4, and so does the one built from the rudder column alone. The aileron rolls the aircraft directly, the roll couples into yaw and sideslip through $\mathring N_p$ and gravity, and so every state is reached. The rank says nothing about how hard it is: the aileron's $\mathcal C$ has a condition number of about 7000, so some state directions need large deflections. The Boeing 747 longitudinal model of Lab 2 Task 3, with the elevator alone, also has a full-rank (5) controllability matrix, which is why pole placement works there.

## 4. Observability (7.7–7.9)

### What the sensors measure

The longitudinal and lateral state vectors are (Eq. 7.7)

$$
\mathbf x_\text{long}=\begin{bmatrix}u&w&q&\theta\end{bmatrix}^T,\qquad\mathbf x_\text{lat}=\begin{bmatrix}v&p&r&\phi\end{bmatrix}^T.\qquad(7.7)
$$

Gyroscopes measure the body rates $p$, $q$ and $r$ directly. The translational velocities and the attitude angles are not measured directly; they are inferred by combining air data, inertial and GPS measurements. Air data give the airspeed $V$, the angle of attack $\alpha$ and the sideslip angle $\beta$, from which the body-axis velocities follow (Eq. 7.8). With $\alpha$ defined by $\tan\alpha=w/u$ and $\beta$ by $\sin\beta=v/V$, the sideslip component is

$$
v=V\sin\beta,
$$

and the remaining speed in the $xz$-plane is $\sqrt{u^2+w^2}=\sqrt{V^2-v^2}=V\cos\beta$, split by $\alpha$:

$$
u=V\cos\alpha\cos\beta,\qquad v=V\sin\beta,\qquad w=V\sin\alpha\cos\beta.\qquad(7.8)
$$

As a check, $u^2+v^2+w^2=V^2\cos^2\beta(\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha)+V^2\sin^2\beta=V^2$. The attitude angles $\phi$ and $\theta$ are estimated by combining integrated gyro rates with accelerometers and other attitude references ([[SESA2027 C1 - Sensing Systems, Sensor Principles and Sensor Fusion|SESA2027 C1]]).

Few systems have every state measured. The question is whether the measurements available contain enough information to reconstruct all the states that describe the dynamics.

### The test

Assemble the **observability matrix** from $\mathbf A$ and the output matrix $\mathbf C$ ($p\times n$) (Eq. 7.9):

$$
\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf C\\\mathbf C\mathbf A\\\mathbf C\mathbf A^2\\\vdots\\\mathbf C\mathbf A^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.\qquad(7.9)
$$

It is $np\times n$. If $\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal O)=n$, the system is fully observable: every state trajectory can be told apart from every other by its outputs alone.

### Why this matrix

Take the free response, $\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf x(t)=e^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0$ and the output is

$$
\mathbf y(t)=\mathbf Ce^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0 .
$$

Differentiate repeatedly and evaluate at $t=0$, using $\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf At}=\mathbf Ae^{\mathbf At}$:

$$
\mathbf y(0)=\mathbf C\mathbf x_0,\qquad\dot{\mathbf y}(0)=\mathbf C\mathbf A\mathbf x_0,\qquad\ddot{\mathbf y}(0)=\mathbf C\mathbf A^2\mathbf x_0,\qquad\dots
$$

Stack the first $n$ of them:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf y(0)\\\dot{\mathbf y}(0)\\\vdots\\\mathbf y^{(n-1)}(0)\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal O\,\mathbf x_0 .
$$

The left-hand side is known from the measurements. If $\mathcal O$ has rank $n$, this linear system has exactly one solution $\mathbf x_0$, and with $\mathbf x_0$ known the whole trajectory follows. Higher derivatives add nothing, because by Cayley–Hamilton $\mathbf C\mathbf A^n$ is a combination of the rows already in $\mathcal O$. If the rank is less than $n$, some $\mathbf x_0\neq\mathbf 0$ has $\mathcal O\mathbf x_0=\mathbf 0$, and then $\mathbf y(t)=\mathbf Ce^{\mathbf At}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf 0$ for all $t$: that initial state is invisible. With inputs the argument is the same after subtracting the known forced response from $\mathbf y$.

Observability is the mirror image of controllability: $(\mathbf A,\mathbf C)$ is observable exactly when $(\mathbf A^T,\mathbf C^T)$ is controllable, since $\mathcal O^T=[\mathbf C^T\ \ \mathbf A^T\mathbf C^T\ \cdots]$ is the controllability matrix of the transposed system.

The rest of the module assumes the system is fully observable, and in the exam you may be asked to decide whether a system in state-space form is. When it is fully observable but the states are not measured directly, an **observer**, such as a Kalman filter, reconstructs them from the outputs; observers belong to FEEG6043 Intelligent Mobile Robotics.

### Practice problem 2: observability of a phugoid model

The system (Eq. 7.20) has

$$
\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&-10\\3&4&0\\5&6&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf C=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\end{bmatrix},
$$

with states $[u,w,\theta]^T$, so only $\theta$ is measured. Build $\mathcal O$ row by row:

$$
\mathbf C\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}5&6&0\end{bmatrix},
$$

the third row of $\mathbf A$, and

$$
\mathbf C\mathbf A^2=\begin{bmatrix}5&6&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}5+18&10+24&-50+0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}23&34&-50\end{bmatrix}.
$$

So

$$
\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\5&6&0\\23&34&-50\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Expand along the first row, whose only non-zero entry is the 1 at position (1,3), sign $+$:

$$
\det\mathcal O=1\cdot\begin{vmatrix}5&6\\23&34\end{vmatrix}=5\times34-6\times23=170-138=32\neq0 .
$$

The rank is 3 and the system is fully observable: measuring the pitch attitude alone is enough to reconstruct $u$ and $w$, because $\theta$ is driven by both of them through the third row of $\mathbf A$.

### Worked example: a sensor that sees too little

For the linearised system of [[SESA3047 7.2 - Pole Placement#Practice problem 3: linearise, check, place|7.2 practice problem 3]], $\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-2&0\\0&0&1\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}$. Measuring $z$ alone, $\mathbf C=[0\ 0\ 1]$:

$$
\mathbf C\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf C\mathbf A^2=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\end{bmatrix},
$$

so all three rows of $\mathcal O$ are multiples of $[0\ 0\ 1]$ and the rank is 1. The equation $\dot z=-z+u$ involves neither $x$ nor $y$, so the measurement of $z$ carries no information about them. Measuring $x$ instead, $\mathbf C=[1\ 0\ 0]$, gives rows $[1,0,0]$, $[-1,-2,0]$ and $[1,2,-2]$, with determinant $1\times\big((-2)(-2)-0\big)=4$: fully observable, because $x$ is driven by $y$ and $y$ by $z$. For the Navion's lateral model, any one of $v$, $p$, $r$ or $\phi$ measured alone gives an observability matrix of rank 4.
