# SESA3047 3.1 - Rotational Kinematics and Euler-Angle Rates

> [!abstract] Summary
> The body's angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}$ has body-axis components $[p,q,r]^T$, which is what gyros measure. Its attitude is described by the 3-2-1 Euler angles $\boldsymbol\Phi=[\phi,\theta,\psi]^T$. The Euler-angle rates $\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}$ are **not** the components of $\boldsymbol\omega$: each rate spins about the axis of its own rotation, and those three axes are different and generally not orthogonal (yaw about the NED down axis, pitch about the once-rotated $y_1$, roll about the final body $x_b$). Adding the three contributions in FRD gives
> $$\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}=\mathbf E(\boldsymbol\Phi)\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi},\qquad\mathbf E=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-s_\theta\\0&c_\phi&s_\phi c_\theta\\0&-s_\phi&c_\phi c_\theta\end{bmatrix}.$$
> Inverting it gives the **rotational kinematic equation** $\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}=\mathbf H(\boldsymbol\Phi)\,\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}$, which propagates attitude from gyro readings. $\det\mathbf E=\cos\theta$, so $\mathbf H$ blows up at $\theta=\pm90^\circ$: this is gimbal lock seen from the rates side. At small angles $\mathbf H\approx\mathbf I$, so $\dot\phi\approx p$, $\dot\theta\approx q$, $\dot\psi\approx r$. That approximation is fine for small-disturbance models, but not in general.

## The topic in one physical picture

Strap a three-axis rate gyro to an aircraft. It reports $p$, $q$ and $r$: how fast the airframe is spinning about its own nose, wing and belly axes. Now ask the question an attitude display needs answered: **how fast are the heading, pitch and bank angles changing?**

It is tempting to say $\dot\psi=r$, $\dot\theta=q$, $\dot\phi=p$. Try a steady level turn with 30° of bank:

- the heading changes steadily, but the pitch and bank angles stay constant;
- yet the gyro reports a **pitch rate** $q\neq0$ and a reduced yaw rate $r$. The aircraft's "down" axis is tilted by the bank, so the turn about the vertical shows up partly about the body $y$ axis and partly about the body $z$ axis.

The three Euler angles are angles about three **different** axes in a chain: yaw about the vertical, then pitch about a level wing axis, then roll about the nose. Their rates are therefore three spin vectors along three different directions. To compare them with $p,q,r$ you have to resolve all three into body axes. That bookkeeping is the whole of §3.1.

## 1. Frames, assumptions and the body rates (§3.1.1, L7 ll. 125–132)

**Assumptions** (the [[Flat-Earth Model]] of Chapter 1, L7 ll. 125–127):

- $F_r$ is the local reference frame, tangent to the Earth's surface, with **NED** coordinates;
- the Earth's curvature and rotation are neglected, so $F_r$ is treated as **inertial**;
- $F_b$ is the body-fixed frame, with **FRD** coordinates.

**Body angular velocity** (Eq. 3.1):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}=\begin{bmatrix}p&q&r\end{bmatrix}^T.
$$

- $p$: roll rate, about the body forward axis $x_b$;
- $q$: pitch rate, about the body right axis $y_b$;
- $r$: yaw rate, about the body down axis $z_b$.

These are the components of **one physical vector**, all resolved along the same, final, body axes.

## 2. Euler angles and their rates (§3.1.1, L7 ll. 133–159)

From Chapter 2, the 3-2-1 transformation from NED to FRD is (Eq. 3.2)

$$
\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}=\mathbf C_x(\phi)\,\mathbf C_y(\theta)\,\mathbf C_z(\psi),
$$

with the angles collected in a column matrix and its derivative (Eqs. 3.3–3.4):

$$
\boldsymbol\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\phi&\theta&\psi\end{bmatrix}^T,\qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi&\dot\theta&\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}^T.
$$

> [!important] Key idea (notes, p. 2)
> $\dot\phi$, $\dot\theta$ and $\dot\psi$ are the rates of three **successive** rotations from NED to FRD. $p$, $q$ and $r$ are the components of a **single** angular-velocity vector in FRD. Because the second and third rotation axes have already been moved by the earlier rotations, $\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}$ is **not** a vector expressed in any one coordinate system. It is just a column of three numbers.

> [!warning] Notation trap
> $\boldsymbol\Phi$ is a column matrix of angles, not a vector. Finite rotations do not add like vectors ([[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#Non-commutativity (Eq. 2.33)|2.2, non-commutativity]]), so you cannot "resolve" $\boldsymbol\Phi$ into components. Only **infinitesimal** rotations, and hence angular velocities, add as vectors ([[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#9. Small rotations do behave like a vector (derivation)|2.2 §9]]). That is why $\boldsymbol\omega$ can be built as a sum of three rate vectors.

## 3. Composing the three rotation rates (§3.1.2, L7 ll. 160–194)

![[amc_euler_rates_derivation.png|900]]

### Where each rate vector points

Follow the sequence NED $\to$ frame 1 $\to$ frame 2 $\to$ FRD:

| Rotation | Angle | About | Rate vector |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st (yaw) | $\psi$ | $z_{NED}=z_1$ | $\dot\psi\,\mathbf z_{NED}$ |
| 2nd (pitch) | $\theta$ | $y_1=y_2$ | $\dot\theta\,\mathbf y_1$ |
| 3rd (roll) | $\phi$ | $x_2=x_b$ | $\dot\phi\,\mathbf x_b$ |

Angular velocities of successive frames add ([[Angular Velocity Vector]], $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/2}+\boldsymbol\omega_{2/1}+\boldsymbol\omega_{1/r}$):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}=\dot\phi\,\mathbf x_b+\dot\theta\,\mathbf y_1+\dot\psi\,\mathbf z_{NED}.
$$

### Resolving each one into FRD

To add them we need all three in **FRD components**. Each must be carried through the rotations that come **after** it in the sequence:

- **roll rate**: the last rotation, so it is already along $x_b$: $[\dot\phi,0,0]^T$, no transformation;
- **pitch rate**: along $y_2$, so in frame-2 components it is $[0,\dot\theta,0]^T$. To reach FRD it passes through the **roll** rotation only: $\mathbf C_x(\phi)[0,\dot\theta,0]^T$;
- **yaw rate**: along $z_1$, so it is $[0,0,\dot\psi]^T$ in frame-1 components. It passes through **pitch and then roll**: $\mathbf C_x(\phi)\mathbf C_y(\theta)[0,0,\dot\psi]^T$.

The lecture's mnemonic (L7, ll. 177–185): *"the very first [Euler] angle rate is performed in a system that is rotated twice with respect to the final product… The second rotation needs to consider only the rotation of the roll, and the final one is already in the correct system, so it directly applies."* We work **backwards** through the sequence (*"we need to account for the rotation in the reverse order"*, ll. 193–194).

Factorising the common $\mathbf C_x(\phi)$ gives Eq. 3.5:

$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}=\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf C_x(\phi)\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\\dot\theta\\0\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf C_y(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}\right)}
$$

with the elementary DCMs (Eq. 3.6)

$$
\mathbf C_x(\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&c_\phi&s_\phi\\0&-s_\phi&c_\phi\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf C_y(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}c_\theta&0&-s_\theta\\0&1&0\\s_\theta&0&c_\theta\end{bmatrix}.
$$

## 4. Multiplying it out, one step at a time (Appendix §3.4.1, L7 ll. 200–300)

**Step 1: the yaw rate through the pitch rotation.** Only the third column of $\mathbf C_y$ survives:

$$
\mathbf C_y(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_\theta\cdot0+0\cdot0+(-s_\theta)\dot\psi\\0\cdot0+1\cdot0+0\cdot\dot\psi\\s_\theta\cdot0+0\cdot0+c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\0\\c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Physically: tilting the nose up by $\theta$ makes part of the vertical yaw axis point **backwards** along $-x_2$.

**Step 2: add the pitch rate.**

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0\\\dot\theta\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\0\\c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\\dot\theta\\c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 3: rotate the sum through roll.** Row 1 of $\mathbf C_x$ copies the first entry; rows 2 and 3 mix the second and third entries:

$$
\mathbf C_x(\phi)\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\\dot\theta\\c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\c_\phi\dot\theta+s_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi\\-s_\phi\dot\theta+c_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 4: add the roll rate.**

$$
\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-s_\theta\dot\psi\\c_\phi\dot\theta+s_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi\\-s_\phi\dot\theta+c_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**The three scalar equations:**

$$
p=\dot\phi-s_\theta\,\dot\psi,
$$

$$
q=c_\phi\,\dot\theta+s_\phi c_\theta\,\dot\psi,
$$

$$
r=-s_\phi\,\dot\theta+c_\phi c_\theta\,\dot\psi.
$$

## 5. The kinematic transformation matrix E (§3.1.2, Eqs. 3.7–3.9; L7 ll. 301–322)

Collect the coefficients of $\dot\phi$, $\dot\theta$ and $\dot\psi$ into columns:

$$
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-s_\theta\\0&c_\phi&s_\phi c_\theta\\0&-s_\phi&c_\phi c_\theta\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf E(\boldsymbol\Phi)}\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\\dot\theta\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}=\mathbf E(\boldsymbol\Phi)\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}}
$$

**Reading the columns.** Column $k$ is the unit vector of the $k$-th rotation axis, written in FRD:

- column 1, $[1,0,0]^T$: the roll axis is $x_b$ itself;
- column 2, $[0,c_\phi,-s_\phi]^T$: the pitch axis $y_1$, tilted by the bank;
- column 3, $[-s_\theta,s_\phi c_\theta,c_\phi c_\theta]^T$: the NED down axis seen from the body. It is exactly the third column of $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$, the same vector that gives gravity in body axes ([[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#Gravity in body axes (derivation)|2.2]]).

> [!warning] E is not a DCM
> - $\mathbf E$ does not convert the components of one vector between coordinate systems. It maps three angle **rates** onto the components of an angular-velocity vector (notes, p. 3).
> - Its columns are unit vectors but are **not orthogonal**: column 2 · column 3 $=c_\phi s_\phi c_\theta-s_\phi c_\phi c_\theta=0$, but column 1 · column 3 $=-s_\theta\neq0$. The yaw axis and the roll axis are not perpendicular when the nose is pitched.
> - So $\mathbf E^{-1}\neq\mathbf E^T$, and $\det\mathbf E\neq1$ in general.

> [!tip] Why matrix form (L7, ll. 195–199)
> *"It's better if we write everything into a compact matrix form… This equation is something that a computer can process."* The goal is software: a flight simulator or an attitude estimator integrates these equations at every time step.

## 6. Inverting: the rotational kinematic equation (§3.1.3, Eqs. 3.10–3.11; L7 ll. 323–341)

Gyros measure $p,q,r$ (L7, ll. 323–325); we need $\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}$ to update the attitude. The notes simply quote $\mathbf E^{-1}$. Here it is derived by solving the three scalar equations.

**Determinant.** Expand along column 1:

$$
\det\mathbf E=1\cdot\left(c_\phi\cdot c_\phi c_\theta-s_\phi c_\theta\cdot(-s_\phi)\right)=c_\theta\left(c_\phi^2+s_\phi^2\right)=\cos\theta.
$$

**Step 1: eliminate $\dot\theta$ to get $\dot\psi$.** Multiply the $q$ equation by $s_\phi$ and the $r$ equation by $c_\phi$:

$$
s_\phi q=s_\phi c_\phi\,\dot\theta+s_\phi^2c_\theta\,\dot\psi,
\qquad
c_\phi r=-s_\phi c_\phi\,\dot\theta+c_\phi^2c_\theta\,\dot\psi.
$$

Add them; the $\dot\theta$ terms cancel and $s_\phi^2+c_\phi^2=1$:

$$
s_\phi q+c_\phi r=c_\theta\,\dot\psi
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\dot\psi=\frac{q\sin\phi+r\cos\phi}{\cos\theta}.
$$

**Step 2: eliminate $\dot\psi$ to get $\dot\theta$.** Multiply the $q$ equation by $c_\phi$ and the $r$ equation by $s_\phi$:

$$
c_\phi q=c_\phi^2\,\dot\theta+s_\phi c_\phi c_\theta\,\dot\psi,
\qquad
s_\phi r=-s_\phi^2\,\dot\theta+s_\phi c_\phi c_\theta\,\dot\psi.
$$

Subtract the second from the first; the $\dot\psi$ terms cancel:

$$
\dot\theta=q\cos\phi-r\sin\phi.
$$

**Step 3: back-substitute into the $p$ equation.** $p=\dot\phi-s_\theta\dot\psi$, so

$$
\dot\phi=p+\sin\theta\,\dot\psi=p+\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\left(q\sin\phi+r\cos\phi\right)=p+q\sin\phi\tan\theta+r\cos\phi\tan\theta.
$$

**Result (Eqs. 3.10–3.14):**

$$
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\\dot\theta\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&s_\phi\tan\theta&c_\phi\tan\theta\\0&c_\phi&-s_\phi\\0&s_\phi/c_\theta&c_\phi/c_\theta\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf H(\boldsymbol\Phi)}\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix},\qquad\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}=\mathbf H(\boldsymbol\Phi)\,\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}_{b/r}}
$$

This is the **rotational kinematic equation** (or Euler kinematical equation), one of the state equations of the 6-DOF model (L7, ll. 338–341). It contains **no** force, moment, mass or inertia: it is pure geometry. *(Checked symbolically: $\mathbf H\mathbf E=\mathbf I$.)*

> [!check] A second derivation, from the DCM
> [[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#11. Preview: how the DCM evolves in time|2.2 §11]] showed $\dot{\mathbf C}_{b/r}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^b\mathbf C_{b/r}$, so $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}^b=-\dot{\mathbf C}\mathbf C^T$. Differentiating $\mathbf C=\mathbf C_x(\phi)\mathbf C_y(\theta)\mathbf C_z(\psi)$ with the product rule and reading $p,q,r$ off the skew matrix reproduces exactly $\mathbf E\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}$ (checked in SymPy). Two independent routes, one answer.

## 7. Physical interpretation and the small-angle form (§3.1.4)

*Not yet lectured; from the notes.*

**Geometric coupling.** Each Euler rate depends on several body rates:

$$
\dot\phi=p+q\sin\phi\tan\theta+r\cos\phi\tan\theta,\qquad
\dot\theta=q\cos\phi-r\sin\phi,\qquad
\dot\psi=\frac{q\sin\phi+r\cos\phi}{\cos\theta}.
$$

The roll-angle rate is not just $p$. These couplings come from the **chosen Euler sequence**: they are geometric, not aerodynamic or inertial (notes, p. 4).

![[amc_euler_rates_vs_body_rates.png|900]]

**Small angles** (Eqs. 3.15–3.16). For small $\phi$ and $\theta$, use $\sin\phi\approx\phi$, $\cos\phi\approx1$, $\tan\theta\approx\theta$, $\cos\theta\approx1$:

$$
\dot\phi\approx p+q\phi\theta+r\theta,\qquad\dot\theta\approx q-r\phi,\qquad\dot\psi\approx q\phi+r.
$$

The extra terms are products of small quantities ($\phi$, $\theta$ and the rates are all small in a small-disturbance model), so they are dropped:

$$
\boxed{\dot\phi\approx p,\qquad\dot\theta\approx q,\qquad\dot\psi\approx r.}
$$

This is what the linearised SESA2027 models used, e.g. $\dot\theta=q$ in the longitudinal state-space model ([[SESA2027 A1 - Dynamic Systems and Aircraft Equations of Motion#Linearised, decoupled equations of motion|SESA2027 A1]]). It holds **only** near zero attitude; the nonlinear model must use $\mathbf H$.

## 8. The singularity at θ = ±90° (extension)

$\mathbf H$ contains $\tan\theta$ and $1/\cos\theta$, and $\det\mathbf E=\cos\theta$. As $\theta\to\pm90^\circ$, $\mathbf E$ becomes singular and $\dot\phi$, $\dot\psi$ become unbounded for any finite $q$ or $r$ (panel b above: $1/\cos\theta=11.5$ at $85^\circ$, 57 at $89^\circ$).

- This is [[Gimbal Lock]] again ([[SESA3047 2.2 - Direction Cosine Matrices and Euler Angles#8. The singularity: gimbal lock (§2.4.5)|2.2 §8]]), seen through the rates: at $\theta=90^\circ$ the yaw axis (column 3 of $\mathbf E$, $[-1,0,0]^T$) lines up with the roll axis (column 1, $[1,0,0]^T$), so the three rotation axes span only a plane. A body rate about the missing direction cannot be produced by any finite Euler rates.
- The aircraft itself is fine. The singularity belongs to the **description**, not the physics.
- Simulators of aerobatic aircraft and spacecraft therefore integrate the DCM directly ($\dot{\mathbf C}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf C$) or use quaternions, and convert to Euler angles only for display.

## 9. Flat-Earth interpretation (§3.1.5)

*Not yet lectured; from the notes.* Under the [[Flat-Earth Model]] (Eqs. 3.17–3.19):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega_{r/e}=\mathbf 0\ \ (\text{NED frame fixed to Earth}),\qquad\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}=\mathbf 0\ \ (\text{Earth treated as inertial}),
$$

and since angular velocities of successive frames add,

$$
\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}+\boldsymbol\omega_{r/e}+\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}}.
$$

> [!important] Why this matters
> Gyros measure rotation relative to **inertial** space, $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}$. The Euler angles describe attitude relative to the **local NED** frame, so Eq. 3.11 needs $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}$. On a flat, non-rotating Earth the two are equal. On a rotating Earth, subtract the local frame's own rotation first: $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/r}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}-\boldsymbol\omega_{r/i}$, with both terms in the same coordinates before subtracting (notes, Key idea p. 5). The Earth rate is $7.29\times10^{-5}$ rad/s ($0.004^\circ$/s), far below the rates of a manoeuvring aircraft, but it matters for navigation-grade gyros over hours.

## 10. Worked examples

### 10.1 Body rates in a steady level turn (suggested)

An aircraft flies a level coordinated turn at $V=100$ m/s with bank $\phi=30^\circ$, $\theta=0$.

**Turn rate.** Vertical balance $L\cos\phi=mg$ and horizontal $L\sin\phi=mV\dot\psi$; divide:

$$
\dot\psi=\frac{g\tan\phi}{V}=\frac{9.81\times0.5774}{100}=0.05664\ \text{rad/s}=3.25^\circ/\text{s}.
$$

A full turn takes $2\pi/0.05664=111$ s.

**Body rates.** With $\dot\phi=\dot\theta=0$ and $\theta=0$, Eq. 3.7 gives

$$
p=0-0=0,
$$

$$
q=s_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi=0.5\times0.05664=0.0283\ \text{rad/s}=1.62^\circ/\text{s},
$$

$$
r=c_\phi c_\theta\dot\psi=0.8660\times0.05664=0.0491\ \text{rad/s}=2.81^\circ/\text{s}.
$$

> [!tip] Intuition
> In a banked turn the pilot feels the aircraft **pitching** at $1.6^\circ$/s even though the pitch angle never changes. The turn axis is vertical; the body $z$ axis is tilted $30^\circ$ from it; so the turn rate splits into $\dot\psi\cos\phi$ about body $z$ and $\dot\psi\sin\phi$ about body $y$. This is why the elevator must be pulled back in a turn.

> [!check] Round trip
> $\mathbf H$ at $(\phi,\theta)=(30^\circ,0)$ applied to $[0,0.0283,0.0491]$: $\dot\phi=0$, $\dot\theta=0.0283\times0.866-0.0491\times0.5=0.0000$, $\dot\psi=0.0283\times0.5+0.0491\times0.866=0.0566$ ✓.

### 10.2 From gyro readings to Euler rates (suggested)

At $\phi=20^\circ$, $\theta=10^\circ$ the gyros read $p=0.10$, $q=0.05$, $r=-0.02$ rad/s.

$$
\mathbf H=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.0603&0.1657\\0&0.9397&-0.3420\\0&0.3473&0.9542\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row by row:

$$
\dot\phi=0.10+0.0603(0.05)+0.1657(-0.02)=0.0997\ \text{rad/s},
$$

$$
\dot\theta=0.9397(0.05)-0.3420(-0.02)=0.0538\ \text{rad/s},
$$

$$
\dot\psi=0.3473(0.05)+0.9542(-0.02)=-0.0017\ \text{rad/s}.
$$

The small-angle shortcut would have given $\dot\psi\approx r=-0.020$ rad/s: wrong by a factor of 12, and even in the right sign only by luck. At $20^\circ$ of bank the "small" coupling term $q\sin\phi/\cos\theta$ is the same size as $r$.

## Common mistakes

1. Writing $\dot\phi=p$, $\dot\theta=q$, $\dot\psi=r$ outside the small-angle regime.
2. Transforming every Euler rate through the full $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$. Each rate passes only through the rotations **after** it in the sequence.
3. Treating $\mathbf E$ as a DCM and inverting it by transposing.
4. Forgetting the minus in $p=\dot\phi-\sin\theta\,\dot\psi$, or the $-s_\phi$ in $\dot\theta=q\cos\phi-r\sin\phi$.
5. Integrating $\mathbf H\boldsymbol\omega$ through $\theta=\pm90^\circ$.
