# SESA3047 4.1 - Inertia Models and Rotor Dynamics

> [!abstract] Summary
> Expand $\mathbf M=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)$ by finding $\mathbf h=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ first. A real symmetric $\mathbf J$ has orthogonal **principal axes**; only when the chosen body axes coincide with them is $\mathbf J$ diagonal. Diagonal inertia still permits nonlinear rate coupling: with no external moment, any two non-zero rates make all three change (L12). Aircraft symmetry cancels $J_{xy}$ and $J_{yz}$, leaving a coupled roll–yaw block. A rotor adds momentum $\mathbf h_R$, which splits the angular acceleration into $\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{base}+\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{add}$ (L12–L13).

## The topic in one physical picture

An elongated body is easy to spin about its long axis and harder to spin across it. Choose axes along its natural symmetry directions and a spin about one axis produces momentum along that axis. Choose oblique axes attached to the same body and the momentum components mix. Turning the vehicle relative to Earth changes neither the mass distribution nor its inertia expressed in those attached axes.

![[amc_principal_axes.png|850]]

A propeller gives the assembly momentum even with zero body rates. Tilting that momentum requires a moment; changing its magnitude also produces a reaction on the airframe.

## 1. Definitions and inertia conventions (4.1–4.3)

All components are FRD, moments are about the centre of mass, and the mass distribution is fixed:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}L\\M\\N\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}
J_{xx}&-J_{xy}&-J_{xz}\\
-J_{xy}&J_{yy}&-J_{yz}\\
-J_{xz}&-J_{yz}&J_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Notation: 3.3, [[Rotational Dynamic Equation]], Lab 1 and Coursework 1 write the diagonal moments as $J_x,J_y,J_z$; Chapter 4 writes $J_{xx},J_{yy},J_{zz}$. These are the same quantities, $J_x\equiv J_{xx}$, $J_y\equiv J_{yy}$, $J_z\equiv J_{zz}$.

The scalar $M$ is the pitching moment; $\mathbf M$ is the moment vector. Products are defined **without** their matrix minus signs:

$$
J_{xx}=\int_B(y^2+z^2)\,\mathrm dm,\quad
J_{yy}=\int_B(x^2+z^2)\,\mathrm dm,\quad
J_{zz}=\int_B(x^2+y^2)\,\mathrm dm,
$$

$$
J_{xy}=\int_Bxy\,\mathrm dm,\quad
J_{xz}=\int_Bxz\,\mathrm dm,\quad
J_{yz}=\int_Byz\,\mathrm dm.
$$

The mass-element derivation is in [[SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics#3. Angular momentum about the centre of mass (§3.3.2, Eqs. 3.47–3.63)|3.3 §3]].

## 2. Expand the full equation (4.4–4.5; L11 ll. 72–180)

### Step 1: multiply inertia by angular acceleration

$$
\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=
\begin{bmatrix}
J_{xx}\dot p-J_{xy}\dot q-J_{xz}\dot r\\
-J_{xy}\dot p+J_{yy}\dot q-J_{yz}\dot r\\
-J_{xz}\dot p-J_{yz}\dot q+J_{zz}\dot r
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each row uses all three acceleration components. L11 ll. 91–92 garble the first two rows; do not copy the repeated $\dot p$ literally.

### Step 2: find the momentum components

Use the same multiplication with the dots removed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_p&=J_{xx}p-J_{xy}q-J_{xz}r,\\
h_q&=-J_{xy}p+J_{yy}q-J_{yz}r,\\
h_r&=-J_{xz}p-J_{yz}q+J_{zz}r.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 3: take the cross product

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf h
=\begin{bmatrix}0&-r&q\\r&0&-p\\-q&p&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}h_p\\h_q\\h_r\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}qh_r-rh_q\\rh_p-ph_r\\ph_q-qh_p\end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Step 4: combine both contributions

$$
\begin{aligned}
L={}&J_{xx}\dot p-J_{xy}\dot q-J_{xz}\dot r\\
&+q(-J_{xz}p-J_{yz}q+J_{zz}r)\\
&-r(-J_{xy}p+J_{yy}q-J_{yz}r),\\[3pt]
M={}&-J_{xy}\dot p+J_{yy}\dot q-J_{yz}\dot r\\
&+r(J_{xx}p-J_{xy}q-J_{xz}r)\\
&-p(-J_{xz}p-J_{yz}q+J_{zz}r),\\[3pt]
N={}&-J_{xz}\dot p-J_{yz}\dot q+J_{zz}\dot r\\
&+p(-J_{xy}p+J_{yy}q-J_{yz}r)\\
&-q(J_{xx}p-J_{xy}q-J_{xz}r).
\end{aligned}
$$

These are Eq. (4.5). Collecting the $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ parts (the handout's *angular-velocity coupling terms*, p. 3) gives a sign check:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h)_x
&=(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr+J_{xy}pr-J_{xz}pq+J_{yz}(r^2-q^2),\\
(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h)_y
&=(J_{xx}-J_{zz})pr-J_{xy}qr+J_{xz}(p^2-r^2)+J_{yz}pq,\\
(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h)_z
&=(J_{yy}-J_{xx})pq+J_{xy}(q^2-p^2)+J_{xz}qr-J_{yz}pr.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 5: obtain the derivative for a solver

L11 ll. 35–68 revisit the final equation of Chapter 3:

$$
\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf M-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega,
$$

$$
\boxed{\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf J^{-1}(\mathbf M-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega).}
$$

Numerically, solve the linear system rather than explicitly forming its inverse:

    h = J @ omega
    omega_dot = np.linalg.solve(J, moment - np.cross(omega, h))

This assumes a non-degenerate body with invertible inertia. The ideal point-mass rod in 3.3 has zero inertia about its rod axis and is an exception.

## 3. Principal axes and diagonal inertia (4.6–4.9)

L11 ll. 181–203 use the eigenvalue problem. Since $\mathbf J$ is real symmetric, choose orthonormal eigenvectors:

$$
\mathbf J\mathbf e_i=\lambda_i\mathbf e_i,\quad
\mathbf Q=[\mathbf e_1\ \mathbf e_2\ \mathbf e_3],\quad
\mathbf Q^T\mathbf Q=\mathbf I.
$$

The columns of $\mathbf Q$ are principal directions in the original body basis, so $\boldsymbol\omega_P=\mathbf Q^T\boldsymbol\omega_B$. Then

$$
\mathbf J_P=\mathbf Q^T\mathbf J_B\mathbf Q
=\mathbf Q^T\mathbf Q\boldsymbol\Lambda
=\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)}.
$$

Choose eigenvector signs so $\det\mathbf Q=+1$ for a right-handed frame.

### Euler's equations, one row at a time

Set all products of inertia to zero:

$$
\mathbf h=\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}p\\J_{yy}q\\J_{zz}r\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h=
\begin{bmatrix}qJ_{zz}r-rJ_{yy}q\\rJ_{xx}p-pJ_{zz}r\\pJ_{yy}q-qJ_{xx}p\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr\\(J_{xx}-J_{zz})pr\\(J_{yy}-J_{xx})pq\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Move each term to the right and divide by its diagonal inertia:

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\dot p&=\frac{L+(J_{yy}-J_{zz})qr}{J_{xx}},\\
\dot q&=\frac{M+(J_{zz}-J_{xx})pr}{J_{yy}},\\
\dot r&=\frac{N+(J_{xx}-J_{yy})pq}{J_{zz}}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

> [!warning] Diagonal does not mean uncoupled
> Rate products remain in principal axes. They vanish in special cases: pure rotation about one principal axis, or $J_{xx}=J_{yy}=J_{zz}$. L11 ll. 204–228 emphasise axis alignment. Attitude relative to Earth does not determine whether **body-frame** inertia is diagonal.

### Inertia coupling with no external moment (L12 ll. 22–119)

L12 opened the Thursday double with this point. The handout gives it one paragraph (p. 4). Remove the external moments to see what the inertia alone does (ll. 22–26). Set $L=M=N=0$ in the boxed equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot p&=\frac{(J_{yy}-J_{zz})\,qr}{J_{xx}},\\
\dot q&=\frac{(J_{zz}-J_{xx})\,pr}{J_{yy}},\\
\dot r&=\frac{(J_{xx}-J_{yy})\,pq}{J_{zz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each rate's derivative is driven by the **other two** rates (ll. 35–40). This is *"a coupling that is generated by the inertia matrix"*, and it is *"important on the manoeuvrability of an aircraft"* (ll. 39–46). The lecture took three cases.

**Case 1: two non-zero rates (ll. 47–68).** Start with $q_0=0$ and $p_0,r_0\neq0$. Substitute into each row:

$$
\dot p(0)=\frac{(J_{yy}-J_{zz})(0)\,r_0}{J_{xx}}=0,
$$

$$
\dot q(0)=\frac{(J_{zz}-J_{xx})\,p_0r_0}{J_{yy}}\neq0\quad\text{unless }J_{zz}=J_{xx},
$$

$$
\dot r(0)=\frac{(J_{xx}-J_{yy})\,p_0(0)}{J_{zz}}=0.
$$

So $q$ starts to grow even though nothing pitches the body (ll. 54–62). Once $q\neq0$, the first and third rows switch on as well. Hence *"when you have … at least two active rotations … all three angular velocities will change in time"* (ll. 64–68).

**Case 2: equal inertias (ll. 70–78).** If $J_{xx}=J_{zz}$, the second row gives $\dot q=0$, so $q$ stays at zero. Then the other two rows stay at zero, and nothing changes. The lecturer said this is the only cure *"in this specific case"* (l. 71). In general, $J_{xx}=J_{zz}$ removes only the $\dot q$ coupling. If $q\neq0$ as well, $\dot p$ and $\dot r$ still couple, and all coupling vanishes only for $J_{xx}=J_{yy}=J_{zz}$ (handout p. 4).

**Case 3: a single-axis spin (ll. 100–113).** Take $p_0\neq0$ and $q_0=r_0=0$. Every row contains a zero factor:

$$
\dot p=\dot q=\dot r=0.
$$

The body *"will continuously rotate around the forward axis"* (ll. 110–111). A zero derivative means no angular **acceleration**; the angular velocity is still there (ll. 115–118).

>
> Then $q$ rises to $\pm0.097$ rad/s, $r$ swings between $\pm0.05$ rad/s, and $p$ dips to 0.492 rad/s, so all three change. $|\mathbf h|$ and the kinetic energy stay constant to within $2\times10^{-8}$, as they must with no moment. The run is in `generate_amc_ch4_figures.py --check`.

> [!tip] Which way does the nose go?
> A conventional aircraft has $J_{zz}>J_{xx}$, since the fuselage mass lies along $x$. A roll with a yaw rate of the same sign therefore gives $\dot q>0$, and the nose pitches **up** by itself. This is the *inertia coupling* (textbooks: roll coupling) that L12 l. 45 ties to manoeuvrability; 3.3 §5.1 gives its history.

> [!warning] A steady spin is an equilibrium, not necessarily a stable one
> Case 3 says only that a perfect single-axis spin stays perfect. Whether a small disturbance stays small depends on the axis. Spin about the largest or smallest principal moment is stable, but spin about the intermediate one is not. See the tennis-racket simulation in [[SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics#5.3 Torque-free motion and the intermediate-axis theorem (suggested)|3.3 §5.3]]. For a conventional aircraft $J_{xx}$ is usually the smallest, so the lecture's roll spin is the stable case.

### Worked eigenvalue check (suggested)

$$
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}4&-1&0\\-1&4&0\\0&0&6\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$

The in-plane characteristic equation is

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&-1\\-1&4-\lambda\end{bmatrix}
=(4-\lambda)^2-1=0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_1=3,\ \lambda_2=5.
$$

For $\lambda_1=3$, $x-y=0$; for $\lambda_2=5$, $-x-y=0$. Hence

$$
\mathbf e_1=[1,1,0]^T/\sqrt2,\quad
\mathbf e_2=[-1,1,0]^T/\sqrt2,\quad
\mathbf e_3=[0,0,1]^T,\quad\lambda_3=6.
$$

> [!check] Computed check
> $\mathbf Q^T\mathbf J\mathbf Q=\operatorname{diag}(3,5,6)$ and $\mathbf Q^T\mathbf Q=\mathbf I$.

## 4. Aircraft symmetry and the roll–yaw inverse (4.10–4.14)

**Lectured in L12, ll. 120–208.** A conventional aircraft has a plane of symmetry: the $x$–$z$ plane, which *"cuts our aircraft in two parts"* (ll. 125–131). Reflection through $y=0$ changes the signs of $xy$ and $yz$ but leaves $xz$ unchanged:

$$
J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0,\qquad
\mathbf J=\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}&0&-J_{xz}\\0&J_{yy}&0\\-J_{xz}&0&J_{zz}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The lecture's rule of thumb (l. 137): *"all the J elements that have Y become equal to zero"*. Each mass element at $+y$ has a mirror twin at $-y$, so their $xy$ and $yz$ contributions cancel in pairs.

> [!tip] Here "plus and minus" is the right argument
> In L10 (ll. 161–164) the same words were used for $\int\mathbf r\,\mathrm dm=\mathbf 0$. That integral is zero by the **definition** of the centre of mass and needs no symmetry ([[SESA3047 3.3 - Rigid-Body Rotational Dynamics#Two facts L10 glossed|3.3]]). Here, $J_{xy}=J_{yz}=0$ is **not** automatic: it really does need the mirror symmetry. An aircraft without a symmetry plane needs the full form of §2 (L12 ll. 189–191).

The lecturer called this *"a middle case"* between the full and the diagonal matrix, and the one *"more representative of … a conventional aircraft"* (ll. 138–140). Thus the $y$ axis is principal, but the chosen $x,z$ axes need not be. Substitution into §2 gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
L&=J_{xx}\dot p-J_{xz}\dot r+(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr-J_{xz}pq,\\
M&=J_{yy}\dot q+(J_{xx}-J_{zz})pr+J_{xz}(p^2-r^2),\\
N&=-J_{xz}\dot p+J_{zz}\dot r+(J_{yy}-J_{xx})pq+J_{xz}qr.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 1: isolate the acceleration terms

$$
R_L=L-(J_{zz}-J_{yy})qr+J_{xz}pq,\qquad
R_N=N-(J_{yy}-J_{xx})pq-J_{xz}qr.
$$

$$
J_{xx}\dot p-J_{xz}\dot r=R_L,\qquad
-J_{xz}\dot p+J_{zz}\dot r=R_N.
$$

### Step 2: invert the two-by-two block

$$
\Gamma=J_{xx}J_{zz}-J_{xz}^2,
$$

$$
\begin{bmatrix}J_{xx}&-J_{xz}\\-J_{xz}&J_{zz}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
=\frac1\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}J_{zz}&J_{xz}\\J_{xz}&J_{xx}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This is the inverse written on the board in L12, ll. 158–170, where $\Gamma$ was introduced as *"this term that is gamma"* (l. 169). The full $3\times3$ inverse is Eq. 4.12, quoted in §5 Step 4. For positive-definite inertia, $\Gamma>0$. Multiplication gives

$$
\dot p=\frac{J_{zz}R_L+J_{xz}R_N}{\Gamma},\qquad
\dot r=\frac{J_{xz}R_L+J_{xx}R_N}{\Gamma}.
$$

### Step 3: expand the numerators

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma\dot p={}&J_{zz}L+J_{xz}N\\
&+J_{xz}[J_{zz}-(J_{yy}-J_{xx})]pq\\
&-[J_{zz}(J_{zz}-J_{yy})+J_{xz}^2]qr,\\
\Gamma\dot r={}&J_{xz}L+J_{xx}N\\
&+[J_{xz}^2-J_{xx}(J_{yy}-J_{xx})]pq\\
&-J_{xz}[J_{zz}-J_{yy}+J_{xx}]qr.
\end{aligned}
$$

Together with the pitch equation, this gives Eq. (4.14). Keeping the long numerators separate avoids an overflowing display:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P={}&J_{zz}L+J_{xz}N+J_{xz}(J_{xx}-J_{yy}+J_{zz})pq\\
&-[J_{zz}(J_{zz}-J_{yy})+J_{xz}^2]qr,\\
R={}&J_{xz}L+J_{xx}N-J_{xz}(J_{xx}-J_{yy}+J_{zz})qr\\
&+[J_{xx}(J_{xx}-J_{yy})+J_{xz}^2]pq.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boxed{\dot p=P/\Gamma,\qquad
\dot q=\frac{M+(J_{zz}-J_{xx})pr-J_{xz}(p^2-r^2)}{J_{yy}},
\qquad\dot r=R/\Gamma.}
$$

> [!tip] Check the limit
> Setting $J_{xz}=0$ recovers Euler's equations. At zero body rates, a pure roll moment gives $\dot r=J_{xz}L/\Gamma$: products of inertia alone can couple accelerations.

### What L12 said to remember (ll. 179–208)

The lecturer showed Eq. 4.14 from the notes (*"they're quite long"*, l. 179) and drew three messages from it:

1. **The pattern is the same as the diagonal case.** The rate-product terms that couple the equations are still there. *"The case that I would generally remember is this one"*: the diagonal form of §3, because *"the physics is mostly the same"* (ll. 180–187).
2. **Keep the matrix form for computation.** It is *"more compact"*. For numerical integration or code, *"let the software, for example, MATLAB, Python … develop the matrix product and solve the equations for you"* (ll. 196–201). That is the `np.linalg.solve` line in [[#Step 5: obtain the derivative for a solver|§2 Step 5]].
3. **Explicit accelerations for a full $\mathbf J$ are possible but not worth it.** *"It becomes very complicated and I don't think it's very useful"* (ll. 202–208). This is why §2 stops at Eq. 4.5 and solves numerically.

> [!tip] Reading the emphasis (our inference, not a stated exam rule)
> Be ready to *derive* the diagonal case (Eq. 4.9) and *explain* the coupling. The aircraft case needs the method (block inverse with $\Gamma$) more than the memorised numerators. The lecture did not multiply Eq. 4.14 out (ll. 142–144, 161–162), and §4 Steps 1–3 above do it in full.

### Worked principal axes of the aircraft inertia (suggested)

**Suggested, not lecturer-set.** L11 described the eigenvalue route to principal axes (ll. 181–203) without numbers. Apply it to the aircraft matrix just used, $J_{xx}=1285$, $J_{yy}=1825$, $J_{zz}=2667$, $J_{xz}=100$ kg m².

**Step 1: the $y$ axis is already principal.** Row 2 of $\mathbf J$ is $[0,J_{yy},0]$, so

$$
\mathbf J\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}=1825\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},
\qquad\lambda_2=1825\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\quad \mathbf e_2=[0,1,0]^T.
$$

**Step 2: characteristic equation of the $x$–$z$ block.**

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix}1285-\lambda&-100\\-100&2667-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=0,
$$

$$
(1285-\lambda)(2667-\lambda)-100^2=0,
$$

$$
\lambda^2-3952\,\lambda+(1285\times2667-100^2)=0,
$$

$$
\lambda^2-3952\,\lambda+3\,417\,095=0.
$$

The constant term is $\Gamma$ from §4: the determinant of the block.

**Step 3: solve the quadratic.**

$$
\lambda=\frac{3952\pm\sqrt{3952^2-4(3\,417\,095)}}{2}
=\frac{3952\pm\sqrt{1\,949\,924}}{2}
=\frac{3952\pm1396.397}{2},
$$

$$
\lambda_1=1277.80\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\qquad\lambda_3=2674.20\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$

**Step 4: the eigenvector for $\lambda_1$.** The first row of $(\mathbf J-\lambda_1\mathbf I)\mathbf e_1=\mathbf 0$ reads

$$
(1285-1277.80)\,e_x-100\,e_z=0,
$$

$$
\frac{e_z}{e_x}=\frac{7.1984}{100}=0.071984.
$$

Write $\mathbf e_1=[\cos\varepsilon,0,\sin\varepsilon]^T$, so $\tan\varepsilon=0.071984$ and

$$
\varepsilon=4.117^\circ.
$$

The principal roll axis points $4.1^\circ$ **below** body $x$, since $+z$ is down. Then $\mathbf e_3=[-\sin\varepsilon,0,\cos\varepsilon]^T$ completes a right-handed set ($\det\mathbf Q=+1$).

**Step 5: a closed-form cross-check.** Rotate by $\varepsilon$ about $y$ and demand that the new product of inertia vanishes:

$$
\mathbf e_1^T\mathbf J\,\mathbf e_3
=(J_{zz}-J_{xx})\sin\varepsilon\cos\varepsilon-J_{xz}(\cos^2\varepsilon-\sin^2\varepsilon)=0,
$$

$$
\tfrac12(J_{zz}-J_{xx})\sin2\varepsilon=J_{xz}\cos2\varepsilon,
$$

$$
\tan2\varepsilon=\frac{2J_{xz}}{J_{zz}-J_{xx}}=\frac{200}{1382}=0.144718
\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varepsilon=4.117^\circ.\ \checkmark
$$

> [!check] Computed check
> `numpy.linalg.eigh` gives principal moments $[1277.80160,\ 1825,\ 2674.19840]$ kg m². With $\mathbf Q=[\mathbf e_1\ \mathbf e_2\ \mathbf e_3]$, $\mathbf Q^T\mathbf J\mathbf Q$ is diagonal to rounding and $\det\mathbf Q=1$. The trace is unchanged: $1285+1825+2667=5777=1277.80+1825+2674.20$. These are asserted in `generate_amc_ch4_figures.py --check`.

> [!tip] Why the diagonal form is usually good enough
> A product of inertia that is small next to $J_{zz}-J_{xx}$ tilts the principal axes only slightly. Here the principal moments differ from $J_{xx}$ and $J_{zz}$ by just 0.56% and 0.27%. That is the quantitative side of L12's *"the physics is mostly the same"* (ll. 180–187). A positive $J_{xz}=\int xz\,\mathrm dm$ means mass ahead of the centre of mass sits slightly low and mass aft (the fin) slightly high. That tilts the long axis nose-down.

> [!warning] Diagonal inertia means using the tilted axes everywhere
> Using $\operatorname{diag}(1277.80,1825,2674.20)$ is exact only if $p,q,r$ and $L,M,N$ are also resolved along $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$, i.e. axes pitched $4.1^\circ$ nose-down from FRD. Mixing principal moments with FRD rates is the axis-alignment error L11 warned about (ll. 204–228).

## 5. Spinning internal rotors (4.15–4.25)

**Lectured in L12, ll. 222–350 (Eqs. 4.15–4.20 derived on the board), and L13, ll. 1–86 (Eqs. 4.21–4.24).** Eq. 4.25 is from the handout only. See [[Rotor Angular Momentum]] and [[Momentum Bias and Gyroscopic Rigidity]].

### Step 1: define inertia and extra momentum consistently

Until now, $\mathbf h_{cm/i}=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}$ (L12 ll. 224–227). With rotors, *"we would simply have an additional momentum contribution of the rotor"*, in any direction (ll. 228–233). Let $\mathbf J$ include the complete assembly's inertia, including the rotor as if locked to the body. Add the momentum from spin **relative to the body**:

$$
\mathbf h=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega+\mathbf h_R.
$$

For a balanced axisymmetric fixed-axis rotor, $\mathbf h_R=I_R\Omega_R\mathbf e_R$, with relative spin rate $\Omega_R$. This avoids counting locked rotor inertia twice.

**Why the rigid-body equations still apply (L12 ll. 234–242).** The derivation assumed a fixed centre of mass and *"no relative movement of components"*. Spinning blades are *"theoretically a problem"*. But an **axisymmetric** rotor does not move the global centre of mass as it turns, so the body part of the equation can be kept and the rotor added on top.

The assembly is no longer literally one rigid body. Constant $\mathbf J$ remains a consistent approximation for balanced, fixed-axis, axisymmetric rotors. A constant centre of mass alone does not guarantee constant inertia: the axisymmetry is what keeps the locked inertia the same at every rotor angle.

### Step 2: differentiate total momentum

Start from the moment balance of Chapter 3, with the derivative taken in the body frame (L12 ll. 243–263, Eq. 4.16):

$$
\mathbf M={}^b\dot{\mathbf h}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h,
$$

$$
\mathbf M={}^b\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega+\mathbf h_R)
+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega+\mathbf h_R),
$$

$$
\mathbf M=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+{}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R
+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R.
$$

The middle line used ${}^b\dot{\mathbf J}=\mathbf 0$, so the derivative of $\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega$ is $\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$ (ll. 275–289). The cross product was split term by term (ll. 298–302). This is Eq. 4.18.

### Step 3: constant rotor momentum, the lectured case (Eqs. 4.19–4.20)

The lecture assumed *"the momentum of the rotor doesn't change in time"* (L12 ll. 295–297). Precisely, it is constant **in body coordinates**, so ${}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R=\mathbf 0$. Drop that term:

$$
\mathbf M=\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R.
$$

Move both cross products to the left (ll. 310–320):

$$
\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf M-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R.
$$

*"We cannot divide something by a matrix"*, so premultiply by $\mathbf J^{-1}$ (ll. 321–327):

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf J^{-1}\left(\mathbf M-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R\right).
$$

Write the cross products as skew matrices (ll. 330–334):

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf J^{-1}\left(\mathbf M-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf h_R\right).
$$

Distribute $\mathbf J^{-1}$ over the last term:

$$
\boxed{\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\underbrace{\mathbf J^{-1}\left(\mathbf M-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega\right)}_{\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{base}}
\ \underbrace{-\ \mathbf J^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf h_R}_{\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{add}}.}
$$

The lecturer asked the room what the first term is (ll. 336–342). It is the rotational dynamic equation used until now, i.e. Eq. 4.5, 4.9 or 4.14 depending on $\mathbf J$. The lecture calls it the **base** (or body) contribution; the second is the **additional** contribution of the rotors (ll. 343–349).

### Step 3b: changing rotor momentum (Eq. 4.25, handout only)

If the rotor speed or spin axis changes, keep ${}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R$:

$$
\boxed{\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=\mathbf J^{-1}\left(\mathbf M-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega
-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R-{}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R\right).}
$$

For constant momentum **in body coordinates**, only its body derivative vanishes, which returns Step 3:

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{add}=-\mathbf J^{-1}(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R).
$$

> [!warning] Constant spin does not remove the rotor term
> Body rotation turns a body-fixed rotor momentum vector in inertial space. Changing spin adds $-\mathbf J^{-1}{}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R$. Motor torque is internal to the combined assembly; do not count its reaction a second time as an external moment on that assembly.

### Step 4: explicit aircraft rotor corrections

L13 (ll. 9–34) assembled the three ingredients on the board. Take $\mathbf h_R=[h_x,h_y,h_z]^T$ (ll. 12–15) and the skew matrix *"that we have used until now"* (ll. 18–27):

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf h_R
=\begin{bmatrix}0&-r&q\\r&0&-p\\-q&p&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}h_x\\h_y\\h_z\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}qh_z-rh_y\\rh_x-ph_z\\ph_y-qh_x\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its negative is the vector that $\mathbf J^{-1}$ acts on:

$$
-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf h_R
=\begin{bmatrix}rh_y-qh_z\\ph_z-rh_x\\qh_x-ph_y\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The inverse is the aircraft one, Eq. 4.22 (*"the same that we have used before"*, ll. 16–17):

$$
\mathbf J^{-1}=\frac1\Gamma
\begin{bmatrix}J_{zz}&0&J_{xz}\\0&\Gamma/J_{yy}&0\\J_{xz}&0&J_{xx}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row 1 of $\mathbf J^{-1}$ picks the first and third entries:

$$
\dot p_{add}=\frac1\Gamma\left[J_{zz}(rh_y-qh_z)+0\cdot(ph_z-rh_x)+J_{xz}(qh_x-ph_y)\right].
$$

Row 2 picks only the middle entry, and the $\Gamma$ cancels:

$$
\dot q_{add}=\frac1\Gamma\cdot\frac{\Gamma}{J_{yy}}(ph_z-rh_x)=\frac{ph_z-rh_x}{J_{yy}}.
$$

Row 3 mirrors row 1 with $J_{xz}$ and $J_{xx}$. Together these are Eq. (4.23). The board version of this multiplication is lost in the recording (L13 ll. 63–81):

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\dot p_{add}&=\frac{J_{zz}(rh_y-qh_z)+J_{xz}(qh_x-ph_y)}{\Gamma},\\
\dot q_{add}&=\frac{ph_z-rh_x}{J_{yy}},\\
\dot r_{add}&=\frac{J_{xz}(rh_y-qh_z)+J_{xx}(qh_x-ph_y)}{\Gamma}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Add these to the base derivatives in §4, row by row (L13 ll. 35–52, Eq. 4.24):

$$
\dot p=\dot p_{base}+\dot p_{add},\qquad
\dot q=\dot q_{base}+\dot q_{add},\qquad
\dot r=\dot r_{base}+\dot r_{add}.
$$

For varying momentum, also add the inverse applied to $-{}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R$ (Step 3b).

### Diagonal inertia: the quadcopter form (L13 ll. 82–86)

*"This will change depending on J … more complicated if we have a full J, or … simpler if we have a diagonal J, because everything comes from this equation"* (ll. 83–86). The diagonal case was not written out, so here it is. Set $J_{xz}=0$:

$$
\Gamma=J_{xx}J_{zz}-0^2=J_{xx}J_{zz}.
$$

Substitute into row 1 of Eq. 4.23:

$$
\dot p_{add}=\frac{J_{zz}(rh_y-qh_z)+0}{J_{xx}J_{zz}}=\frac{rh_y-qh_z}{J_{xx}}.
$$

Row 3 likewise:

$$
\dot r_{add}=\frac{0+J_{xx}(qh_x-ph_y)}{J_{xx}J_{zz}}=\frac{qh_x-ph_y}{J_{zz}}.
$$

So with principal axes,

$$
\boxed{\dot p_{add}=\frac{rh_y-qh_z}{J_{xx}},\qquad
\dot q_{add}=\frac{ph_z-rh_x}{J_{yy}},\qquad
\dot r_{add}=\frac{qh_x-ph_y}{J_{zz}}.}
$$

Each row is simply $-(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R)$ divided by that axis's moment of inertia, which is the same pattern as Euler's equations.

> [!tip] Quadcopter: why the rotor directions alternate
> A quadcopter's four rotors all spin about body $z$, so $h_x=h_y=0$ and $h_z=\sum_i I_R\Omega_i$, with the sign set by each rotor's sense of spin. Only two terms survive: $\dot p_{add}=-qh_z/J_{xx}$ and $\dot q_{add}=ph_z/J_{yy}$. A pitch rate produces a roll acceleration, and a roll rate a pitch acceleration. With two rotors spinning each way at equal speeds, $h_z=0$ and this rotor cross-coupling cancels. Yaw is commanded by speeding up one pair and slowing the other. The net rotor drag torque then turns the body, and while the speeds change, ${}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R\neq\mathbf 0$ adds a transient reaction (Step 3b).

### Worked rotor check (suggested)

Add $\mathbf h_R=[40,0,0]^T$ kg m²/s to the aircraft example:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R
=[0,\ 0.05(40),\ -0.1(40)]^T=[0,2,-4]^T\ \mathrm{N\,m},
$$

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{add}=\mathbf J^{-1}[0,-2,4]^T
=\begin{bmatrix}0.00011706\\-0.00109589\\0.00150420\end{bmatrix}
\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}.
$$

For spin-up $\,{}^b\dot{\mathbf h}_R=[10,0,0]^T$ N m, the further contribution is

$$
-\mathbf J^{-1}[10,0,0]^T
=\begin{bmatrix}-0.00780488\\0\\-0.00029265\end{bmatrix}
\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}.
$$

Both contributions were checked against direct matrix solves.

### Worked engine-rotor contribution in a pull-up (suggested)

**Suggested, not lecturer-set.** It applies the lectured Eqs. 4.15–4.24 to a real rotor. Take the light aircraft above ($J_{xx}=1285$, $J_{yy}=1825$, $J_{zz}=2667$, $J_{xz}=100$ kg m², $\Gamma=3\,417\,095$ kg² m⁴). Its propeller and crankshaft have $I_R=1.6$ kg m² and turn at 2400 rpm, clockwise as seen from the cockpit. With the thumb pointing forward, the right-hand rule makes that a **positive** spin about $x_b$.

**Step 1: rotor momentum.**

$$
\Omega_R=2400\times\frac{2\pi}{60}=251.327\ \mathrm{rad/s},
$$

$$
h_x=I_R\Omega_R=1.6\times251.327=402.124\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s},
\qquad\mathbf h_R=[402.124,\ 0,\ 0]^T.
$$

**Step 2: a pull-up.** The pilot pitches up at $q=0.2$ rad/s with $p=r=0$. Take the cross product from §5 Step 4:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R
=\begin{bmatrix}qh_z-rh_y\\rh_x-ph_z\\ph_y-qh_x\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-0.2\times402.124\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-80.425\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N\,m}.
$$

The rotor term enters the dynamics as $-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R=[0,\ 0,\ +80.425]^T$ N m. It acts like a **yawing moment to the right**, although no external moment acts.

**Step 3: the added accelerations (Eq. 4.23).** With $h_y=h_z=0$ and $p=r=0$, only the $qh_x$ terms survive:

$$
\dot p_{add}=\frac{J_{xz}\,qh_x}{\Gamma}=\frac{100\times80.425}{3\,417\,095}=0.0023536\ \mathrm{rad/s^2},
$$

$$
\dot q_{add}=\frac{ph_z-rh_x}{J_{yy}}=0,
$$

$$
\dot r_{add}=\frac{J_{xx}\,qh_x}{\Gamma}=\frac{1285\times80.425}{3\,417\,095}=0.0302438\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}.
$$

The diagonal-inertia form gives $\dot r_{add}=qh_x/J_{zz}=0.0301555$ rad/s², within 0.3%.

**Step 4: how much it matters.** Held for 2 s with no correction (and ignoring aerodynamic yaw damping, which would limit it), the yaw rate would build to $2\times0.0302438=0.0605$ rad/s $=3.47^\circ$/s. A rudder moment $N=-80.4$ N m cancels it completely. Because $\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{add}=\mathbf J^{-1}(-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_R)$, an equal and opposite moment removes $\dot p_{add}$ as well. On a fin arm of about 4.5 m, that is a side force of only 17.9 N.

> [!check] Computed check
> `np.linalg.solve(J, -np.cross([0, 0.2, 0], [402.124, 0, 0]))` returns $[0.0023536,\ 0,\ 0.0302438]$ rad/s². Adding the rudder moment $[0,0,-80.425]$ N m returns $[0,0,0]$. Both are asserted in `generate_amc_ch4_figures.py --check`.

> [!tip] The precession rule gives the same answer (textbook gyroscopic precession)
> Pitching up tilts the rotor's momentum vector up. The required change of $\mathbf h_R$ is upward, $\dot{\mathbf h}=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ is along $-z_b$, and the airframe must supply that moment. The reaction on the airframe is $+z_b$: nose right. Reverse the pitch rate and the yaw reverses. That is why a tailwheel aircraft with a clockwise propeller swings **left** as the tail comes up on take-off ($q<0$). The same term, scaled up by the much larger momentum of a jet or turboprop rotor, is why engine-rotor terms appear in the full 6-DOF model ([[SESA3047 4.3 - Complete Flat-Earth 6-DOF Model|4.3]]).
