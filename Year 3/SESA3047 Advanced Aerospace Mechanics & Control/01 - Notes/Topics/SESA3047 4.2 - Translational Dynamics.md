# SESA3047 4.2 - Translational Dynamics

> [!abstract] Summary
> Newton's law requires **inertial** acceleration. On rotating Earth, centrifugal acceleration can be absorbed into effective gravity, leaving a Coriolis term. Under the flat-Earth approximation, local NED is inertial and gravity is constant. Body axes still rotate, so their measured velocity derivatives differ from inertial acceleration:
>
> $$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf v}^{FRD}=\mathbf F^{FRD}/m+\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}\mathbf g^{NED}-\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}\times\mathbf v^{FRD}.}$$
>
> Here $\mathbf F$ **excludes gravity**. These three derivatives complete the dynamics needed for the 12-state model.

## The topic in one physical picture

Imagine a sensor reporting the velocity components along the aircraft's nose, right wing and belly. If the aircraft yaws while its velocity in Earth directions stays the same, the forward and lateral readings change. That change does not need a new force: the coordinate axes have turned.

The term $-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ accounts for that turning. Gravity is a separate physical acceleration; its direction is simple in NED and must be rotated into FRD before adding it to body-axis forces.

L13 introduced the section by saying that *"there is not much to do"* (ll. 89–94). Every acceleration needed was already derived in Chapter 3, so this section only substitutes them into Newton's second law. After that, all four equation groups exist and can be assembled into the 6-DOF model (ll. 94–97; [[SESA3047 4.3 - Complete Flat-Earth 6-DOF Model|4.3]]).

## 1. Newton's law and the two meanings of gravity (4.26–4.31)

Let $\mathbf F$ contain aerodynamic, propulsive and other **non-gravitational** forces, and let $\mathbf G$ be true gravitational acceleration. This split is how L13 divides the external forces: *"F are all the forces that are propulsive, aerodynamics … and G is … the gravitational acceleration"*, multiplied by $m$ to make it a force (ll. 162–167). The momentum here is the **linear** momentum $\mathbf Q$, *"not the angular momentum h"* (ll. 105–111). For constant mass,

$$
\mathbf Q=m\mathbf v_{cm/i},
$$

$$
\frac{{}^i\mathrm d\mathbf Q}{\mathrm dt}
=m\mathbf a_{cm/i}
=\sum\mathbf F_{ext}
=\mathbf F+m\mathbf G.
$$

### Step 1: keep Earth rotation initially

Let $\boldsymbol\Omega=\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}$ be constant (a shorthand used only in this note; the handout and L13 keep $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}$ throughout) and take the Earth-centred origin to have negligible inertial acceleration. The moving-frame acceleration formula from [[SESA3047 3.2 - Translational Kinematics and Accelerations in Moving Frames|3.2]] gives the following. L13 recalled it for a general point $P$ and then set $P$ to the centre of mass: *"we don't have to derive nothing new"* (ll. 122–157).

$$
\mathbf a_i=\mathbf a_e+
\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf p)
+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_e.
$$

There is no angular-acceleration term because $\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}=\mathbf0$. Here $\mathbf p$ is measured from the **Earth's centre**, not the local NED origin.

### Step 2: substitute into Newton's law

$$
m\left[\mathbf a_e+
\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf p)
+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_e\right]
=\mathbf F+m\mathbf G,
$$

$$
\mathbf a_e=\frac{\mathbf F}{m}+\mathbf G
-\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf p)
-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_e.
$$

This is Eq. 4.30, written on the board in L13 ll. 183–190. The lecturer labelled the terms: centrifugal, $-\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf p)$; Coriolis, $-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_e$; and $\mathbf F/m+\mathbf G$, the acceleration from the external forces (ll. 187–189). Every quantity here is a **vector**, including $\mathbf G$, as the answer to a student's question confirmed (ll. 193–200).

> [!warning] "Inertial" means two different things here
> The handout labels $\mathbf F/m+\mathbf G$ the *inertial acceleration*: the acceleration the real forces produce, as seen from the inertial frame. L13 ll. 188–189 called these *"the inertial forces or the external forces"*. In most mechanics texts, however, **inertial forces** are the apparent ones, here the centrifugal and Coriolis terms that appear only because the Earth-fixed frame rotates. Keep them apart: $\mathbf F$ and $m\mathbf G$ are real forces; the two $\boldsymbol\Omega$ terms are frame effects.

### Step 3: define effective gravity

**Handout only (Eq. 4.31).** L13 skipped this step and went straight to flat Earth (ll. 202–204). Under flat Earth $\mathbf g=\mathbf G$, so nothing in the lectured result depends on it.

$$
\mathbf g=\mathbf G-\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf p),
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_e=\mathbf F/m+\mathbf g-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_e.}
$$

> [!warning] Do not count centrifugal acceleration twice
> If $\mathbf g$ is effective gravity, the centrifugal term is already included. $\mathbf G$ denotes gravitation alone. The Coriolis term remains separate.

## 2. Apply the flat-Earth assumptions (4.32)

Treat a local NED frame as inertial, neglect Earth rotation and curvature, and take constant gravity:

$$
\boldsymbol\Omega=\mathbf0,\qquad
\mathbf a_i=\mathbf a_e,\qquad
\mathbf g=\mathbf G,\qquad
\mathbf g^{NED}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\g\end{bmatrix},\quad g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

Newton's law then becomes

$$
\mathbf a_{cm/e}=\mathbf F/m+\mathbf g.
$$

*"The centrifugal and the Coriolis acceleration are not considered anymore, because … omega E i is equal to 0"* (L13 ll. 202–210). This is the lecture's main point for the section, repeated at ll. 274–277.

The down component is positive because NED uses **down**, not height, as its third coordinate. This approximation does **not** say that the body frame is inertial.

## 3. Derive the body-velocity equation (4.33–4.37)

### Step 1: use the transport theorem

The acceleration in §2 is the derivative taken **in the Earth frame**. For the state equation we want the derivative **in the body frame**, because that is what $\dot u,\dot v,\dot w$ are (L13 ll. 212–216). For the Earth-relative velocity vector,

$$
{}^e\dot{\mathbf v}_{cm/e}
={}^b\dot{\mathbf v}_{cm/e}
+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf v_{cm/e}.
$$

Under flat Earth, $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}=\boldsymbol\omega_{b/i}$. Expressing all components in FRD gives

$$
\mathbf a^{FRD}_{cm/e}
=\dot{\mathbf v}^{FRD}+\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}\times\mathbf v^{FRD}.
$$

The dot on components is the body-frame derivative; it is the derivative required when integrating $u,v,w$. L13 wrote this as Eq. 4.33 (ll. 217–222), noting that it is *"a result that we have already obtained"* in Chapter 3. Had the derivative been left in the Earth frame, there would be no transport term at all (ll. 267–269). The $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ term exists only because of the body-frame derivative.

### Step 2: substitute the force balance

$$
\dot{\mathbf v}^{FRD}+\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}\times\mathbf v^{FRD}
=\mathbf F^{FRD}/m+\mathbf g^{FRD},
$$

$$
\dot{\mathbf v}^{FRD}
=\mathbf F^{FRD}/m+\mathbf g^{FRD}
-\boldsymbol\omega^{FRD}\times\mathbf v^{FRD}.
$$

This is Eq. 4.35, *"finally, … the translational dynamics"* (L13 ll. 226–232).

### Step 3: rotate gravity

L13 came back to this point after first skipping it: *"I forgot one thing … it's something that I forgot to tell you"* (ll. 289–303). The simple form $[0,0,g]^T$ holds only in **NED** coordinates, where down is always down on a flat Earth (ll. 294–299). In Eq. 4.35, $\mathbf g$ carries FRD components, so *"this term needs to be rotated with the matrix"* (l. 302). The forces $\mathbf F$ are already in body axes and stay as they are (ll. 304–306).

The DCM $\mathbf C=\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$ maps **NED components into FRD**. Multiplying by $[0,0,g]^T$ selects its third column:

$$
\mathbf g^{FRD}=\mathbf C\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\g\end{bmatrix}
=g\begin{bmatrix}
-\sin\theta\\
\sin\phi\cos\theta\\
\cos\phi\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The same components follow from geometry in two moves, matching the 3-2-1 sequence. Yaw comes first but does nothing to a vertical vector.

1. **Pitch by $\theta$.** $\mathbf g$ splits into $-g\sin\theta$ along $x_b$ (backwards when the nose is up) and $g\cos\theta$ normal to $x_b$.
2. **Roll by $\phi$ about $x_b$.** The $g\cos\theta$ part splits into $g\cos\theta\sin\phi$ along $y_b$ (towards the low wing) and $g\cos\theta\cos\phi$ along $z_b$.

![[amc_gravity_body_components.png|900]]

The squares add back to $g^2$:

$$
g^2\sin^2\theta+g^2\cos^2\theta\left(\sin^2\phi+\cos^2\phi\right)
=g^2\sin^2\theta+g^2\cos^2\theta
=g^2.
$$

Hence Eq. (4.37) is

$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf v}
=\frac{\mathbf F}{m}+\mathbf C\mathbf g^{NED}
-\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathbf v.}
$$

The last step swapped the cross product for the skew matrix $\tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}$ (L13 ll. 307–309).

> [!tip] Gravity gives a useful DCM check
> No yaw angle appears because yaw about local down cannot change a vertical vector. Orthogonality preserves $|\mathbf g^{FRD}|=g$. At zero roll and pitch, gravity is $[0,0,g]^T$ in FRD too.

### The derivation in four moves (L13 ll. 243–278)

The lecturer ran through the derivation a second time (*"Can I repeat that one more time?"*, l. 244). As an exam narrative:

1. **Newton needs an inertial acceleration.** $\sum\mathbf F_{ext}=m\mathbf a_{cm/i}$ (ll. 245–247).
2. **Rotating Earth.** Expressing that acceleration relative to Earth adds a centrifugal and a Coriolis term, because the Earth-fixed frame is not inertial (ll. 248–260).
3. **Flat Earth.** $\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}=\mathbf 0$, so both terms vanish and $\mathbf a_{cm/e}=\mathbf F/m+\mathbf g$ (ll. 261, 274–277).
4. **Body-frame derivative.** The transport theorem brings in $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}\times\mathbf v_{cm/e}$ (ll. 262–272).

Finally, rotate $\mathbf g$ into FRD with $\mathbf C_{FRD/NED}$ (ll. 289–303).

> [!tip] Two different rotations, two different terms
> Steps 2 and 4 both come from rotating axes, but they are different rotations. The Coriolis and centrifugal terms come from **Earth** turning relative to inertial space ($\boldsymbol\omega_{e/i}$), and flat Earth deletes them. The $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ term comes from the **body** turning relative to Earth ($\boldsymbol\omega_{b/e}$), and it survives every Earth model.

## 4. Scalar equations and force conventions (4.38–4.40)

**Handout only**: L13 did not expand the rows or introduce the $X,Y,Z$ convention. Expand

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v
=\begin{bmatrix}p\\q\\r\end{bmatrix}
\times\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\w\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}qw-rv\\ru-pw\\pv-qu\end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Forces excluding gravity

With $\mathbf F=[F_x,F_y,F_z]^T$,

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\dot u&=F_x/m-g\sin\theta+rv-qw,\\
\dot v&=F_y/m+g\sin\phi\cos\theta+pw-ru,\\
\dot w&=F_z/m+g\cos\phi\cos\theta+qu-pv.
\end{aligned}}
$$

### Forces including gravity

The handout switches convention before Eq. (4.38). Define

$$
\mathbf F_B=\mathbf F+m\mathbf g^{FRD}
=\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
\mathbf F_B=m(\dot{\mathbf v}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v),
$$

$$
\boxed{X=m(\dot u+qw-rv),\quad
Y=m(\dot v+ru-pw),\quad
Z=m(\dot w+pv-qu).}
$$

> [!warning] Choose one force convention consistently
> In Eq. (4.37), $\mathbf F$ excludes weight and gravity is added explicitly. In Eq. (4.40), $X,Y,Z$ include weight. Adding another $g$ to the second convention doubles gravity.

## 5. Worked derivative check (suggested)

Choose

$$
m=1000\ \mathrm{kg},\quad
[\phi,\theta,\psi]=[20^\circ,10^\circ,30^\circ],
$$

$$
\mathbf v=[50,2,3]^T\ \mathrm{m/s},\quad
\boldsymbol\omega=[0.5,0.1,0.05]^T\ \mathrm{rad/s},
$$

$$
\mathbf F=[1000,100,-9000]^T\ \mathrm N
\quad\text{(excluding gravity)}.
$$

Rotate gravity:

$$
\mathbf g^{FRD}
=9.81[-\sin10^\circ,\ \sin20^\circ\cos10^\circ,\ \cos20^\circ\cos10^\circ]^T,
$$

$$
\mathbf g^{FRD}=[-1.70348862,\ 3.30424431,\ 9.07833663]^T\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

Find the coordinate-rotation term:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v
=\begin{bmatrix}0.1(3)-0.05(2)\\0.05(50)-0.5(3)\\0.5(2)-0.1(50)\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0.2\\1\\-4\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

Add force per mass and gravity, then subtract the cross product:

$$
\dot{\mathbf v}
=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0.1\\-9\end{bmatrix}
+\begin{bmatrix}-1.70348862\\3.30424431\\9.07833663\end{bmatrix}
-\begin{bmatrix}0.2\\1\\-4\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf v}
=[-0.90348862,\ 2.40424431,\ 4.07833663]^T\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.}
$$

> [!check] Verify the convention independently
> The total force is $\mathbf F_B=[-703.48862,3404.24431,78.33663]^T$ N. Dividing by mass and subtracting the same cross product gives the same derivative. Rotating $\dot{\mathbf v}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ back to NED gives $\mathbf C^T\mathbf F/m+[0,0,g]^T=[-1.99989,\ 2.50824,\ 1.34129]^T$ m/s² both ways. All three are asserted in `generate_amc_ch4_figures.py --check`.

## 6. Worked example: forces for a steady level turn (suggested)

**Suggested, not lecturer-set.** Section 5 used Eq. 4.37 forwards: state and forces in, derivatives out. Here it runs backwards: the motion is known, so find the force that sustains it. The aircraft is the one in [[SESA3047 3.1 - Rotational Kinematics and Euler-Angle Rates#10.1 Body rates in a steady level turn (suggested)|3.1 §10.1]]. There, the turn rate came from a force balance in NED coordinates. Here the body-axis equations produce it.

**Data.** $m=60\,000$ kg, level flight at $V=100$ m/s, steady right turn at roll angle $\phi=30^\circ$, $\theta=0$. Take the velocity along the nose, $\mathbf v=[100,0,0]^T$ m/s (zero angle of attack and sideslip), and a coordinated turn, $F_y=0$.

### Step 1: body rates of a level turn

Only the heading changes, so $\dot\phi=\dot\theta=0$. The rotational kinematics of 3.1 (Eq. 3.7) give

$$
p=\dot\phi-\dot\psi\sin\theta=0,
$$

$$
q=\dot\theta\cos\phi+\dot\psi\sin\phi\cos\theta=\dot\psi\sin\phi,
$$

$$
r=-\dot\theta\sin\phi+\dot\psi\cos\phi\cos\theta=\dot\psi\cos\phi.
$$

### Step 2: steady means constant body components

The body sees the same $u,v,w$ throughout the turn, so $\dot u=\dot v=\dot w=0$. Put $v=w=0$, $p=0$ and $\theta=0$ into the scalar rows of [[#Forces excluding gravity|§4]]:

$$
0=\dot u=F_x/m-g\cdot0+r\cdot0-q\cdot0=F_x/m,
$$

$$
0=\dot v=F_y/m+g\sin\phi+0-ru,
$$

$$
0=\dot w=F_z/m+g\cos\phi+qu-0.
$$

### Step 3: the x row

$F_x=0$: thrust balances drag, as in straight flight.

### Step 4: the y row gives the turn rate

With $F_y=0$:

$$
ru=g\sin\phi.
$$

Substitute $r=\dot\psi\cos\phi$ and $u=V$:

$$
\dot\psi\cos\phi\,V=g\sin\phi,
$$

$$
\boxed{\dot\psi=\frac{g\tan\phi}{V}}=\frac{9.81\times0.577350}{100}=0.0566381\ \mathrm{rad/s}=3.245^\circ/\mathrm s.
$$

This is the result 3.1 took from Earth-axis force balance, now produced by the body-axis equation. The body rates follow:

$$
q=0.0566381\times0.5=0.0283190\ \mathrm{rad/s},\qquad
r=0.0566381\times0.866025=0.0490500\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
$$

### Step 5: the z row gives the lift

Rearrange the third row:

$$
F_z/m=-g\cos\phi-qu.
$$

Substitute $qu=\dot\psi\sin\phi\,V=g\tan\phi\sin\phi$:

$$
F_z/m=-g\cos\phi-\frac{g\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}.
$$

Put both terms over $\cos\phi$:

$$
F_z/m=-\frac{g\left(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi\right)}{\cos\phi}=-\frac{g}{\cos\phi}.
$$

Numbers:

$$
F_z/m=-\frac{9.81}{0.866025}=-11.3276\ \mathrm{m/s^2},
\qquad
F_z=-679\,657\ \mathrm N.
$$

The load factor is $n=-F_z/(mg)=1/\cos\phi=1.1547$, the familiar result for a 30° roll angle (pilots: 30° of bank). The turn radius is $R=V/\dot\psi=1766$ m, and a full turn takes $2\pi/\dot\psi=110.9$ s.

![[amc_level_turn_balance.png|800]]

> [!check] Numerical check
> With $\mathbf F=[0,0,-679\,657]^T$ N, $\mathbf g^{FRD}=[0,\ 4.905,\ 8.49571]^T$ m/s² and $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v=[0,\ ru,\ -qu]^T=[0,\ 4.905,\ -2.83190]^T$ m/s², Eq. 4.37 gives $\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf 0$ to $10^{-12}$. The transform matrix $\mathbf H$ maps $[0,\ 0.0283190,\ 0.0490500]^T$ back to Euler rates $[0,\ 0,\ 0.0566381]^T$: a pure heading change. Rotating $\dot{\mathbf v}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ to NED gives $[0,\ 5.66381,\ 0]^T$ m/s². That is $g\tan\phi=V^2/R$, horizontal and pointing east, the direction of the turn centre for a right turn heading north. All asserted in `generate_amc_ch4_figures.py --check`.

> [!tip] All of the centripetal acceleration lives in the ω × v term
> The aircraft accelerates at $0.58g$ towards the centre, yet $\dot u=\dot v=\dot w=0$. In body axes nothing changes; the acceleration is entirely the rotation of the axes carrying $\mathbf v$ with them. This is the opposite case to the turning-coordinates limit in §7. There, $\dot{\mathbf v}$ is non-zero although no force acts; here, force acts but $\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf 0$.

> [!warning] Simplifications
> Zero angle of attack puts the whole lift along $-z_b$. With $\alpha\neq0$, $w\neq0$ and the rows pick up $qw$ and $pw$ terms. The method is unchanged: set $\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf 0$ and solve for $\mathbf F$. Here $\mathbf F$ excludes weight, the Eq. 4.37 convention. In the $X,Y,Z$ convention of Eq. 4.40, weight is included, so $Y=mg\sin\phi=294\,300$ N and $Z=F_z+mg\cos\phi=-169\,914$ N. These equal $m(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v)$, exactly as Eq. 4.40 requires when $\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf 0$.

## 7. Limiting checks

- **Level, no body rotation, free fall:** $\mathbf F=\mathbf0$ gives $\dot{\mathbf v}=[0,0,g]^T$.
- **Level supported flight:** $\mathbf F=[0,0,-mg]^T$, zero body rates, gives $\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf0$.
- **Turning coordinates:** with zero physical acceleration, $\dot{\mathbf v}=-\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$; body components change while Earth velocity does not.
- **Height sign:** $h=-z$ relative to the chosen NED origin, so $\dot h=-\dot z$.
