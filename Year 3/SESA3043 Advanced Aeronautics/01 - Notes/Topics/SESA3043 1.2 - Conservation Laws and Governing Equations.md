# SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations

> [!abstract] Summary
> Apply the Reynolds transport theorem to mass, momentum and energy, then use the divergence theorem to obtain local partial differential equations. Their conservation form balances accumulation and flux against sources. Product rules plus continuity produce the material, non-conservation form. A Newtonian stress law closes the viscous momentum terms. The complete compressible system needs thermodynamic closure; for the incompressible flow used in this module, density is constant, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, energy decouples, and the model reduces to continuity plus three momentum equations.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** you derive or apply continuity, momentum or energy, or test whether a velocity field is admissible.
> - **Assumptions:** continuum, Newtonian fluid with Stokes' hypothesis; the module's reduced set is incompressible ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, energy decoupled).
> - **Method:** 1. RTT on mass, momentum or energy. 2. Divergence theorem → local PDE in conservation form. 3. Product rule plus continuity → non-conservation (material) form. 4. Close the stresses with $\tau_{ij}$. 5. Reduce to incompressible if justified.
> - **Common mistakes:** dropping the $-\tfrac23\mu\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$ term in a compressible flow; equating "incompressible" with "constant pressure".
> - **Practise:** [[#9. Worked example: stress and momentum in a linear flow (suggested)]] · [[SESA3043 Mock Questions - Chapters 1-3#Q1. Conservation laws and a viscous channel flow|Mock Q1]]

## The topic in one physical picture

Picture a tiny fixed box, $\mathrm dx\times\mathrm dy\times\mathrm dz$, in a flowing fluid, and keep three ledgers for it (L2, ll. 63–88).

- **Mass:** what is stored can change only by what flows in through the six faces minus what flows out. Nothing is created inside.
- **Momentum:** what is stored changes by the momentum carried in and out on the moving fluid, **plus** the forces acting: gravity on the contents, pressure pushing on every face, and viscous stresses dragging and stretching the faces.
- **Energy:** stored energy changes by energy carried in and out, plus heat conducted in, plus the work done by those same forces.

Each ledger, divided by the box volume and with the box shrunk to a point, is one partial differential equation. "In minus out" on two opposite faces becomes a derivative by a Taylor expansion, which is why every flux appears as $\partial(\dots)/\partial x_j$. Written for the fixed box (Eulerian), the result is the **conservation** form. Follow a moving parcel instead (Lagrangian) and the same physics reads mass × acceleration = force, the **non-conservation** form (CH1-2 slides 28–31; L2, ll. 418–423). Finally, most of this module is low-speed air or water: density is constant, so the box can neither fill up nor empty, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, and the energy ledger is no longer needed to find the velocity.

## 1. Integral-to-differential strategy

Every derivation follows the same route:

1. state conservation for a material system;
2. replace the system with a control volume using [[Reynolds Transport Theorem]];
3. write surface forces and fluxes with a consistent outward normal;
4. convert surface integrals to volume integrals with Gauss's theorem;
5. because the control volume is arbitrary, set the local integrand to zero.

The divergence theorem is

$$
\oint_{CS}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA
=\int_{CV}\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,\mathrm dV.
$$

## 2. Conservation of mass

For a material system, mass cannot change:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dm_{sys}}{\mathrm dt}=0.
$$

Set the specific transported quantity $b=1$ in the Reynolds transport theorem:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA=0.
$$

Apply Gauss:

$$
\int_{CV}\left[
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)
\right]\mathrm dV=0.
$$

The control volume is arbitrary, so

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0
}
$$

or in index form

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho u_i)}{\partial x_i}=0
}.
$$

This is the [[Continuity Equation in Conservation Form]].

### Differential-element derivation

The same equation follows directly from a fixed rectangular element $\mathrm dV=\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz$. Taylor-expand the mass flux on the two $x$ faces about the element centre:

$$
(\rho u)_{x+\mathrm dx/2}
\approx \rho u+\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}\frac{\mathrm dx}{2},
$$

$$
(\rho u)_{x-\mathrm dx/2}
\approx \rho u-\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}\frac{\mathrm dx}{2}.
$$

Outflow minus inflow through those faces is

$$
\left[(\rho u)_{x+\mathrm dx/2}-(\rho u)_{x-\mathrm dx/2}\right]\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz
=\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dV.
$$

Adding the $y$ and $z$ directions gives total net outward mass flow

$$
\left[
\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z}
\right]\mathrm dV.
$$

Mass stored in the element changes at rate $(\partial\rho/\partial t)\mathrm dV$. Accumulation plus net outflow is zero, so division by the arbitrary $\mathrm dV$ gives

$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z}=0.
$$

![[aa_continuity_differential_element.png|760]]

> [!tip] Divergence is "net outflow per unit volume"
> The bracket $\partial(\rho u_j)/\partial x_j$ is exactly the net mass **outflow** per unit volume. The lecture booked it the other way round, as net **inflow** $=-\partial(\rho u_j)/\partial x_j$, and equated that to the storage rate (L2, ll. 371–393). Either way the equation is the same: storage rate plus net outflow equals zero. Keep the sign straight by asking "if more leaves than enters, must the density inside fall?" Yes, so $\partial\rho/\partial t<0$ when $\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)>0$.

The same element carries momentum too. The step-by-step figure in [[#y- and z-momentum by the differential element (lecture homework)]] shows the full 3D sequence: element, Taylor step, continuity, then $x$, $y$ and $z$ momentum.

### Non-conservation form

Expand the divergence:

$$
\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)
=\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\rho.
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\rho
+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$

Recognise the material derivative:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{D\rho}{Dt}+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0
}.
$$

Hence the dilatation rate $\Delta$ (CH1-2 slide 34) is

$$
\boxed{
\Delta=-\frac1\rho\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf u
}.
$$

Compression has $D\rho/Dt>0$ and negative divergence; expansion has $D\rho/Dt<0$ and positive divergence.

> [!tip] What "dilatation rate" means physically
> A fluid element of fixed mass $\delta m=\rho\,\delta V$ has $D(\rho\,\delta V)/Dt=0$, so $\frac{1}{\delta V}\frac{D\,\delta V}{Dt}=-\frac1\rho\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$. The velocity divergence is the **fractional rate of change of volume** of a moving element (L2, l. 430). Example: $u=\alpha x$ with $\alpha=100$ s⁻¹ makes every element grow by 10% per millisecond. See [[#Part (b): a compressible stretching flow]].

> [!important] Non-conservation is a name, not a leak
> *"The non-conservation form doesn't mean that the mass is not conserved"* (L2, ll. 419–421). It is the same law written for a **moving** element rather than a fixed box.

## 3. Conservation of momentum

Newton's second law for a material system is

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{sys}\rho\mathbf u\,\mathrm dV
=\mathbf F_{body}+\mathbf F_{surface}.
$$

Let $\mathbf f$ be body force per unit mass. Split surface traction into pressure and viscous stress:

$$
\mathbf t=-p\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n.
$$

The integral balance is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho\mathbf u\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho\mathbf u(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA
=\int_{CV}\rho\mathbf f\,\mathrm dV
-\oint_{CS}p\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA
+\oint_{CS}\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA.
$$

Apply Gauss and localise. In index form:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(\rho u_i)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho u_i u_j)}{\partial x_j}
=\rho f_i-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
}.
$$

The free index $i$ produces the $x$, $y$ and $z$ momentum equations.

### Where each local momentum term comes from

The indexed integral equation is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho u_i\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho u_i u_j n_j\,\mathrm dA
=\int_{CV}\rho f_i\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}(-p\delta_{ij}+\tau_{ij})n_j\,\mathrm dA.
$$

Apply Gauss term by term:

$$
\oint_{CS}\rho u_i u_j n_j\,\mathrm dA
=\int_{CV}\frac{\partial(\rho u_i u_j)}{\partial x_j}\,\mathrm dV,
$$

$$
\oint_{CS}(-p\delta_{ij})n_j\,\mathrm dA
=-\int_{CV}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}\,\mathrm dV,
$$

$$
\oint_{CS}\tau_{ij}n_j\,\mathrm dA
=\int_{CV}\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}\,\mathrm dV.
$$

The pressure sign follows from inward pressure traction $-p\mathbf n$. On an $x$-aligned element,

$$
\left[p_{x-\mathrm dx/2}-p_{x+\mathrm dx/2}\right]\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz
=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dV.
$$

The explicit $x$-momentum equation is

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho u^2)}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial(\rho uv)}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial(\rho uw)}{\partial z}
=\rho f_x-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xx}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xy}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xz}}{\partial z}.
$$

The $y$ and $z$ equations follow by choosing the free index $i=2$ and $i=3$.

> [!warning] Stress sign convention on the element (L2, ll. 497–544)
> $\tau_{ij}$ is the stress **in direction $i$** on a face whose **outward normal is $+\mathbf e_j$**. A positive $\tau_{ij}$ therefore points along $+\mathbf e_i$ on the positive face and along $-\mathbf e_i$ on the opposite (negative) face. That is why the face pair always gives $+\partial\tau_{ij}/\partial x_j\,\mathrm dV$. A student asked why the two shear arrows are opposite (ll. 542–544): if they pointed the same way the element would simply be pushed, not sheared, and the net force would be twice the stress times the area, not a small difference. Pressure is the exception: it always pushes **into** each face, hence $-p\mathbf n$ and the minus sign in $-\partial p/\partial x_i$.

> [!warning] Face areas and stress labels in the transcript
> Each face pair has its own area: faces normal to $x$ have $\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz$, normal to $y$ have $\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz$, normal to $z$ have $\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy$. The transcript gives the $x$-momentum flow through the $y$ face as *"rho u x u y dx dy"* (L2, l. 460); it is $\rho u_xu_y\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz$. It also calls the stress on the $z$ faces *"tau xy"* (l. 528) before using $\tau_{xz}$ correctly (ll. 529–534). A wrong area does not change the final equation only because every term is divided by $\mathrm dV$ at the end; in an exam derivation it costs marks.

### y- and z-momentum by the differential element (lecture homework)

The lecture derived only $x$-momentum on the element and said *"y and z, you do it on your own"* (L2, ll. 444–445 and 551–552). The procedure is identical. Only the **transported component** and the **stress row** change.

**$y$-momentum.** The specific quantity is now $v$, so every face carries mass flux times $v$. Use the lecture convention: $\tau_{ij}$ is the stress in direction $i$ on a face normal to $j$.

| Face pair | $y$-momentum flow at centre face | Net inflow after Taylor expansion | Surface force in $y$ | Net force |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| normal to $x$ (area $\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz$) | $\rho u\,v\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz$ | $-\dfrac{\partial(\rho uv)}{\partial x}\mathrm dV$ | $\tau_{yx}$ (shear) | $+\dfrac{\partial\tau_{yx}}{\partial x}\mathrm dV$ |
| normal to $y$ (area $\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz$) | $\rho v\,v\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz$ | $-\dfrac{\partial(\rho v^2)}{\partial y}\mathrm dV$ | $-p$ and $\tau_{yy}$ (normal) | $\left(-\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial y}+\dfrac{\partial\tau_{yy}}{\partial y}\right)\mathrm dV$ |
| normal to $z$ (area $\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy$) | $\rho w\,v\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy$ | $-\dfrac{\partial(\rho vw)}{\partial z}\mathrm dV$ | $\tau_{yz}$ (shear) | $+\dfrac{\partial\tau_{yz}}{\partial z}\mathrm dV$ |

Each net term comes from the same two-face Taylor step as in the $x$ case. For example, for the faces normal to $y$:

$$
\left[\rho v^2-\frac{\partial(\rho v^2)}{\partial y}\frac{\mathrm dy}{2}\right]\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz
-\left[\rho v^2+\frac{\partial(\rho v^2)}{\partial y}\frac{\mathrm dy}{2}\right]\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dz
=-\frac{\partial(\rho v^2)}{\partial y}\mathrm dV.
$$

Pressure acts only on the faces normal to $y$, because pressure is always normal to a surface. Add the body force $\rho f_y\,\mathrm dV$, equate everything to the rate of change $\partial(\rho v)/\partial t\,\mathrm dV$, and cancel $\mathrm dV$:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho uv)}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial(\rho v^2)}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial(\rho vw)}{\partial z}
=\rho f_y-\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{yx}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{yy}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{yz}}{\partial z}
}.
$$

**$z$-momentum.** Transport $w$; pressure acts on the faces normal to $z$; use the stress row $\tau_{zj}$:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho uw)}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial(\rho vw)}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial(\rho w^2)}{\partial z}
=\rho f_z-\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{zx}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{zy}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{zz}}{\partial z}
}.
$$

> [!check] Pattern check
> In the $i$-th equation, every flux term is $\partial(\rho u_i u_j)/\partial x_j$, pressure appears only as $\partial p/\partial x_i$, and the stresses are row $i$ of $\boldsymbol\tau$. Collapsing all three equations reproduces the index form above. Because $\tau_{ij}=\tau_{ji}$, writing $\tau_{xy}$ instead of $\tau_{yx}$ (Anderson's convention) changes nothing.

### Material form

Expand the conservation-form terms:

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho u_i)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial(\rho u_i u_j)}{\partial x_j}
=\rho\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}+u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right)
+u_i\left[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial(\rho u_j)}{\partial x_j}\right].
$$

The bracket is zero by continuity. Hence

$$
\boxed{
\rho\frac{Du_i}{Dt}
=\rho f_i-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
}.
$$

Vector form:

$$
\boxed{
\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}
=\rho\mathbf f-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau
}.
$$

#### Component version — Revision Question 1.2 Q3

The lecture set this as homework: *"take the similar steps [as] for continuity… do it yourself at home… Revision Questions chapter 1.2, question three"* (L2, ll. 564–570). The steps are the same as for continuity: product rule, then recognise the material derivative. Written out for $x$:

**Step 1 — expand every conservation-form term with the product rule.**

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial t}=\rho\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t},
\qquad
\frac{\partial(\rho u\,u_j)}{\partial x_j}=\rho u_j\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_j}+u\frac{\partial(\rho u_j)}{\partial x_j}.
$$

Here $\rho u_j$ is kept together as the mass flux. Explicitly, the second expansion is

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho u^2)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(\rho uv)}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial(\rho uw)}{\partial z}
=\rho\left(u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right)
+u\left[\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z}\right].
$$

**Step 2 — group the terms multiplied by $u$.**

$$
\text{LHS}=\rho\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right)
+u\underbrace{\left[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)\right]}_{=0\ \text{(continuity)}}.
$$

**Step 3 — recognise the material derivative.** The first bracket is $Du/Dt$, so

$$
\boxed{
\rho\frac{Du}{Dt}
=\rho f_x-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xx}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xy}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{xz}}{\partial z}
}
$$

and likewise $\rho\,Dv/Dt$ and $\rho\,Dw/Dt$ for $y$ and $z$.

> [!important] "Non-conservation" is a name for the framework, not the physics
> Momentum is still conserved. This form describes a **moving** fluid element (Lagrangian: $\rho\,D\mathbf u/Dt$ is mass × particle acceleration, so it is Newton's second law for the particle). The conservation form describes a **fixed** control volume (Eulerian). The two are equal only because continuity removes the $u[\dots]$ bracket. Say this explicitly in an exam.

## 4. Newtonian stress tensor

For a Newtonian fluid,

$$
\tau_{ij}
=\mu\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
\right)
+\lambda\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}.
$$

With Stokes' hypothesis, $\lambda=-2\mu/3$:

$$
\boxed{
\tau_{ij}
=\mu\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
-\frac23\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}
\right)
}.
$$

Here $\delta_{ij}=1$ if $i=j$ and zero otherwise. Conservation of angular momentum gives

$$
\tau_{ij}=\tau_{ji},
$$

so the stress tensor is symmetric. The diagonal entries are viscous normal stresses; the off-diagonal entries are shear stresses.

Vector form:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau
=\mu\left[
\nabla\mathbf u+(\nabla\mathbf u)^T
-\frac23(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf I
\right].
$$

The kinematic viscosity is $\nu=\mu/\rho$.

### What Stokes' hypothesis does

Take the trace (set $j=i$ and sum) of the general Newtonian law. Since $\delta_{ii}=3$,

$$
\tau_{ii}=2\mu\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}+3\lambda\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k}.
$$

Both divergences are the same scalar $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$, so

$$
\tau_{ii}=(2\mu+3\lambda)\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf u.
$$

With $\lambda=-\tfrac23\mu$ the bracket is zero:

$$
\boxed{\tau_{ii}=\tau_{xx}+\tau_{yy}+\tau_{zz}=0}.
$$

The **mean viscous normal stress** is zero. Viscosity only distorts an element; it adds nothing to the average push on its faces, so the mean normal stress is $-p$ alone. The combination $\lambda+\tfrac23\mu$ is the **bulk viscosity**, and Stokes' hypothesis sets it to zero.

> [!tip] When λ matters, and when it does not
> $\lambda$ multiplies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$. In incompressible flow $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so the value of $\lambda$ is irrelevant and the hypothesis is never tested. It matters only in compressible flow (shock structure, sound absorption), where it is exact for dilute monatomic gases and the standard engineering approximation for air. Worked numbers: [[#Part (b): a compressible stretching flow]].

> [!warning] Symmetric is not the same as diagonal
> $\tau_{ij}=\tau_{ji}$ (L2, ll. 583–594) halves the number of independent shear stresses from six to three. It does **not** make the shear stresses zero, and it is a consequence of angular-momentum balance on the element, not of the Newtonian law. That symmetry is also why Anderson's opposite index convention gives identical equations (L2, ll. 504–507; L3, ll. 2–4).

## 5. Conservation of total energy

Define the specific total energy, $E_T$ on the slides (CH1-2 slide 70),

$$
E_T=e+\frac12u_i u_i.
$$

For a material system, the first law is

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{sys}\rho E_T\,\mathrm dV
=\dot Q+\dot W.
$$

Reynolds transport converts the left-hand side to

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho E_T\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho E_T(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA.
$$

There are three power or heat contributions:

1. body-force power

   $$\int_{CV}\rho\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf u\,\mathrm dV;$$

2. surface-traction power, using $\mathbf t=-p\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n$,

   $$\oint_{CS}\mathbf t\cdot\mathbf u\,\mathrm dA
   =\oint_{CS}\left[-p\mathbf u+(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf u)\right]\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA;$$

3. heat entering the control volume. If $\mathbf q$ is the outward conductive heat flux, this is

   $$-\oint_{CS}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA.$$

The integral energy balance is therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho E_T\,\mathrm dV
&+\oint_{CS}\rho E_T\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\\
&=\int_{CV}\rho\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf u\,\mathrm dV
-\oint_{CS}p\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA\\
&\quad+\oint_{CS}(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA
-\oint_{CS}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA.
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying Gauss to each surface term and localising gives

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho E_T)}{\partial t}
+\nabla\cdot(\rho E_T\mathbf u)
=\rho\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf u
-\nabla\cdot(p\mathbf u)
+\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf u)
-\nabla\cdot\mathbf q.
$$

> [!warning] Sign of the pressure work
> Pressure pushes **into** the surface, so its power on the control volume is $-\oint p\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA$ and its local term is $-\partial(pu_j)/\partial x_j$, as on CH1-2 slide 72. The transcript reads this term without its minus sign (L3, l. 43). Moving it to the left-hand side makes it $+$, which is how $p$ joins $\rho E_T$ to form $\rho E_T+p=\rho\left(E_T+p/\rho\right)$, the total-enthalpy flux (L3, ll. 45–49).

Move the pressure-work term to the left. This yields the energy equation in conservation form (CH1-2 slide 72):

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(\rho E_T)}{\partial t}
+\nabla\cdot[(\rho E_T+p)\mathbf u]
=\rho\mathbf f\cdot\mathbf u
+\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf u)
-\nabla\cdot\mathbf q
}.
$$

The left contains stored energy and advected energy plus pressure work. The right contains body-force work, viscous work and heat conduction. With Fourier's law, $\mathbf q=-k\nabla T$.

Equivalent internal-energy or enthalpy forms follow by combining this equation with continuity and momentum.

In index notation,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(\rho E_T)}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial[(\rho E_T+p)u_j]}{\partial x_j}
=\rho f_i u_i
+\frac{\partial(\tau_{ij}u_i)}{\partial x_j}
-\frac{\partial q_j}{\partial x_j}
}.
$$

## 6. Compact compressible conservation form

Mass, three momenta and energy form five equations:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial\mathbf Q}{\partial t}
+\frac{\partial\mathbf F_j}{\partial x_j}=\mathbf S
}.
$$

The **conservation variables** (CH1-2 slide 75; the fluxes are $\mathbf F_j$ and the sources $\mathbf S$) are

$$
\mathbf Q=
\begin{bmatrix}
\rho\\
\rho u_1\\
\rho u_2\\
\rho u_3\\
\rho E_T
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The unknowns are typically $\rho,u,v,w,p$ and a thermal variable such as $e$ or $T$. Five conservation equations alone do not close six primitive unknowns; an equation of state and caloric relation supply the thermodynamic closure.

> [!warning] "Five equations, six unknowns" is a closure statement
> The transcript says *"only five partial differential equations, can solve this"* (L3, l. 88); the meaning is **cannot** solve it as it stands. The fix is not another conservation law but thermodynamics: $p=\rho RT$ and $e=c_vT$ for a perfect gas (plus Fourier's law for $\mathbf q$ and a law for $\mu(T)$). In [[SESA3029 W01 - Compressible-Flow Toolkit, Normal Shocks and Pitot Probes|Aerothermodynamics]] these closures are always in play; here the incompressible assumption removes the need for them.

> [!important] Why the equations are difficult
> The equations are nonlinear and coupled. The open mathematical question associated with Navier–Stokes concerns global existence and smoothness, not whether engineers can compute useful approximate solutions for specified cases. (This is the Clay Institute Millennium **Prize** problem, transcribed "price", L3, ll. 75–82. The lecturer mentioned a recently announced claimed solution that was *"still being validated"*, ll. 82–86; for this module treat the problem as open.)

## 7. Incompressible reduction

For constant density,

$$
\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0}.
$$

> [!warning] Which terms vanish under which assumption (L3, ll. 93–110)
> - **Steady** removes only $\partial/\partial t$ terms. Continuity becomes $\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0$, *not* $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$: a steady compressible nozzle flow still has $\rho$ varying in space.
> - **Incompressible** (the lecture's definition: $\rho$ constant in time **and** space) removes $\partial\rho/\partial t$ and lets $\rho$ come outside every derivative, giving $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ even in unsteady flow.
> - Strictly, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ only needs $D\rho/Dt=0$ (each parcel keeps its density). A stratified ocean has $\rho$ varying from parcel to parcel yet is divergence-free. In this module "incompressible" always means one constant $\rho$.
> - **Constant $\mu$** is a separate assumption. The transcript says $\mu$ *"is constant if we have incompressible flows"* (L3, ll. 25–26); in fact $\mu$ depends on temperature, so constant $\mu$ really assumes the temperature varies little. That is normally true in the same low-speed flows, which is why the two assumptions travel together.

> [!tip] Why low-speed air counts as incompressible
> From isentropic flow, $\Delta\rho/\rho\approx\tfrac12M^2$. At $M=0.3$ (about 100 m/s at sea level) density changes by under 5%. Below that, treating $\rho$ as constant is an excellent model even though air is obviously compressible in a piston.

The momentum equation becomes

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}
+u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
=f_i-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}
+\frac1\rho\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}.
$$

For constant $\mu$, incompressibility removes the dilatational term and

$$
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
=\mu\frac{\partial^2u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}.
$$

The cancellation is explicit. Start from

$$
\tau_{ij}=\mu\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
\right),
$$

which follows because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. With constant $\mu$,

$$
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
=\mu\left[
\frac{\partial^2u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial^2u_j}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}
\right].
$$

Interchange the order of differentiation in the second term:

$$
\frac{\partial^2u_j}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}
\left(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_j}\right)
=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)=0.
$$

Only $\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u$ remains.

> [!tip] Zero net viscous force does not mean zero viscous stress
> $\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u$ is the **net** viscous force per unit volume: the difference between the stresses on opposite faces. Any flow whose velocity is linear in $x,y,z$ has $\nabla^2\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$ and so feels no net viscous force, even though every face carries a shear stress. See [[#9. Worked example: stress and momentum in a linear flow (suggested)]].

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}
+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=\mathbf f-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u
},
$$

with

$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0}.
$$

There are four unknowns, $u,v,w,p$, and four equations. The hydrodynamic field can be solved without the energy equation when temperature-dependent properties and heat transfer do not influence the flow.

> [!tip] What pressure does in incompressible flow
> With $\rho$ fixed there is no equation of state linking $p$ to $\rho$ and $T$. Pressure is no longer a thermodynamic variable: it takes whatever value makes the velocity field satisfy $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. That is why the energy equation decouples (L3, ll. 150–158) and why, in [[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]], the boundary layer simply inherits its pressure from the outer flow.

### Explicit two-dimensional incompressible equations

With $w=0$ and $\partial/\partial z=0$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0
},
$$

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
=f_x-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}
+\nu\left(
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}
\right)
},
$$

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}
+u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}
=f_y-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}
+\nu\left(
\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial y^2}
\right)
}.
$$

## 8. Model hierarchy by assumptions

| Assumption | Reduced equation |
|---|---|
| General Newtonian compressible flow | continuity + three momentum + energy + thermodynamic closure |
| Incompressible, constant $\rho$ and $\mu$ | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ and incompressible Navier–Stokes |
| Two-dimensional | $w=0$, $\partial/\partial z=0$; unknowns $u,v,p$ |
| Steady | all $\partial/\partial t=0$ |
| Inviscid | $\mu=\nu=0$; Euler equation |
| Irrotational | $\nabla\times\mathbf u=0$; introduce $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$ |

Every simplification needs an explicit physical justification.

## 9. Worked example: stress and momentum in a linear flow (suggested)

Not set in the lectures; it exercises the Newtonian stress law (L2, ll. 571–594; L3, ll. 5–28), incompressible continuity and the momentum equation on one concrete field. Air, $\rho=1.2$ kg/m³, $\mu=1.8\times10^{-5}$ Pa s. Steady 2-D field, no body force:

$$
u=Ax+By,\qquad v=Cx-Ay,\qquad w=0,
$$

with $A=200$ s⁻¹, $B=300$ s⁻¹, $C=-100$ s⁻¹. Work at $P=(x,y)=(1,2)$ cm, where $u=8$ m/s and $v=-5$ m/s.

### Part (a): continuity, stress, vorticity, pressure

**Step 1 — continuity.**

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=A+(-A)=0.
$$

The field is divergence-free, so it is a valid incompressible flow.

**Step 2 — velocity-gradient tensor** $G_{ij}=\partial u_i/\partial x_j$:

$$
G=\begin{bmatrix}A&B&0\\C&-A&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}200&300&0\\-100&-200&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

**Step 3 — stresses.** With $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ the Stokes term drops and $\tau_{ij}=\mu(G_{ij}+G_{ji})$:

$$
\tau_{xx}=2\mu A=2(1.8\times10^{-5})(200)=7.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{Pa},
$$

$$
\tau_{yy}=-2\mu A=-7.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{Pa},\qquad\tau_{zz}=0,
$$

$$
\tau_{xy}=\tau_{yx}=\mu(B+C)=1.8\times10^{-5}(200)=3.6\times10^{-3}\ \text{Pa}.
$$

The tensor is symmetric and its trace is $7.2-7.2+0=0$, as [[#What Stokes' hypothesis does]] requires.

**Step 4 — net viscous force.** Every $\tau_{ij}$ is a constant, so

$$
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u=\mathbf 0.
$$

**Step 5 — vorticity.**

$$
\omega_z=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=C-B=-400\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

The flow is rotational (elements spin clockwise), so no velocity potential exists.

**Step 6 — acceleration.** Steady, so $a_i=u_j\,\partial u_i/\partial x_j$:

$$
a_x=u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=(Ax+By)A+(Cx-Ay)B=(A^2+BC)\,x,
$$

$$
a_y=u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=(Ax+By)C+(Cx-Ay)(-A)=(A^2+BC)\,y.
$$

With $K=A^2+BC=40\,000-30\,000=10\,000$ s⁻², at $P$: $\mathbf a=(100,\,200)$ m/s².

**Step 7 — momentum gives the pressure.** With $\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$ and zero net viscous force, $\rho\,D\mathbf u/Dt=-\nabla p$:

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}=-\rho Kx=-120\ \text{Pa/m},\qquad\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}=-\rho Ky=-240\ \text{Pa/m}\quad\text{at }P.
$$

Both components integrate to one function (the cross-derivatives agree), so a pressure field exists:

$$
p=p_0-\tfrac12\rho K\left(x^2+y^2\right),
$$

$$
p_P-p_0=-\tfrac12(1.2)(10\,000)(0.01^2+0.02^2)=-3.0\ \text{Pa}.
$$

**Step 8 — do not use Bernoulli across streamlines.** Bernoulli from the stagnation point at the origin would give $p_P-p_0=-\tfrac12\rho q^2=-\tfrac12(1.2)(8^2+5^2)=-53.4$ Pa, wrong by a factor of 18. Steady inviscid momentum still makes $p+\tfrac12\rho q^2$ constant **along each streamline**, but the constant differs from one streamline to the next because $\omega_z\neq0$: it is $p_0+50.4$ Pa on the streamline through $P$ and $p_0+36.0$ Pa on the one through $(2,1)$ cm. The figure shows circular isobars cutting across the hyperbolic streamlines.

![[aa_linear_flow_stress_example.png|700]]

> [!tip] How big are the viscous stresses?
> $\tau_{xy}=3.6$ mPa, while the pressure changes by 3 Pa over 2 cm: more than 800 times larger. In air, velocity gradients of a few hundred per second produce tiny viscous stresses. Viscosity only competes with inertia where gradients are huge, which is inside thin boundary layers ([[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]]).

### Part (b): a compressible stretching flow

Now let $u=\alpha x$, $v=w=0$ with $\alpha=100$ s⁻¹ (same air). Then $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\alpha=100$ s⁻¹ and the Stokes term is active.

$$
\tau_{xx}=\mu\left(2\alpha-\tfrac23\alpha\right)=\tfrac43\mu\alpha=2.4\times10^{-3}\ \text{Pa},
$$

$$
\tau_{yy}=\tau_{zz}=\mu\left(0-\tfrac23\alpha\right)=-\tfrac23\mu\alpha=-1.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{Pa}.
$$

The trace is $2.4-1.2-1.2=0$. Note that $\tau_{yy}\neq0$ even though $v\equiv0$: the $-\tfrac23\mu(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)$ term acts on every normal stress. Had we set $\lambda=0$ instead, $\tau_{xx}=3.6$ mPa and $\tau_{yy}=\tau_{zz}=0$, a mean viscous normal stress of $+1.2$ mPa that would act like a spurious change of pressure.

Continuity in non-conservation form:

$$
\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=-(1.2)(100)=-120\ \text{kg/(m}^3\,\text{s)}.
$$

Each element's density falls, and its volume grows, by 10% per millisecond.

> [!check] Python check (NumPy and SymPy)
> (a) $\mathrm{tr}\,G=0$; $\tau=\begin{bmatrix}7.2&3.6&0\\3.6&-7.2&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\times10^{-3}$ Pa, symmetric, trace 0; $\omega_z=-400$ s⁻¹; symbolic $\mathbf a=(10\,000x,\,10\,000y)$, $\nabla^2u=0$; at $P$: $\mathbf u=(8,-5)$ m/s, $\mathbf a=(100,200)$ m/s², $\nabla p=(-120,-240)$ Pa/m, $\partial a_x/\partial y-\partial a_y/\partial x=0$. Integrating particle paths with an ODE solver from $P$ for 2 ms: $p-p_0+\tfrac12\rho q^2=50.400$ Pa to $10^{-12}$ along the path; $36.0$ Pa at $(2,1)$ cm ✓.
> (b) $\mathrm{diag}\,\tau=(2.4,-1.2,-1.2)\times10^{-3}$ Pa, trace $4\times10^{-19}$ (round-off); with $\lambda=0$: $(3.6,0,0)\times10^{-3}$ Pa, mean $1.2\times10^{-3}$ Pa; $D\rho/Dt=-120$ kg/(m³ s) ✓.
