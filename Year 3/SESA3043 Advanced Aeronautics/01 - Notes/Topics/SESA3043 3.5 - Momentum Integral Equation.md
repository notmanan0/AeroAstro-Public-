# SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation

> [!abstract] Summary
> For many problems the details of $u(x,y)$ matter less than a few overall numbers: $\delta^*$ (the effective body) and $\theta$ (the friction drag). Integrating the BL equations across the layer removes $y$ and leaves one ODE in $x$, the **von Kármán momentum integral equation (MIE)**:
>
> $$\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}+(2+H)\frac{\theta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{C_f}{2}.$$
>
> No assumption about the profile was made, so it holds for **laminar and turbulent** layers alike. But it has three unknowns ($\theta$, $H$, $C_f$), so it must be closed with an assumed profile (Pohlhausen), empirical correlations (Thwaites, turbulent power laws) or a similarity solution. For a flat plate it reduces to $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$, the result found by control volume in 3.1. The ship-hull example shows its power: knowing only roughness height, length and speed, one can estimate the friction penalty of biofouling.

## The topic in one physical picture

Draw a thin slice of boundary layer of length $\mathrm dx$, reaching from the wall up into the outer flow. Do the momentum accounts for it:

- more momentum **leaves** through the downstream face than through the upstream one if the layer is getting faster, less if it is losing momentum to the wall;
- mass also enters through the top (the layer entrains outer fluid), carrying edge momentum $U_e$;
- the pressure pushes on the slice according to $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$;
- the wall pulls back with $\tau_w\,\mathrm dx$.

The MIE is exactly this balance, written in terms of $\theta$ and $\delta^*$. The $2\theta$ in $(2+H)\theta$ comes from momentum changes with $U_e$; the $H\theta=\delta^*$ term from the pressure force acting on the displacement thickness.

## 1. The derivation, step by step (slides 5–38)

Start from the 2-D BL equations (1.4):

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,\qquad(1)
$$

$$
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.\qquad(2)
$$

Write $U_e'=\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$; recall $U_e=U_e(x)$ only.

### Step 1: combine continuity and momentum (slide 9)

Multiply (1) by $(u-U_e)$. Since (1) is zero, so is this:

$$
(u-U_e)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+(u-U_e)\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0.\qquad(3)
$$

Add (3) to (2):

$$
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+(u-U_e)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+(u-U_e)\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=U_eU_e'+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.\qquad(4)
$$

### Step 2: rewrite two terms with the product rule (slides 11–19)

The first term (red on the slides): since $\partial(u-U_e)/\partial x=\partial u/\partial x-U_e'$,

$$
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=u\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u-U_e)+uU_e'.
$$

The third term (blue): $U_e$ does not depend on $y$, so

$$
v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=v\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(u-U_e).
$$

(4) becomes

$$
\underbrace{u\frac{\partial(u-U_e)}{\partial x}}_{①}+\underbrace{uU_e'}_{②}+\underbrace{(u-U_e)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{③}+\underbrace{v\frac{\partial(u-U_e)}{\partial y}}_{④}+\underbrace{(u-U_e)\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}}_{⑤}=\underbrace{U_eU_e'}_{⑥}+\underbrace{\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}}_{⑦}.
$$

### Step 3: group into exact derivatives (slide 20)

- ①+③ is a product rule in $x$: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[u(u-U_e)\right]$.
- ④+⑤ is a product rule in $y$: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[v(u-U_e)\right]$.
- Move ⑥ to the left and combine with ②: $uU_e'-U_eU_e'=(u-U_e)U_e'$.

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[u(u-U_e)\right]+(u-U_e)U_e'+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[v(u-U_e)\right]=\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
$$

### Step 4: make the integrands dimensionless (slide 20)

Multiply and divide the first term by $U_e^2$ and the second by $U_e$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[U_e^2\frac{u}{U_e}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}-1\right)\right]+\left(\frac{u}{U_e}-1\right)U_eU_e'+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[v(u-U_e)\right]=\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
$$

### Step 5: integrate over 0 < y < ∞, term by term (slides 24–36)

Label the four integrals I, II, III, IV.

**Term I.** The limits do not depend on $x$, so $\partial/\partial x$ comes out of the integral as $\mathrm d/\mathrm dx$ (Leibniz); and $\frac{u}{U_e}(\frac{u}{U_e}-1)=-\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})$:

$$
\text{I}=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left[U_e^2\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}-1\right)\mathrm dy\right]=-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left(U_e^2\theta\right).
$$

**Term II.** $U_eU_e'$ does not depend on $y$:

$$
\text{II}=U_eU_e'\int_0^\infty\left(\frac{u}{U_e}-1\right)\mathrm dy=-U_eU_e'\,\delta^*.
$$

**Term III.** An exact derivative, so evaluate at the limits:

$$
\text{III}=\big[v(u-U_e)\big]_0^\infty=v_e(U_e-U_e)-0\cdot(0-U_e)=0.
$$

At the edge $u=U_e$ (even though $v_e\neq0$); at the wall $v=0$.

**Term IV.**

$$
\text{IV}=\nu\left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right]_0^\infty=\nu\left(0-\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_0\right)=-\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}.
$$

### Step 6: assemble (slides 36–37)

I + II + III = IV:

$$
-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left(U_e^2\theta\right)-U_eU_e'\delta^*=-\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}.
$$

Change sign:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left(U_e^2\theta\right)+U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}\delta^*=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}.
$$

Expand the first term with the product rule:

$$
U_e^2\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}+2\theta U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+U_e\delta^*\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}.
$$

Divide by $U_e^2$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}+\frac{(2\theta+\delta^*)}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_e^2}.
$$

Use $\delta^*=H\theta$ and $\tau_w/\rho U_e^2=C_f/2$:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}+(2+H)\frac{\theta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{C_f}{2}}
$$

## 2. What it means (slides 37–39)

- **Two PDEs in $(x,y)$ become one ODE in $x$** (slide 37).
- **No assumption about $u(x,y)$** was made, so the MIE applies to **laminar and turbulent** layers (slide 38). (For turbulent flow, $u$ is the time mean and $\tau_w$ the mean wall stress.)
- **It is not closed**: three unknowns, $\theta$, $H$, $C_f$. Every integral method supplies $H$ and $C_f$ as functions of $\theta$ and the local conditions.
- **Flat plate** ($\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=0$):

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{C_f}{2},
$$

as found in [[SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures#5. Wall shear, skin friction and the drag link (slide 32)|3.1]] by control volume and confirmed by Blasius in [[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions|3.2]].

> [!check] The flat-plate MIE against Blasius (Python)
> Blasius gives $\theta=0.664\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}=0.664\sqrt{\nu x/U_e}$, so $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=0.332\sqrt{\nu/(U_ex)}=0.332/\sqrt{Re_x}$, which is exactly $C_f/2$ with $C_f=0.664/\sqrt{Re_x}$. In numbers, at $U_e=10$ m/s, $\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s, $x=0.5$ m: $Re_x=3.333\times10^5$, $\theta=0.5750$ mm, $C_f/2=5.750\times10^{-4}$, and a central difference of $\theta(x)$ over $\pm1$ mm gives $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=5.750\times10^{-4}$.

> [!warning] The flat-plate form needs zero pressure gradient, not a flat plate
> $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$ needs $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=0$. A thin aerofoil at incidence is "nearly flat" but has strong pressure gradients near its leading edge, so the full MIE applies there. Conversely, the flat-plate form is fine on any surface where $U_e$ is constant, such as most of a long ship hull (§4).

> [!warning] Local versus mean skin friction
> $C_f(x)=\tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho U_e^2)$ is **local**. Drag needs the **mean** over the length, $\overline{C_f}=\frac1L\int_0^LC_f\,\mathrm dx$, and for a flat plate the MIE turns that integral into one number: $\overline{C_f}=2\theta(L)/L$ (§4). For laminar Blasius flow $\overline{C_f}=1.328/\sqrt{Re_L}$, exactly twice the local value at the trailing edge, because $C_f\propto x^{-1/2}$.

> [!tip] Reading the pressure-gradient term
> In an adverse gradient ($U_e'<0$) the term $(2+H)\frac{\theta}{U_e}U_e'$ is negative, so $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2+(\text{positive})$: the layer thickens faster than friction alone would make it, and $H$ rises. Near separation $C_f\to0$ and the growth is driven entirely by the pressure gradient.

## 3. Closing the MIE

| Closure | $H$ and $C_f$ from | Accuracy (laminar flat plate $\theta\sqrt{Re_x}/x$, exact 0.664) |
|---|---|---|
| linear profile | $H=3$, $C_f=2\nu/U_e\delta$ | 0.577 (−13%) |
| Pohlhausen quartic, $\lambda=0$ | §3 of [[SESA3043 3.3 - Pohlhausen Method and Pressure Gradients\|3.3]] | 0.685 (+3%) |
| Thwaites (correlation) | fits to exact solutions | 0.671 (+1%) |
| turbulent power law, $H=9/7$ | empirical $C_f$ | turbulent; see 3.6 Q3 |

### Thwaites' method (extension; mentioned on CH3-6 slide 3)

Multiply the MIE by $U_e\theta/\nu$ and write $\lambda_\theta=\frac{\theta^2}{\nu}U_e'$. Exact laminar solutions show that $\frac{U_e}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm d\theta^2}{\mathrm dx}\approx0.45-6\lambda_\theta$ for all of them. This linear form integrates exactly:

$$
\theta^2(x)=\frac{0.45\,\nu}{U_e^6}\int_0^xU_e^5\,\mathrm dx.
$$

For a flat plate: $\theta^2=0.45\nu x/U_e$, so $\theta/x=0.671/\sqrt{Re_x}$. Separation is predicted at $\lambda_\theta\approx-0.09$. Thwaites is what XFOIL-type codes use for laminar layers ([[SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction|3.6]]).

## 4. Example: drag penalty from ship-hull fouling (slides 40–44)

- **Problem:** seaweed and barnacles roughen a hull and raise $C_f$; friction drag is proportional to fuel burn. Measuring drag, $\tau_w$ or $u$ on a ship in service is impractical. Can the penalty $\Delta C_f$ relative to a smooth hull, $C_{f_s}$, be estimated without in-situ measurement?
- **Method** (Monty et al., 2016): the MIE for a flat plate plus an assumed **turbulent** similarity profile (log law with a roughness shift), valid at the very high $Re_x$ of ships ($10^8$–$10^{10}$).
- **Inputs:** only the characteristic fouling height $k_s$ (from scanning a sample of fouled surface and lab BL measurements), the ship length $L$ and the operating speed $U_\infty$.
- **Output:** a chart of $\overline{C_f}$ against $Re_x$ for smooth and rough walls of given $k_s$, a Moody chart for external flows (slide 44, $k_s=0.325$ mm). Example points on it: a frigate (FFG-7) at cruise and full speed, and a VLCC tanker at cruise.

The point of the example: because the MIE needs only integral quantities, a small-scale lab measurement of $k_s$ can be scaled up to a full ship.

**What slide 44 shows** (read from a 330 dpi render):
- the **smooth** curve, e.g. $\overline{C_f}\approx2.03\times10^{-3}$ at $Re_x=10^8$, $1.49\times10^{-3}$ at $1.07\times10^9$, $1.06\times10^{-3}$ at $2\times10^{10}$;
- black **rough** curves at fixed unit Reynolds number $U_\infty/\nu=1.2,\ 2.2,\ 4.7,\ 8.6,\ 17.2\times10^6$ m⁻¹: $\overline{C_f}$ over a hull of growing length $x$ at one speed;
- grey curves at fixed **length** $L=50,\ 124,\ 250,\ 500$ m as the speed rises: they leave the smooth curve and go **flat**, at $\overline{C_f}\approx2.50,\ 2.17,\ 1.95,\ 1.77\times10^{-3}$;
- the FFG-7 ($L=124$ m) at cruise ($U_\infty/\nu=8.6\times10^6$ m⁻¹, $Re_L\approx1.07\times10^9$) and at full speed ($17.2\times10^6$ m⁻¹, $Re_L\approx2.1\times10^9$), both at $\overline{C_f}\approx2.17\times10^{-3}$; the smooth value at cruise, $\overline{C_{f_s}}\approx1.49\times10^{-3}$, is marked with the $\Delta\overline{C_f}$ arrow;
- a VLCC tanker at cruise, near $Re_L\approx3.1\times10^9$, $\overline{C_f}\approx1.88\times10^{-3}$.

### The method behind the chart

The slides give the method only by reference (Monty et al. 2016, with MATLAB code). Its core is the lectured MIE; only the closure is new.

**Step 1: drag from θ alone.** Flat-plate MIE (§2):

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{C_f}{2}=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_\infty^2}.
$$

Drag per unit width of a hull of length $L$:

$$
D'=\int_0^L\tau_w\,\mathrm dx.
$$

Substitute $\tau_w=\rho U_\infty^2\,\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx$:

$$
D'=\rho U_\infty^2\int_0^L\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}\,\mathrm dx=\rho U_\infty^2\left[\theta(L)-\theta(0)\right]=\rho U_\infty^2\,\theta(L),
$$

which is the 22 Oct result $D=\rho U_e^2\theta$ (ll. 227–230). Divide by $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2L$:

$$
\boxed{\overline{C_f}=\frac{2\,\theta(L)}{L}}.
$$

So the whole drag question is: **how thick is the momentum deficit at the stern?**

**Step 2: a turbulent similarity profile** (the closure; beyond the lectured laminar ones). In wall units, $u^+=u/u_\tau$, $y^+=yu_\tau/\nu$, $u_\tau=\sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}$, the log law plus Coles' wake with a roughness shift $\Delta U^+$:

$$
u^+=\frac1\kappa\ln y^++A-\Delta U^++\frac\Pi\kappa W\!\left(\frac y\delta\right),\qquad W(\eta)=2\sin^2\frac{\pi\eta}{2}.
$$

**Step 3: the edge condition.** At $y=\delta$, $W=2$ and $u=U_\infty$. Write $S\equiv U_\infty/u_\tau$; since $C_f=2u_\tau^2/U_\infty^2$, $S=\sqrt{2/C_f}$:

$$
S=\frac1\kappa\ln\delta^++A-\Delta U^++\frac{2\Pi}{\kappa}.
$$

**Step 4: θ from the profile.** Subtract the profile from the edge value. The log-law constants cancel and leave the velocity **defect**, with $\eta=y/\delta$:

$$
S-u^+=-\frac1\kappa\ln\eta+\frac\Pi\kappa\left(2-W(\eta)\right)\equiv\mathcal D(\eta).
$$

So $u/U_\infty=1-\mathcal D/S$. Put this into the definition of $\theta$:

$$
\frac\theta\delta=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac{\mathcal D}{S}\right)\frac{\mathcal D}{S}\,\mathrm d\eta=\frac1S\int_0^1\mathcal D\,\mathrm d\eta-\frac{1}{S^2}\int_0^1\mathcal D^2\,\mathrm d\eta.
$$

With $\int_0^1(-\ln\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta=1$ and $\int_0^1(2-W)\,\mathrm d\eta=2-1=1$:

$$
\frac\theta\delta=\frac{1+\Pi}{\kappa S}-\frac{I_2}{S^2},\qquad I_2=\int_0^1\mathcal D^2\,\mathrm d\eta=26.89\quad(\kappa=0.384,\ \Pi=0.5).
$$

**Step 5: the fully rough wall.** For sand-grain roughness well above the viscous sublayer (Nikuradse), the log law becomes $u^+=\frac1\kappa\ln(y/k_s)+8.5$, independent of $\nu$. Matching with Step 2 gives the shift:

$$
\Delta U^+=\frac1\kappa\ln k_s^++A-8.5,\qquad k_s^+=\frac{k_su_\tau}{\nu}.
$$

Substitute into the edge condition (Step 3):

$$
S=\frac1\kappa\ln\delta^++A-\frac1\kappa\ln k_s^+-A+8.5+\frac{2\Pi}{\kappa}.
$$

The $A$ terms cancel and $\ln\delta^+-\ln k_s^+=\ln(\delta/k_s)$:

$$
\boxed{S=\frac1\kappa\ln\frac{\delta}{k_s}+8.5+\frac{2\Pi}{\kappa}}.
$$

**Viscosity has gone.** On a fully rough hull the friction depends only on $\delta/k_s$, so $\overline{C_f}$ depends only on $L/k_s$, not on speed. That is why the grey lines on slide 44 go flat, and why the FFG-7 has the same $\overline{C_f}$ at cruise and full speed. Physically, the drag is now the pressure drag of the roughness elements themselves, not viscous shear.

**Step 6: march down the hull.** At each $x$: from $Re_\theta=U_\infty\theta/\nu$ find $S$ by solving $Re_\theta=(\theta/\delta)(S)\cdot\delta^+\cdot S$ (Steps 3–5), then step

$$
\frac{\mathrm dRe_\theta}{\mathrm dRe_x}=\frac{C_f}{2}=\frac{1}{S^2},
$$

from the bow (turbulent from the start) to the stern, and finish with $\overline{C_f}=2Re_\theta(L)/Re_L$. This is the reproduction in panel (a) below, with $\kappa=0.384$, $A=4.17$, $\Pi=0.5$, and $\Delta U^+=0$ while the Step 5 shift is negative (hydraulically smooth).

### Worked numbers: the FFG-7 frigate at cruise

**Data** (slide 44): $k_s=0.325$ mm, $L=124$ m, $U_\infty/\nu=8.6\times10^6$ m⁻¹. For dimensional values take sea water with $\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}$ m²/s and $\rho=1025$ kg/m³, so $U_\infty=8.6$ m/s (16.7 kn).

Hull Reynolds number:

$$
Re_L=\frac{U_\infty}{\nu}L=8.6\times10^6\times124=1.066\times10^9.
$$

Roughness scales:

$$
\frac{k_sU_\infty}{\nu}=0.325\times10^{-3}\times8.6\times10^6=2795,\qquad\frac{L}{k_s}=\frac{124}{0.325\times10^{-3}}=3.82\times10^5.
$$

**Read the chart:** smooth $\overline{C_{f_s}}=1.49\times10^{-3}$; fouled $\overline{C_f}=2.17\times10^{-3}$.

Penalty:

$$
\Delta\overline{C_f}=2.17\times10^{-3}-1.49\times10^{-3}=0.68\times10^{-3}.
$$

As a fraction of the smooth hull:

$$
\frac{\Delta\overline{C_f}}{\overline{C_{f_s}}}=\frac{0.68}{1.49}=0.46.
$$

Fouling raises the friction drag, and so (slide 42) the fuel burned against friction, by about **46 %**.

**Is the hull fully rough?** From the march, $S=32.97$ at the stern. Then

$$
k_s^+=\frac{k_sU_\infty/\nu}{S}=\frac{2795}{32.97}=84.8,
$$

above the usual fully rough threshold ($k_s^+\gtrsim70$), so Step 5 applies. Check it with the stern boundary-layer thickness from the march, $\delta=1.44$ m:

$$
S=\frac{1}{0.384}\ln\frac{1438}{0.325}+8.5+\frac{2\times0.5}{0.384}=21.86+8.5+2.60=32.97.\ \checkmark
$$

**In newtons.** Dynamic pressure:

$$
q=\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2=\tfrac12\times1025\times8.6^2=37\,905\ \text{Pa}.
$$

Mean wall shear, smooth and fouled:

$$
\bar\tau_{w,s}=q\,\overline{C_{f_s}}=56.5\ \text{Pa},\qquad\bar\tau_w=q\,\overline{C_f}=82.4\ \text{Pa}.
$$

So every 1000 m² of fouled wetted hull adds $26.0$ kN of friction drag, which at 8.6 m/s is $26.0\times8.6=0.22$ MW of extra propulsive power.

**At full speed** ($U_\infty/\nu=17.2\times10^6$ m⁻¹, $Re_L=2.13\times10^9$) the fouled $\overline{C_f}$ is unchanged (fully rough) but the smooth one falls to $1.37\times10^{-3}$, so the penalty grows to $0.80\times10^{-3}$, or **59 %**.

> - $q=37\,904$ Pa; $\bar\tau_w=56.48$ and $82.44$ Pa; extra $25.97$ Pa, i.e. $25.97$ kN and $0.223$ MW per 1000 m².
> - Full speed: smooth $1.3709\times10^{-3}$, fouled $2.1751\times10^{-3}$ (same $\theta=134.9$ mm), $\Delta=0.8043\times10^{-3}$ (58.7 %); $k_s^+=169.6$ at the stern.

> [!warning] What the estimate leaves out
> - **$k_s$ is an equivalent sand-grain height**, not the physical height of the fouling. The scanned coupon on slides 40–42 has peaks up to about 1 mm, yet behaves like sand of 0.325 mm. That equivalence is what the off-site lab measurement provides (slide 43).
> - **Flat plate:** no pressure gradient, no hull curvature; the $(2+H)$ term of the MIE is dropped. Reasonable along most of a long, slender hull, not at the bow and stern.
> - **Friction only.** Wave-making and form drag are not in $\overline{C_f}$, so the 46 % is a penalty on the friction part of the fuel bill, not on all of it.
> - **Turbulent from the bow, uniform fouling** and assumed profile constants. The result is an estimate; its strength is that it needs no measurement on the ship.
> - **The axis says $Re_x$:** for a ship's point on the chart, $x=L$.

> [!tip] Why a rough wall is so costly
> On the smooth hull, $C_f$ keeps falling as the layer grows (panel b, black): the viscous sublayer thickens and shields the wall. Roughness elements that poke through the sublayer ($k_s^+=85$ here) shed wakes and feel pressure drag instead, so the local $C_f$ stays high all the way to the stern (orange). The longer and faster the ship, the more the smooth $C_f$ falls, and the larger the **relative** penalty.

## 5. Summary of the governing equations so far (slide 45)

| Model | Unknowns | Equations | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-D compressible N–S | $\rho,u,v,w,p,E_T$ | 5 PDEs | elliptic (hyperbolic if supersonic) |
| 3-D incompressible N–S | $u,v,w,p$ | 4 PDEs | elliptic |
| 2-D incompressible N–S | $u,v,p$ | 3 PDEs | elliptic |
| 2-D BL | $u,v$ ($p$ from potential flow) | 2 PDEs | parabolic |
| similarity solution | $f(\eta)$ | one 3rd-order ODE | shooting |
| **momentum integral** | $\theta(x)$ | **one 1st-order ODE** | marching in $x$ |
| potential flow | $\phi,p$ | Laplace + Bernoulli | elliptic |

## 6. Common mistakes

1. Forgetting that term III vanishes because $u=U_e$ at the edge, not because $v_e=0$ (it is not).
2. Writing $2+H$ as $2H$ or $H+1$.
3. Using the MIE without a closure and claiming a solution.
4. Thinking the MIE is laminar-only.
5. Quoting the local $C_f$ at the trailing edge as the drag coefficient: drag needs the mean, $\overline{C_f}=2\theta(L)/L$.
6. Reading a fully rough $\overline{C_f}$ as speed-dependent: once $k_s^+\gtrsim70$ it depends only on $L/k_s$.
