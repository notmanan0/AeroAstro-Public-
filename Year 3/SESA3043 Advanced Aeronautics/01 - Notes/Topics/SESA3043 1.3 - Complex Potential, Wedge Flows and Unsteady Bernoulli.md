# SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli

> [!abstract] Summary
> Laplace's equation is linear, so elementary potential flows can be **superposed**. Uniform flow plus a doublet gives flow past a cylinder of radius $R=\sqrt{\mu/2\pi U}$, with surface speed $2U\sin\theta$ and $C_p=1-4\sin^2\theta$. Adding a point vortex gives the lifting cylinder, with lift $\rho U|\Gamma|$. In 2-D the velocity potential and streamfunction combine into one **complex potential** $\Phi(z)=\phi+i\psi$ of $z=x+iy$. Its single ordinary derivative gives both velocity components, $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=u-iv$, and the Cauchy–Riemann equations that make this work are exactly the definitions of $\phi$ and $\psi$. A source–sink pair at any angle $\nu$ collapses to the **general doublet** $\Phi=-\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi}\dfrac{e^{i\nu}}{z}$. Sometimes no superposition is needed at all: the single function $\Phi=-kz^{m+1}$ describes flow past a **wedge** of angle $\beta\pi$ with $\beta=2m/(m+1)$, a flat plate ($m=0$), stagnation flow ($m=1$), or flow into a corner ($m>1$). The wall speed $U_e\propto s^m$ is the Falkner–Skan edge velocity used in boundary-layer theory. Finally, keeping $\partial\mathbf u/\partial t$ in the momentum equation gives the **unsteady Bernoulli equation** $\partial\phi/\partial t+q^2/2+p/\rho=C(t)$. It explains start-up transients and the extra force on bodies in accelerating flow.

## The whole topic in one physical picture

Potential flow is a game with one rule: find a harmonic function whose streamlines include the body surface.

1. **Superposition** lets you build such functions from a kit of parts: uniform flow, sources, vortices and doublets. Choose the strengths so that one streamline closes around a body shape, and you have the flow around that body.
2. **Complex variables** make the kit much more powerful. *Any* differentiable function of $z=x+iy$ automatically has harmonic real and imaginary parts. So every analytic function is a potential flow: the only question left is which body it describes.
3. **Reading the body off** is always the same move: set $\psi=0$ (or any constant) and see what curve it draws. A doublet in a stream draws a circle. A power of $z$ draws a wedge.
4. **Bernoulli then gives the pressure**, and integrating pressure gives forces. Steady potential flow gives zero drag (d'Alembert). Unsteady potential flow does not: an accelerating body must also accelerate the fluid around it.

## 1. Flow past a cylinder by superposition

The lecture redoes this Year 2 result as a template for later problems (CH1-3 slides 22–29; L4, ll. 87–155). The figure carries every step; the text adds the reasoning.

![[aa_cylinder_flow_derivation.png|900]]

### Step 1: superpose uniform flow and a doublet

Uniform flow in $+x$ and a doublet with its source upstream of its sink (the table's doublet, $\nu=\pi$):

$$
\phi=Ux+\frac{\mu\cos\theta}{2\pi r},\qquad \psi=Uy-\frac{\mu\sin\theta}{2\pi r}.
$$

Convert the uniform-flow terms to polar with $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$, then factor:

$$
\phi=Ur\cos\theta+\frac{\mu\cos\theta}{2\pi r}=Ur\cos\theta\left(1+\frac{\mu}{2\pi Ur^2}\right),
$$

$$
\psi=Ur\sin\theta-\frac{\mu\sin\theta}{2\pi r}=Ur\sin\theta\left(1-\frac{\mu}{2\pi Ur^2}\right).
$$

### Step 2: the body is a streamline

Flow cannot cross a solid wall, so the wall is a streamline: set $\psi=0$ (L4, ll. 109–119). The product vanishes when

- $r=0$: the singularity itself;
- $\sin\theta=0$, i.e. $\theta=n\pi$: the $x$-axis (the dividing streamline);
- $\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi Ur^2}=1$: a **circle** of radius

$$
\boxed{R=\left(\frac{\mu}{2\pi U}\right)^{1/2}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\mu=2\pi UR^2.
$$

Substitute $\mu/(2\pi U)=R^2$ back:

$$
\phi=Ur\cos\theta\left(1+\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right),\qquad \psi=Ur\sin\theta\left(1-\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right).
$$

> [!warning] Slide and transcript slip: the sign inside the potential
> Slide 23 (and L4, l. 125, reading it out) writes $\phi=Ur\cos\theta\left(1-R^2/r^2\right)$. That is the bracket of $\psi$, not $\phi$. Step 1 has a **plus** sign. Only the plus version reproduces the slide's own Step 3 velocities: $\partial_r[U\cos\theta(r+R^2/r)]=U\cos\theta(1-R^2/r^2)$ ✓, whereas $\partial_r[U\cos\theta(r-R^2/r)]=U\cos\theta(1+R^2/r^2)$, which would not vanish on the wall.

### Step 3: velocity

$$
q_r=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}=U\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r+\frac{R^2}{r}\right)=U\cos\theta\left(1-\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right),
$$

$$
q_\theta=\frac1r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}=\frac1r\,U\left(r+\frac{R^2}{r}\right)(-\sin\theta)=-U\sin\theta\left(1+\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right).
$$

On the surface $r=R$:

$$
q_r=0\quad(\text{no flow through the wall}),\qquad \boxed{q_\theta=-2U\sin\theta}.
$$

The minus sign only says the flow over the top ($\theta=90^\circ$) moves in the $-\theta$ direction, i.e. left to right as seen from above. The speed peaks at $2U$ at the shoulders.

> [!check] Far field and Laplace
> As $r\to\infty$, $q_r\to U\cos\theta$ and $q_\theta\to-U\sin\theta$: the uniform stream ✓. And $\nabla^2\phi=\phi_{rr}+\phi_r/r+\phi_{\theta\theta}/r^2$ with $\phi=U\cos\theta(r+R^2r^{-1})$ gives $U\cos\theta\,[2R^2r^{-3}+(1-R^2r^{-2})/r-(r+R^2r^{-1})/r^2]=U\cos\theta\,[2R^2-R^2-R^2]/r^3=0$ ✓.

### Step 4: pressure coefficient

Bernoulli from the free stream to the surface, along the dividing streamline (valid everywhere anyway, because the flow is irrotational):

$$
p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho U^2=p+\tfrac12\rho q_\theta^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p-p_\infty=\tfrac12\rho U^2-\tfrac12\rho q_\theta^2.
$$

Divide by $\tfrac12\rho U^2$ and insert $q_\theta^2=4U^2\sin^2\theta$:

$$
C_p=\frac{p-p_\infty}{\tfrac12\rho U^2}=1-\frac{q_\theta^2}{U^2}=\boxed{1-4\sin^2\theta}.
$$

| Point | $\theta$ | $q/U$ | $C_p$ |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| front and rear stagnation | $180^\circ$, $0^\circ$ | 0 | $+1$ |
| $C_p=0$ (local speed equals $U$) | $\pm30^\circ$, $\pm150^\circ$ | 1 | 0 |
| shoulders | $\pm90^\circ$ | 2 | $-3$ |

### Zero drag and zero lift (derivation)

Force per unit span from surface pressure, with outward normal $\mathbf e_r$:

$$
F_x=-\oint p\cos\theta\,R\,\mathrm d\theta,\qquad F_y=-\oint p\sin\theta\,R\,\mathrm d\theta.
$$

With $p=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho U^2(1-4\sin^2\theta)$, the constant parts integrate to zero against $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. The remaining integrals are $\oint\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=0$ and $\oint\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=0$, by symmetry. So $F_x=F_y=0$: **[[D'Alembert's Paradox]]**. The pressure distribution is symmetric front to back.

> [!note] Reality (slides 26–29; L4, ll. 145–154)
> Measured $C_p$ (Achenbach) follows the inviscid curve on the front, then **separates** and stays roughly flat over the rear at negative $C_p$. Nothing recovers to $+1$ at the back, so a real cylinder has large pressure drag. Where it separates depends on Reynolds number: laminar separation near $80^\circ$ from the front at subcritical $Re$, later after the boundary layer turns turbulent (the "drag crisis"). This is the motivation for the boundary-layer theory of [[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]]. See [[Boundary Layer Separation]].

## 2. Other bodies by superposition

Slide 30 lists three more (L4, ll. 156–162). All were met in [[SESA2022 T3 - Potential Flow|SESA2022]], so here are the key derivations only.

### Rankine half-body: uniform flow plus a source

$$
\Phi=Uz+\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln z,\qquad\psi=Ur\sin\theta+\frac{m}{2\pi}\theta.
$$

**Stagnation point.** $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=U+\dfrac{m}{2\pi z}=0\ \Rightarrow\ z_s=-\dfrac{m}{2\pi U}$, on the negative $x$-axis, upstream of the source.

**Body streamline.** At $z_s$, $r\sin\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$, so $\psi_s=m/2$. The body is $\psi=m/2$.

**Width.** Far downstream $\theta\to0$ on the body, so $Uy\to m/2$: half-width $m/(2U)$, full width $m/U$. *Check:* all the source's output $m$ (per unit span) flows inside the body, and far downstream it moves at $U$ across width $m/U$ ✓.

### Rankine oval: uniform flow plus source plus sink

Equal and opposite strengths a finite distance apart close the body. Let the separation shrink to zero with $ml$ fixed and the oval becomes the cylinder of §1. See [[Rankine Oval]].

### Lifting cylinder: uniform flow plus doublet plus point vortex (revision 1.3 Q5)

Add a point vortex at the centre. It is circular, so $\psi$ from a vortex is constant on $r=R$ and the circle stays a streamline. Using the **counter-clockwise-positive** convention of the slides' velocity potential ($\phi=\Gamma\theta/2\pi$):

$$
\Phi=U\left(z+\frac{R^2}{z}\right)-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z,
\qquad
q_\theta\big|_{r=R}=-2U\sin\theta+\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R}.
$$

**Stagnation points.** $q_\theta=0$ gives

$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_s=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi UR}}.
$$

- $|\Gamma|<4\pi UR$: two stagnation points on the surface, symmetric about the $y$-axis.
- $|\Gamma|=4\pi UR$: they merge at the top or bottom.
- $|\Gamma|>4\pi UR$: a single stagnation point lifts off the surface into the flow.

**Lift (derivation).** $q_\theta^2=4U^2\sin^2\theta-\dfrac{2U\Gamma}{\pi R}\sin\theta+\dfrac{\Gamma^2}{4\pi^2R^2}$. In $F_y=-\oint p\sin\theta\,R\,\mathrm d\theta$ with $p=\text{const}-\tfrac12\rho q_\theta^2$, only the middle term survives, because $\oint\sin^3\theta=\oint\sin\theta=0$:

$$
F_y=\frac{\rho R}{2}\oint q_\theta^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\frac{\rho R}{2}\left(-\frac{2U\Gamma}{\pi R}\right)\oint\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\frac{\rho R}{2}\left(-\frac{2U\Gamma}{\pi R}\right)\pi=-\rho U\Gamma.
$$

A **clockwise** circulation (negative $\Gamma$ here) gives upward lift $L'=\rho U|\Gamma|$: the [[Kutta-Joukowski Theorem]], as in panel 6 of the figure. Drag is still zero.

> [!tip] Which way is the circulation?
> Faster flow over the top means lower pressure over the top, so lift up. Faster on top means the vortex adds speed in the $-\theta$ direction on top, i.e. **clockwise**. SESA2022 and thin-aerofoil theory therefore count clockwise as positive. The slides count counter-clockwise as positive. Both give $|L'|=\rho U|\Gamma|$; only the sign label differs.

## 3. Complex numbers and the complex velocity potential

### The complex plane (slide 33; L4, ll. 164–178)

$$
z=x+iy=r\cos\theta+ir\sin\theta=re^{i\theta},
$$

using Euler's identity $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$. Powers become easy: $z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}$. Logarithms too: $\ln z=\ln r+i\theta$. See [[Complex Numbers - Cartesian, Polar and Exponential Forms]].

### The complex potential (slides 34–36; L4, ll. 178–199)

$$
\boxed{\Phi=\phi+i\psi},\qquad \boxed{\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=u-iv}.
$$

> [!note] Transcript notation
> The auto-transcript renders $\phi$ as "V" throughout (e.g. L4, ll. 25, 181: *"capital v here as complex velocity potential where v lowercase v the… velocity potential"*). Read every lowercase "v" that is a potential as $\phi$, and capital "V" as $\Phi$. The lecturer also flags the clash: *"this is a bit of a confusing sign"* (l. 180).

### Why the derivative is u − iv (derivation)

The slide states it "ensures that $\phi$ and $\psi$ satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann condition". Here is what that means.

A complex function has a derivative only if the limit $\Delta\Phi/\Delta z$ is the **same in every direction** of approach.

**Approach along $x$** ($\Delta z=\Delta x$):

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}.
$$

**Approach along $y$** ($\Delta z=i\Delta y$):

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y}=-i\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}+i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right)=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}-i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}.
$$

**Equate real and imaginary parts:**

$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y},\qquad \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\qquad(\text{Cauchy–Riemann}).
$$

These are exactly $u=\phi_x=\psi_y$ and $v=\phi_y=-\psi_x$, the definitions from [[SESA3043 1.3 - Potential-Flow Review#6. Cartesian and polar formulae|the review]]. Substituting them in either expression:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\phi_x+i\psi_x=u+i(-v)=u-iv.\ \checkmark
$$

Three consequences come free:

1. **Both parts are harmonic.** Differentiate CR: $\phi_{xx}=\psi_{yx}$ and $\phi_{yy}=-\psi_{xy}$, so $\nabla^2\phi=0$. Similarly $\nabla^2\psi=0$. *Every analytic function is a potential flow.*
2. **Orthogonality.** $\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi=\phi_x\psi_x+\phi_y\psi_y=\phi_x(-\phi_y)+\phi_y\phi_x=0$: equipotentials cross streamlines at right angles.
3. **Speed.** $q^2=u^2+v^2=\left|\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz\right|^2$, so Bernoulli needs only the modulus.

> [!tip] What the lecturer wants you to take away (L4, ll. 190–198)
> *"Instead of working with two components… you just define one complex potential."* And *"instead of working with partial derivatives… we are working with ordinary differential"*: one derivative $\mathrm d/\mathrm dz$ gives both $u$ and $v$.

## 4. Complex potentials of the elementary flows (revision 1.3 Q1)

The lecture works only the doublet and leaves the rest: *"this you can do on your own… revision question… chapter 1.3 question one"* (L4, ll. 208–213). Here are all four. In each case: write $\Phi=\phi+i\psi$, recognise a function of $z$, differentiate.

**Uniform flow at angle $\alpha$.** $\phi=U(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha)$, $\psi=U(y\cos\alpha-x\sin\alpha)$:

$$
\Phi=U\left[x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha+i(y\cos\alpha-x\sin\alpha)\right]=U(\cos\alpha-i\sin\alpha)(x+iy)=\boxed{Ue^{-i\alpha}z}.
$$

*Check by expanding:* $(\cos\alpha-i\sin\alpha)(x+iy)=x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha+i(y\cos\alpha-x\sin\alpha)$ ✓. Then $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=Ue^{-i\alpha}=U\cos\alpha-iU\sin\alpha$, so $u=U\cos\alpha$, $v=U\sin\alpha$ ✓.

**Source/sink of strength $m$ at the origin.** $\phi=\dfrac{m}{2\pi}\ln r$, $\psi=\dfrac{m}{2\pi}\theta$:

$$
\Phi=\frac{m}{2\pi}(\ln r+i\theta)=\boxed{\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln z},\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{m}{2\pi z}=\frac{m}{2\pi r}e^{-i\theta}.
$$

So $u=\dfrac{m\cos\theta}{2\pi r}$, $v=\dfrac{m\sin\theta}{2\pi r}$: radial, magnitude $m/2\pi r$ ✓. At a point $z_0$, replace $z$ by $z-z_0$.

**Point vortex (counter-clockwise $\Gamma>0$).** $\phi=\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\theta$, $\psi=-\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$:

$$
\Phi=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\left(\theta-i\ln r\right)=-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}(\ln r+i\theta)=\boxed{-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z},\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi z}.
$$

*Check:* $-\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi r}e^{-i\theta}=-\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}(\sin\theta+i\cos\theta)$, so $u=-\dfrac{\Gamma\sin\theta}{2\pi r}$, $v=\dfrac{\Gamma\cos\theta}{2\pi r}$: speed $\Gamma/2\pi r$, counter-clockwise ✓.

> [!warning] The vortex row of the slide tables is internally inconsistent (slides 21 and 37)
> The slides give $\phi=\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\theta$ (counter-clockwise) but $\psi=+\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$ and $\Phi=+\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z$ (both clockwise). Expanding the slide's $\Phi$: $\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}(\ln r+i\theta)=-\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\theta+i\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$, whose real part is $-\Gamma\theta/2\pi$, not the $\phi$ in the same row. The lecturer said at the start: *"I need to double check the sign and then I'll let you know later"* (L4, l. 80). The slide's velocity column also reads *"$q_r=0$ and $q_r=\Gamma/2\pi r$"*; the second should be $q_\theta$. Use one self-consistent set (above, or the clockwise set with every sign flipped) and state your convention in an exam. See also the [[SESA3043 1.3 - Potential-Flow Review#7. Elementary solutions and superposition|review note's sign table]].

**Doublet (source upstream, $\nu=\pi$).** $\phi=\dfrac{\mu\cos\theta}{2\pi r}$, $\psi=-\dfrac{\mu\sin\theta}{2\pi r}$:

$$
\Phi=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r}(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r}e^{-i\theta}=\boxed{\frac{\mu}{2\pi z}},\qquad\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=-\frac{\mu}{2\pi z^2}.
$$

| Flow | $\Phi(z)$ | $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz$ |
|---|---|---|
| uniform, angle $\alpha$ | $Ue^{-i\alpha}z$ | $Ue^{-i\alpha}$ |
| source ($m>0$) / sink ($m<0$) at $z_0$ | $\dfrac{m}{2\pi}\ln(z-z_0)$ | $\dfrac{m}{2\pi(z-z_0)}$ |
| vortex, counter-clockwise $\Gamma>0$ | $-\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z$ | $-\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi z}$ |
| doublet, source upstream ($\nu=\pi$) | $\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi z}$ | $-\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi z^2}$ |
| general doublet, source at angle $\nu$ | $-\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi}\dfrac{e^{i\nu}}{z}$ | $\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi}\dfrac{e^{i\nu}}{z^2}$ |

## 5. The general doublet (derivation)

This is the one example the lecture works in full (slides 38–48; L4, ll. 214–267).

![[aa_general_doublet_derivation.png|900]]

**Set-up.** A sink $-m$ at the origin and a source $+m$ at $z_0=le^{i\nu}$, a distance $l$ away at angle $\nu$ to the real axis (L4, ll. 220–225). The table's doublet is the special case $\nu=\pi$: the source upstream on the negative $x$-axis.

**(a) Superpose** (L4, ll. 227–234):

$$
\Phi=\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln\left(z-le^{i\nu}\right)-\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln z.
$$

**(b) Combine the logarithms**, $\ln A-\ln B=\ln(A/B)$:

$$
\Phi=\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln\left(\frac{z-le^{i\nu}}{z}\right)=\frac{m}{2\pi}\ln\left(1-\frac{le^{i\nu}}{z}\right).
$$

**(c) Taylor-expand** for small $\varepsilon=le^{i\nu}/z$ (L4, ll. 236–240). From $\ln(1-\varepsilon)=-\varepsilon-\varepsilon^2/2-\cdots$, keep the first term:

$$
\Phi\approx\frac{m}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{le^{i\nu}}{z}\right)=-\frac{ml}{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\nu}}{z}.
$$

**(d) Take the doublet limit.** Let $l\to0$ and $m\to\infty$ together, holding $\mu=ml$ fixed (L4, ll. 240–243). The neglected terms are $O(ml^2)=O(\mu l)\to0$, so the result becomes exact:

$$
\boxed{\Phi=-\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\nu}}{z}}.
$$

**(e) Differentiate** (L4, ll. 244–247). $\mathrm d(z^{-1})/\mathrm dz=-z^{-2}$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=-\frac{\mu e^{i\nu}}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)=\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\nu}}{z^2}.
$$

**(f) Go polar**, because the numerator is already exponential (L4, ll. 248–254). $z^2=r^2e^{i2\theta}$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r^2}\,e^{i\nu}e^{-i2\theta}=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r^2}\,e^{-i(2\theta-\nu)}.
$$

**(g) Split.** $e^{-ia}=\cos a-i\sin a$, and $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=u-iv$ (L4, ll. 255–259):

$$
\boxed{u=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r^2}\cos(2\theta-\nu),\qquad v=\frac{\mu}{2\pi r^2}\sin(2\theta-\nu)}.
$$

**(h) Check with $\nu=\pi$.** $\cos(2\theta-\pi)=-\cos2\theta$ and $\sin(2\theta-\pi)=-\sin2\theta$, giving $u=-\dfrac{\mu\cos2\theta}{2\pi r^2}$, $v=-\dfrac{\mu\sin2\theta}{2\pi r^2}$: the table entries ✓ (L4, ll. 262–267). And $\Phi=-\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi}\dfrac{(-1)}{z}=\dfrac{\mu}{2\pi z}$ ✓.

> [!check] Numerical check of the Taylor step
> At $z=1.3+0.4i$, $\nu=0.7$, with $\mu=1$: the exact source–sink potential with $l=0.1$ is $-0.1108-0.0490i$; with $l=0.01$ it is $-0.1080-0.0460i$; the doublet formula gives $-0.1077-0.0457i$. The error falls roughly in proportion to $l$, as the neglected $O(\mu l)$ term predicts.

> [!tip] Why bother with a general doublet?
> Its axis can point anywhere, so it can model a body at incidence, and its form $-\mu e^{i\nu}/(2\pi z)$ is just the uniform-stream image needed by the circle theorem: a uniform stream at angle $\alpha$ plus a doublet aligned with it is a cylinder in that stream. In Chapter 2 doublets become **doublet panels**, the building block of the most common panel method ([[SESA3043 2.3 - Panel Methods|2.3]]).

## 6. The cylinder again, in one line (revision 1.3 Q2)

With $\Phi_{uniform}=Uz$ and $\Phi_{doublet}=\mu/(2\pi z)$, and $\mu=2\pi UR^2$ from §1:

$$
\Phi=U\left(z+\frac{R^2}{z}\right).
$$

**Velocity:**

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=U\left(1-\frac{R^2}{z^2}\right).
$$

**On the surface** $z=Re^{i\theta}$: $R^2/z^2=e^{-i2\theta}$, so

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=U\left(1-e^{-i2\theta}\right)=Ue^{-i\theta}\left(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)=2iU\sin\theta\,e^{-i\theta}.
$$

**Speed:** $q^2=\left|\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz\right|^2=4U^2\sin^2\theta$, and hence $C_p=1-4\sin^2\theta$ as before ✓. Far away, $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz\to U$ ✓. Stagnation points are where $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=0$: $z^2=R^2$, $z=\pm R$ ✓.

Compare the two routes: by superposition this took four steps of partial derivatives; with $\Phi$ it is one derivative and one modulus. Separating real and imaginary parts:

$$
\phi=U\left(r+\frac{R^2}{r}\right)\cos\theta,\qquad \psi=U\left(r-\frac{R^2}{r}\right)\sin\theta,
$$

because $z+R^2/z=re^{i\theta}+(R^2/r)e^{-i\theta}$. These match §1, including the **plus** sign in $\phi$.

> [!note] Extension: the circle theorem
> Milne-Thomson's circle theorem says: if $f(z)$ is a flow with no singularities inside $|z|<R$, then $f(z)+\overline{f}(R^2/z)$ is the same flow with a cylinder of radius $R$ inserted. For $f=Uz$, $\overline f(R^2/z)=UR^2/z$, which is exactly the doublet. This is the idea that conformal mapping in Chapter 2 starts from ([[SESA3043 2.1 - Complex Functions and Conformal Mapping|2.1]]).

## 7. A mystery flow: wedges and corners

*"Principle of superposition may not be needed if we use the complex potential"* (slide 48; L4, ll. 271–285). Take a single power of $z$, with constants $k>0$ and $m$:

$$
\Phi=-kz^{m+1}.
$$

![[aa_wedge_flow_family.png|900]]

### Step 1: split into φ and ψ

$z^{m+1}=r^{m+1}e^{i(m+1)\theta}=r^{m+1}\left[\cos(m+1)\theta+i\sin(m+1)\theta\right]$, so

$$
\phi=-kr^{m+1}\cos\left[(m+1)\theta\right],\qquad\psi=-kr^{m+1}\sin\left[(m+1)\theta\right].
$$

### Step 2: find the body from ψ = 0

$\psi=0$ (for $r>0$) when $\sin[(m+1)\theta]=0$ (L4, ll. 297–312):

$$
(m+1)\theta=0\quad\text{or}\quad(m+1)\theta=\pm\pi\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\theta=0\quad\text{or}\quad\theta=\pm\frac{\pi}{m+1}.
$$

- $\theta=0$, the positive $x$-axis, is the **dividing streamline** that runs into the apex.
- $\theta=\pm\pi/(m+1)$ are the two **walls**, which meet at the origin.

The body lies outside the walls, around the negative $x$-axis. Each wall makes an angle $\pi-\dfrac{\pi}{m+1}=\dfrac{m\pi}{m+1}$ with the negative $x$-axis; the slides call these $\beta_\pm\pi$. The total wedge angle is

$$
\boxed{\beta\pi=\frac{2m\pi}{m+1},\qquad\beta=\frac{2m}{m+1}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad m=\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}.
$$

> [!note] Slide notation
> Slides 50–53 write $\beta_\pm\pi=\pi-\dfrac{\pi}{m+1}=\pm\dfrac{m\pi}{m+1}$. The "$\pm$" belongs to the two walls, above and below the axis; the first equality holds for the upper wall. Then $\beta\pi=\beta_+\pi-\beta_-\pi$, the angle between the walls measured through the body.

### Step 3: velocity

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=-k(m+1)z^m=-k(m+1)r^me^{im\theta}=u-iv,
$$

$$
\boxed{u=-k(m+1)r^m\cos m\theta,\qquad v=k(m+1)r^m\sin m\theta}
$$

(L4, ll. 321–330; the lecturer did the split in MATLAB and suggests you check it in MATLAB or Python, l. 330.) On the dividing streamline $\theta=0$: $u=-k(m+1)r^m<0$, so the flow comes **from the right** towards the apex, which is a stagnation point for $m>0$.

### Step 4: what m does (slide 58; L4, ll. 333–342)

| $m$ | $\beta$ | Body | Flow region angle $(2-\beta)\pi$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ | semi-infinite flat plate along $x<0$ (uniform flow $\Phi=-kz$) | $2\pi$ |
| $0<m<1$ | $0<\beta<1$ | wedge of angle $\beta\pi$ | between $\pi$ and $2\pi$ |
| $1$ | $1$ | infinite wall: stagnation-point flow, $\Phi=-kz^2$ | $\pi$ |
| $m>1$ | $1<\beta<2$ | flow *inside* a concave corner | $2\pi/(m+1)<\pi$ |
| $-\tfrac12<m<0$ | $\beta<0$ | use **one half** only: wall along $\theta=0$, flow turning round a **convex** corner | $\pi/(m+1)$, between $\pi$ and $2\pi$ |

For $m<0$ the symmetric picture breaks down (the flow region $2\pi/(m+1)$ would exceed $2\pi$), so the dividing streamline $\theta=0$ is taken as a wall and only one side is used. This is how negative-$\beta$ Falkner–Skan flows are interpreted.

### Velocity and its gradient along the wall (revision 1.3 Q3)

On the upper wall $\theta_w=\pi/(m+1)$, with $s=r$ the distance from the apex:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=-k(m+1)s^me^{im\pi/(m+1)}.
$$

**Speed:** the modulus,

$$
\boxed{U_e(s)=k(m+1)\,s^m}.
$$

**Direction:** $u+iv=\overline{u-iv}=-k(m+1)s^me^{-im\pi/(m+1)}=k(m+1)s^me^{i[\pi-m\pi/(m+1)]}=k(m+1)s^me^{i\pi/(m+1)}$. The angle $\pi/(m+1)$ is the wall direction itself: the flow runs **along** the wall, away from the apex ✓ (no flow through it).

**Gradient:**

$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm ds}=k\,m(m+1)\,s^{m-1}=\frac{m\,U_e}{s}}.
$$

**Pressure gradient** (Bernoulli along the wall): $\dfrac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm ds}=-\rho U_e\dfrac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm ds}=-\rho\,m\,\dfrac{U_e^2}{s}$.

- $m>0$ (wedges): the flow accelerates and pressure falls: a **favourable** gradient.
- $m<0$ (convex corners): it decelerates: an **adverse** gradient.

> [!tip] Why this matters later
> $U_e=Cx^m$ is precisely the edge velocity of the **Falkner–Skan** boundary layers, and their pressure-gradient parameter is the same $\beta=2m/(m+1)$ (the Hartree parameter). $m=0$ is the Blasius flat plate ([[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]]), $m=1$ the stagnation-point (Hiemenz) layer. Potential flow supplies $U_e(x)$; the boundary-layer equations then take it as given.

## 8. The unsteady Bernoulli equation

Source: `Ch1_Notes_Unsteady Bernoulli Eqn.pdf`. Week 1 stated the result and left the derivation to these notes (L3, ll. 214–218); [[SESA3043 1.3 - Potential-Flow Review#Momentum becomes Bernoulli|the review note]] has a compact version.

![[aa_unsteady_bernoulli.png|900]]

### Vector-form derivation, one step per line

Start from incompressible Navier–Stokes without body force:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u.
$$

**(i) Convective term.** The vector identity is

$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla\left(\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}{2}\right)+(\nabla\times\mathbf u)\times\mathbf u .
$$

For irrotational flow the second term vanishes, leaving $\nabla(q^2/2)$, where $q^2/2=u_iu_i/2$ is the kinetic energy per unit mass.

> [!warning] Typo in the notes' Eq. (1)
> The PDF writes $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=(\nabla\times\mathbf u)+\nabla\left(\tfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}{2}\right)$. The first term must be $(\nabla\times\mathbf u)\times\mathbf u$: as printed, it adds the vorticity (a vector with different units) to an acceleration. The conclusion is unaffected, because the term is zero in potential flow anyway. *Check of the identity in 2-D:* the $x$-component of $(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf u)$ with $\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\mathbf k$ is $-\omega v$. Then $\partial_x(\tfrac12(u^2+v^2))-\omega v=uu_x+vv_x-(v_x-u_y)v=uu_x+vu_y$ ✓.

**(ii) Viscous term.** The identity $\nabla^2\mathbf u=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)-\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$ gives zero, because both the divergence (incompressible) and the curl (irrotational) vanish.

**(iii) Unsteady term.** With $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$ and smooth $\phi$, time and space derivatives commute:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\phi)=\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right).
$$

**(iv) Collect.** Every term is now a gradient:

$$
\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)+\nabla\left(\frac{q^2}{2}\right)=-\nabla\left(\frac p\rho\right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{q^2}{2}+\frac p\rho\right)=\mathbf 0.
$$

**(v) Integrate.** A function whose gradient vanishes everywhere cannot vary in space. It can still vary in time:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{q^2}{2}+\frac p\rho=C(t)}.
$$

With gravity, $\mathbf g=-\nabla(gz)$ is also a gradient, so add $gz$ to the bracket.

> [!tip] What is special about potential flow here
> - Steady Bernoulli for a *rotational* inviscid flow holds only **along a streamline**. In potential flow the constant is the same **everywhere**, because $(\nabla\times\mathbf u)\times\mathbf u=0$ in every direction.
> - The viscous term is **zero**, not merely small. The viscous *stress* is not zero, but its net force on each fluid element is. The catch: a potential flow cannot satisfy no-slip, which is exactly why boundary layers exist ([[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]]).
> - $C(t)$ can usually be fixed by a condition far away or at a free surface.

### Tensor-form derivation (the notes' Section 2)

Write the convective term with the vorticity tensor $\omega_{ij}=\partial u_j/\partial x_i-\partial u_i/\partial x_j$ (the notes' sign):

$$
u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}=u_j\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)+u_j\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}=-u_j\omega_{ij}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(\frac{u_ju_j}{2}\right).
$$

The first bracket is zero for potential flow. The viscous term splits the same way:

$$
\nu\frac{\partial^2u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}=\nu\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)+\nu\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_j}\right),
$$

where the first bracket is zero (irrotational) and the second is zero (incompressible). What is left is the notes' Eq. (5):

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(\frac{u_ju_j}{2}\right)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(\frac p\rho\right),
$$

and with $u_i=\partial\phi/\partial x_i$ the same boxed result follows. (The notes' third line has a typo, $\partial^2u_j/\partial x_jx_i$, missing a $\partial$ in the denominator.)

### Example 1: start-up flow from a tank (suggested)

A large tank of depth $h$ drains through a horizontal pipe of length $L$. The valve opens at $t=0$. How fast does the flow establish?

**Model.** Plug flow in the pipe, $\phi=U(t)\,s$ with $s$ measured from the pipe entrance, so $\partial\phi/\partial t=s\,\dot U$. The tank is large, so the free-surface speed and its $\partial\phi/\partial t$ are negligible.

**Apply unsteady Bernoulli** (with $gz$) between the free surface (atmospheric, $z=h$, $q\approx0$, $\phi\approx0$) and the pipe exit (atmospheric, $z=0$, $q=U$, $s=L$):

$$
0+0+\frac{p_a}{\rho}+gh=L\dot U+\frac{U^2}{2}+\frac{p_a}{\rho}+0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{L\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dt}=gh-\frac{U^2}{2}}.
$$

**Solve.** Separate the variables, with $a=\sqrt{2gh}$: $\dfrac{\mathrm dU}{a^2-U^2}=\dfrac{\mathrm dt}{2L}$. Integrating, $\dfrac1a\tanh^{-1}\dfrac Ua=\dfrac{t}{2L}$, so

$$
U(t)=\sqrt{2gh}\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2gh}\,t}{2L}\right).
$$

*Check by substitution:* $\dot U=\dfrac{a^2}{2L}\operatorname{sech}^2=\dfrac{1}{L}\cdot\dfrac{a^2}{2}(1-\tanh^2)=\dfrac1L\left(gh-\dfrac{U^2}{2}\right)$ ✓.

**Numbers** ($h=2$ m, $L=10$ m): $\sqrt{2gh}=6.26$ m/s (steady Torricelli speed). The initial acceleration is $gh/L=1.96$ m/s². The flow reaches 99% of its final speed at $t=\dfrac{2L}{a}\tanh^{-1}0.99=8.45$ s.

> [!check] Verified numerically
> Integrating $L\dot U=gh-U^2/2$ with a Runge–Kutta solver gives $U(1\ \mathrm s)=1.900$, $U(2\ \mathrm s)=3.480$, $U(5\ \mathrm s)=5.740$ m/s, matching the tanh solution to $10^{-9}$.

**Lesson.** Steady Bernoulli would predict 6.26 m/s instantly. The $\partial\phi/\partial t$ term is the inertia of the fluid column, which must be accelerated by the head $gh$. A longer pipe means a slower start; this is the physics behind water hammer and intake surge transients.

### Example 2: force on a cylinder in an accelerating stream (suggested)

A cylinder of radius $R$ sits in a stream whose speed $U(t)$ changes. Steady potential flow gives zero force; what does the unsteady term add?

**Potential:** at each instant $\phi=U(t)\left(r+\dfrac{R^2}{r}\right)\cos\theta$. On the surface,

$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\Big|_{r=R}=2R\,\dot U\cos\theta .
$$

**Surface pressure:** $p=\rho\left[C(t)-2R\dot U\cos\theta-\tfrac12\cdot4U^2\sin^2\theta\right]$.

**Drag:** $F_x=-\oint p\cos\theta\,R\,\mathrm d\theta$. The $C(t)$ term integrates to zero, and the $\sin^2\theta\cos\theta$ term integrates to zero (d'Alembert again). Only the unsteady term survives:

$$
F_x=\rho R\oint2R\dot U\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=2\rho R^2\dot U\,\pi=\boxed{2\pi\rho R^2\,\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dt}}.
$$

This is **twice the displaced mass times the acceleration**. Half is the force needed to accelerate the displaced fluid (the far-field pressure gradient, $-\rho\dot U$ per unit volume, acting on volume $\pi R^2$). The other half is the **added mass** $\rho\pi R^2$: fluid that must be accelerated around the body.

*Numbers:* water ($\rho=1000$ kg/m³), $R=0.1$ m, $\dot U=2$ m/s²: $F_x=2\pi(1000)(0.01)(2)=125.7$ N per metre span. *Verified by numerical integration of the surface pressure.*

## 9. Worked examples

### 9.1 A cylinder in a 10 m/s stream (suggested)

$U=10$ m/s, $R=0.5$ m, air $\rho=1.225$ kg/m³.

- Doublet strength: $\mu=2\pi UR^2=2\pi(10)(0.25)=15.71$ m³/s.
- Top of the cylinder: $q=2U=20$ m/s, $C_p=-3$, so $p-p_\infty=-3\times\tfrac12(1.225)(10^2)=-183.8$ Pa.
- $C_p=0$ (static pressure equals $p_\infty$) at $30^\circ$ from each stagnation point.

### 9.2 Lifting cylinder (suggested)

Same cylinder with clockwise circulation chosen so that $|\sin\theta_s|=\tfrac12$: $|\Gamma|=4\pi UR\cdot\tfrac12=2\pi UR=31.42$ m²/s. Stagnation points at $\theta_s=-30^\circ$ and $-150^\circ$, on the underside. Lift per unit span $L'=\rho U|\Gamma|=1.225(10)(31.42)=384.8$ N/m. *Numerical integration of $-\oint p\sin\theta\,R\,\mathrm d\theta$ gives 384.85 N/m* ✓, and $C_l=L'/(\tfrac12\rho U^2\cdot2R)=384.8/61.25=6.28=2\pi$ for this circulation.

### 9.3 A 90° wedge (suggested)

A $90^\circ$ wedge has $\beta=\tfrac12$, so $m=\beta/(2-\beta)=\tfrac13$, and the walls are at $\theta=\pm\pi/(4/3)=\pm135^\circ$ ✓ (panel b of the wedge figure). Choose $k$ so that $U_e=10$ m/s at $s=1$ m: $k(m+1)=10$, $k=7.5$ m$^{2/3}$/s. Then

$$
U_e=10\,s^{1/3},\qquad\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm ds}=\frac{10}{3}s^{-2/3},\qquad\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm ds}\Big|_{s=1}=-\rho U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm ds}=-1.225(10)(3.33)=-40.8\ \mathrm{Pa/m}.
$$

Favourable, as for every wedge with $m>0$. Near the apex $U_e\to0$ (a stagnation point), but $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm ds\to\infty$: the acceleration is fastest right at the corner.

## 10. Problem-solving workflow

1. **Build $\Phi$:** superpose from the table, or start from a given function of $z$.
2. **Split** with $z=re^{i\theta}$: $\phi=\mathrm{Re}\,\Phi$, $\psi=\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi$.
3. **Body:** set $\psi=$ const (usually 0) and solve for the curve. Use stagnation points ($\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=0$) to choose which constant.
4. **Velocity:** $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=u-iv$. The minus sign on $v$ is the commonest slip.
5. **Pressure:** $C_p=1-q^2/U_\infty^2$, with $q=|\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz|$; add $-\dfrac{2}{U_\infty^2}\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}$ if the flow is unsteady.
6. **Forces:** integrate $-p\,\mathbf n$ round the body; expect zero drag for steady flow, $\rho U|\Gamma|$ lift with circulation.
7. **State your vortex sign convention.**
