# SESA3043 2.2 - Lumped Vortex Method

> [!abstract] Summary
> Thin-aerofoil theory says a thin aerofoil at small incidence has its aerodynamic centre at $c/4$ and a lift slope of $2\pi$. The **lumped-vortex (LV) method** reproduces both with almost no work: replace the aerofoil by **one point vortex at $c/4$** and impose flow tangency at **one control point at $3c/4$**. Balancing the free-stream upwash $U_\infty\alpha$ against the vortex's downwash $\Gamma/[2\pi(c/2)]$ gives $\Gamma=\pi U_\infty\alpha c$ and $C_L=2\pi\alpha$. The power of the method is that several lifting elements can be combined: each control point feels the downwash of **every** vortex, giving a small linear system. Two plates in **tandem** (gap $c/2$) share the lift $2:1$ (front:rear) with no change in the total. An aerofoil in **ground effect** is modelled with a mirror-image vortex and gains lift as $\Gamma/\Gamma_\infty=1+1/(4h/c)^2$. Splitting a **cambered** camber line into $N$ segments gives $C_L=2\pi(\alpha+0.12)$ for the NACA camber $\bar z_c=0.24\bar x(1-\bar x)$, exactly the thin-aerofoil answer. The method is 2-D, cannot represent thickness, and uses singular point vortices, which motivates panel methods (2.3).

## The whole topic in one physical picture

Look at a thin aerofoil from far away. What you see is a bound vortex: circulation $\Gamma$ that produces lift $\rho U_\infty\Gamma$ and turns the flow downwards behind it. Thin-aerofoil theory spreads that circulation over the chord as a vortex sheet $\gamma(x)$. The lumped-vortex method squeezes it back into one point.

Put the point vortex where the lift acts (the aerodynamic centre, $c/4$). Then ask what the plate must do: the flow cannot pass *through* it. The free stream at incidence $\alpha$ pushes flow up through the plate at $U_\infty\alpha$. The vortex must push it back down by the same amount. A single point vortex cannot cancel the upwash *everywhere* on the plate, but it can cancel it at one **control point**. Choosing that point at $3c/4$ makes the answer exactly match thin-aerofoil theory.

Once a "wing" is just a vortex and a control point, any arrangement of wings is a small set of linear equations. Each wing feels the downwash or upwash of all the others, and of their mirror images if there is a ground plane.

## 1. Thin-aerofoil review (slides 25–27; revision 2.2 Q1)

### Centre of pressure

The point where the resultant aerodynamic force acts, i.e. where the moment of the pressure distribution vanishes:

$$
x_{cp}=\frac{\int x\,p(x)\,\mathrm dx}{\int p(x)\,\mathrm dx},\qquad\bar x_{cp}=\frac{x_{cp}}{c}=-\frac{C_{M,LE}}{C_L},
$$

with $C_{M,LE}=M_{LE}/(\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2c^2)$, **nose-up positive**.

*Why the minus sign:* lift $L$ acting at $x_{cp}$ behind the LE produces a nose-**down** moment about the LE, $M_{LE}=-Lx_{cp}$. Divide by $\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2c^2$: $C_{M,LE}=-C_L\bar x_{cp}$.

The centre of pressure **moves** with $\alpha$ for a cambered aerofoil (slide 25's sketch), which makes it awkward for stability work.

### Aerodynamic centre

The point about which the pitching moment **does not change** with $\alpha$. For a thin aerofoil at low speed, $\bar x_{ac}=1/4$ (for supersonic aerofoils, $\bar x_{ac}=1/2$; see [[SESA3029 Aerothermodynamics Hub|SESA3029]], Ackeret theory).

### Key conclusions of thin-aerofoil theory

1. Lift slope $\mathrm dC_L/\mathrm d\alpha=2\pi$ per radian.
2. Aerodynamic centre at $c/4$.
3. Camber shifts the lift curve: $C_L=2\pi(\alpha-\alpha_{L=0})$, and gives a constant $C_{M,c/4}$.
4. A symmetric aerofoil has $\alpha_{L=0}=0$ and $C_{M,c/4}=0$, so its centre of pressure is at $c/4$.

The slide's experimental plot (Jacobs and Sherman, $Re\approx6.6\times10^5$) shows the measured slope a little below $2\pi$ (boundary-layer effects) and stall near $13^\circ$. Full derivations: [[SESA2022 T4 - Thin Aerofoil Theory]] and [[Aerodynamic Centre and Centre of Pressure]].

## 2. The flat plate with one lumped vortex (slides 28–30)

![[aa_lumped_vortex_derivation.png|900]]

**Model.** A flat plate of chord $c$ at small incidence $\alpha$. A vortex $\Gamma$ (clockwise positive, giving upward lift) at $x_L$ from the LE; a control point at $x_T$.

**Step 1: what the vortex induces at the control point.** A point vortex induces a speed $\Gamma/(2\pi r)$ perpendicular to the line joining them. The control point is a distance $x_T-x_L$ **downstream** on the same line, so the velocity there is vertical. For a clockwise vortex it points **down** (a downwash):

$$
w_\Gamma=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi(x_T-x_L)}\quad\text{(downwards)}.
$$

**Step 2: what the free stream induces.** The component of $U_\infty$ normal to the plate is $U_\infty\sin\alpha\approx U_\infty\alpha$, pointing **up** through the plate.

**Step 3: flow tangency at $T$.** The net normal velocity must be zero:

$$
U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi(x_T-x_L)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma=2\pi U_\infty\alpha\,(x_T-x_L).
$$

**Step 4: lift.** Kutta–Joukowski, $L'=\rho U_\infty\Gamma$:

$$
C_L=\frac{\rho U_\infty\Gamma}{\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2c}=\frac{2\Gamma}{U_\infty c}=4\pi\alpha\,\frac{x_T-x_L}{c},\qquad\frac{\mathrm dC_L}{\mathrm d\alpha}=4\pi\,\frac{x_T-x_L}{c}.
$$

**Step 5: choose the points to match thin-aerofoil theory.**

- The lift should act at the aerodynamic centre: $x_L=c/4$.
- The slope should be $2\pi$: $4\pi(x_T-x_L)/c=2\pi$, so $x_T-x_L=c/2$ and $x_T=3c/4$.

$$
\boxed{x_L=\frac c4,\quad x_T=\frac{3c}{4},\quad\Gamma_\infty=\pi U_\infty\alpha c,\quad C_L=2\pi\alpha}
$$

The subscript $\infty$ marks the single aerofoil in free flight; it is the reference for every interference case below.

> [!warning] Slide slip (slide 28)
> *"Apply flow tangency condition at a control point $x_L$ from the leading edge"*: the control point is $x_T$. $x_L$ is the vortex (loading) point. Using the same point for both would put the control point on the singularity.

> [!note] How the lecture argued it (LV, ll. 97–207)
> A single point vortex cannot cancel the upwash everywhere on the plate, so tangency holds at **one** control point and not elsewhere (ll. 109–129). The two thin-aerofoil facts fix the two unknown positions. *"The loading point should be at the aerodynamic centre"* gives $x_L=c/4$ (ll. 146–153). The lift slope $2\pi$ gives $x_T-x_L=c/2$, so $x_T=3c/4$ (ll. 195–201). $\alpha$ must be in **radians** for $\sin\alpha\approx\alpha$ (l. 181).

> [!question] From the class (LV, ll. 219–260)
> **Can the LV method move the position of maximum camber?** Not with one flat plate. The extended method (§5) handles camber but never thickness, because it is built on thin-aerofoil theory; the panel method (next week) handles both. In conformal mapping, by contrast, moving the circle changes thickness ($\varepsilon$) and camber ($\beta$) directly. *"This is what [the coursework] asks you to check"*: how $\beta$, mainly camber, changes the pressure and the lift.

> [!tip] Why $3c/4$ is not arbitrary
> The thin-aerofoil vortex sheet for a flat plate is $\gamma(x)=2U_\infty\alpha\sqrt{(c-x)/x}$. The single point vortex at $c/4$ induces at $3c/4$ exactly the same downwash as the whole sheet induces there. (Pistolesi's theorem; the "3/4-chord point" reappears in lifting-line and vortex-lattice methods.)

### The general LV procedure (slide 31)

1. **Set up:** one vortex at $\bar x_L=1/4$ and one control point at $\bar x_T=3/4$ of each lifting element.
2. **Induced downwash** at every control point from every vortex (including images).
3. **Flow tangency** at every control point: free-stream upwash = total induced downwash.
4. **Solve** the linear system for the $\Gamma_i$.
5. **Aerodynamic quantities:** $L_i=\rho U_\infty\Gamma_i$, moments, centres of pressure.

In matrix form, with $a_{ij}$ the downwash at $T_i$ per unit $\Gamma_j$:

$$
\sum_ja_{ij}\Gamma_j=U_\infty\alpha_i,\qquad a_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\pi d_{ij}}\ \text{(for elements on one line, with signed distance } d_{ij}=x_{T_i}-x_{L_j}).
$$

A vortex **downstream** of a control point ($d_{ij}<0$) produces an **upwash** there, hence a negative coefficient.

> [!tip] The lecturer's bookkeeping rule (LV, ll. 358–369)
> Write **every** induced velocity as a downwash, and enter an upward one as a negative downwash. Then each tangency equation reads "free-stream upwash = sum of downwashes", with no sign decisions left to make mid-equation.

## 3. Tandem aerofoils (slide 32 and LVM notes; revision 2.2 Q3)

Two plates of chord $c$, the second starting $c/2$ behind the first's TE, both at $\alpha$. In the lecture these were two Red Arrows in close formation (LV, ll. 303–327); the gap of $c/2$ is *"quite an extreme example"*, but the conclusion carries over (ll. 417–422). The tangency equations below were derived on the board (ll. 339–412), and the worked solution is on Blackboard (l. 413).

**Positions** (from the LE of plate 1): $\Gamma_1$ at $c/4$, $T_1$ at $3c/4$, $\Gamma_2$ at $c+\tfrac c2+\tfrac c4=\tfrac{7c}{4}$, $T_2$ at $\tfrac{9c}{4}$.

**Distances and signs:**

| From \ at | $T_1$ ($3c/4$) | $T_2$ ($9c/4$) |
|---|---|---|
| $\Gamma_1$ ($c/4$) | $c/2$ upstream: downwash $\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi(c/2)}$ | $2c$ upstream: downwash $\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi(2c)}$ |
| $\Gamma_2$ ($7c/4$) | $c$ **downstream**: **upwash** $-\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi c}$ | $c/2$ upstream: downwash $\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi(c/2)}$ |

**Tangency:**

$$
T_1:\ U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma_1}{\pi c}-\frac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi c},\qquad
T_2:\ U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma_1}{4\pi c}+\frac{\Gamma_2}{\pi c}.
$$

**Multiply both by $2\pi c$** (the notes' "good practice"):

$$
2\pi U_\infty\alpha c=2\Gamma_1-\Gamma_2\quad(3),\qquad2\pi U_\infty\alpha c=\tfrac12\Gamma_1+2\Gamma_2\quad(4).
$$

**Eliminate $\Gamma_2$:** $2\times(3)+(4)$ gives $6\pi U_\infty\alpha c=\tfrac92\Gamma_1$, so

$$
\Gamma_1=\tfrac43\pi U_\infty\alpha c.
$$

**Back-substitute in (3):** $2\pi U_\infty\alpha c=\tfrac83\pi U_\infty\alpha c-\Gamma_2$, so $\Gamma_2=\tfrac23\pi U_\infty\alpha c$.

$$
\boxed{\frac{L_1}{L_2}=\frac{\Gamma_1}{\Gamma_2}=2},\qquad\Gamma_1=\tfrac43\Gamma_\infty,\quad\Gamma_2=\tfrac23\Gamma_\infty.
$$

*"The front plane has twice the lift compared to the one behind it"* (LV, l. 416). To carry equal lift, the rear aircraft must fly at a **higher** angle of attack, which is revision Q5 ([[#Q5. Rear aerofoil at extra incidence β = εα (s = c/2)|§7]]; LV, ll. 425–432).

> [!tip] Physical reading
> The rear wing sits in the front wing's **downwash**, which lowers its effective incidence. The front wing sits in the rear wing's **upwash**, which raises its effective incidence. This is why a canard is so effective and a conventional tailplane works at a reduced angle. The total, $\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2=2\Gamma_\infty$, is unchanged: the interference only **redistributes** lift.

### General gap s = εc (revision 2.2 Q3)

With gap $s=\varepsilon c$ between TE$_1$ and LE$_2$: $\Gamma_2$ is at $\tfrac54c+s$ and $T_2$ at $\tfrac74c+s$. The distances become $T_1\leftarrow\Gamma_2$: $\tfrac c2+s$ (upwash), and $T_2\leftarrow\Gamma_1$: $\tfrac32c+s$. Solving (verified symbolically):

$$
\frac{\Gamma_1}{\Gamma_\infty}=\frac{2\varepsilon+3}{2(\varepsilon+1)},\qquad\frac{\Gamma_2}{\Gamma_\infty}=\frac{2\varepsilon+1}{2(\varepsilon+1)},\qquad\boxed{\frac{L_1}{L_2}=\frac{2\varepsilon+3}{2\varepsilon+1}}.
$$

*Checks:* $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$ gives $4/2=2$ ✓. $\varepsilon\to\infty$ gives $1$ (no interference) ✓. $\varepsilon=0$ (touching plates) gives $3$. And $\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2=2\Gamma_\infty$ for **every** gap.

![[aa_lvm_results.png|900]]

> [!note] Why the total is conserved (a neat check)
> Add the two tangency equations after multiplying by $2\pi$: the cross-coefficients are $-1/(c/2+s)$ and $+1/(3c/2+s)$, and the solution happens to satisfy $\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2=2\Gamma_\infty$ identically. It is a 2-D instance of **Munk's stagger theorem**: the total induced effect of two lifting lines is unchanged by moving one of them along the stream.

## 4. Ground effect by the method of images (slide 33 and notes)

> [!note] Lectured Thu 15 Oct (GE, ll. 26–188)
> The lecturer chose ground effect because *"from this simple setup, you can get results which is practically quite useful"* (ll. 27–28).
>
> He derived the free-flight result $C_L=2\pi\alpha$ first (ll. 49–63), then the derivation below on the board (ll. 64–124), with full details on Blackboard (ll. 125–129).
>
> **A deliberate trap** (ll. 97–116): he first wrote the image's whole induced speed $\Gamma/2\pi r$ into the tangency condition, then asked *"Did I make any mistake?"*. A point vortex induces velocity in the **circumferential** direction, perpendicular to $r$. Only its component **normal to the plate** enters the tangency condition, hence the $\sin\beta$ below.

An aerofoil at height $h$ above the ground. The ground is a streamline: no flow through it. Place a **mirror-image** vortex $\Gamma_I=-\Gamma$ (opposite sense) at depth $h$ below the ground. By symmetry, the vertical velocities of the pair cancel on the ground plane, so the boundary condition there is satisfied automatically. See [[Method of Images]].

**Geometry at $T$.** The image vortex is at $(c/4,-h)$ and $T$ at $(3c/4,h)$:

$$
r^2=\left(\frac c2\right)^2+(2h)^2,\qquad\sin\beta=\frac{c/2}{r},
$$

where $\beta$ is the angle at the image between the vertical and the line to $T$.

**Velocity from the image at $T$.** Magnitude $\Gamma/(2\pi r)$, perpendicular to $r$. Its **vertical** component is $\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}\sin\beta$ and, because the image rotates the other way, it is an **upwash**.

**Tangency at $T$** (notes' terms Ⓐ and Ⓑ):

$$
U_\infty\alpha=\underbrace{\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi\frac c2}}_{Ⓐ\ \text{downwash}}-\underbrace{\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}\sin\beta}_{Ⓑ\ \text{upwash}}.
$$

**Simplify.** $\sin\beta/r=(c/2)/r^2$:

$$
U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi c}-\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\frac{c/2}{r^2}=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi c}\left[1-\frac{c^2}{4r^2}\right].
$$

**Multiply by $\pi c$** and use $4r^2=c^2+16h^2$:

$$
\pi U_\infty\alpha c=\Gamma_\infty=\Gamma\left(1-\frac{c^2}{c^2+16h^2}\right)=\Gamma\,\frac{16h^2}{c^2+16h^2}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma_\infty}=\frac{c^2+16h^2}{16h^2}=1+\frac{1}{(4\bar h)^2}},\qquad\bar h=\frac hc.
$$

| $\bar h$ | 2 | 1 | 0.5 | 0.25 | 0.1 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $\Gamma/\Gamma_\infty$ | 1.016 | 1.063 | 1.250 | 2.000 | 7.25 |

> [!warning] Inviscid only
> The notes' figure shows the viscous reality: lift rises as the aerofoil approaches the ground, then **falls** very close to it, because the boundary layers on the aerofoil and the ground interact and the flow under the aerofoil chokes. The LVM curve rises without limit. (The slides' "increase the lift inverse proportional to ride height square" describes the $1/(4\bar h)^2$ term, which is the leading correction for $\bar h\ll1$.) Ground effect also reduces **induced drag** on a finite wing: the image of the trailing vortices cancels part of the downwash. This is why wing-in-ground-effect craft (the Ekranoplan on slide 33) fly close to the water.

**What the lecture added** (GE, ll. 131–183):
- Lift ratio equals circulation ratio, since $L'=\rho U_\infty\Gamma$ in both cases. So the lift rises as the inverse square of ride height as the wing approaches the ground (ll. 139–144).
- **Viscous reality** (ll. 151–162): very close to the ground the gap flow can separate, and lift can level off and then fall. Different flow regimes appear at different ride heights.
- **Racing cars** (ll. 163–183): a Formula One front wing is an **inverted** wing in ground effect, generating downforce for grip in high-speed corners. Different parts of it sit at different ride heights, which makes the design complicated. The Gen4 Formula E front wing is a simpler two-tier design: a structural top tier, and a main element plus flap below it.
- The recording says the inviscid result *"still works up to … non-dimensional ride height about 2.25, okay, it still works, and the lower …"* (ll. 186–188). That number cannot be right as an upper limit: at $\bar h=2.25$ the correction is only 1.2 % (table above). The *"and the lower"* that follows suggests he meant the theory works **down to** a small ride height, probably $\bar h\approx0.25$. Neither the slide nor the notes give a value, so check before relying on it.

## 5. Camber with N lumped vortices (slides 34–35 and notes)

**How the lecture built it** (GE, ll. 189–235). The flat-plate model ignores camber. Start from the tandem revision variants (ll. 195–199): set the gap to zero and give the front plate its own angle. Two plates meeting at an angle *are* a two-segment camber line. Each segment is then an aerofoil with its own vortex at its local quarter-chord and control point at its local three-quarter-chord, and the total circulation is $\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2$ (ll. 204–215).

Segments **need not be equal** (ll. 224–228): use shorter ones where the camber curves more, and work out the distances carefully.

**Idea.** Represent the camber line by $N$ straight segments. Treat each segment as a flat plate with its own vortex at its local $1/4$ point and control point at its local $3/4$ point. The only new ingredient is that each control point sits on a **sloping** segment, so the free-stream upwash normal to it is reduced by the local camber slope.

**Local upwash.** If the camber line slopes at $\beta\approx\mathrm d\bar z_c/\mathrm d\bar x$ (small), the stream meets the segment at incidence $\alpha-\beta$:

$$
U_\infty\sin(\alpha-\beta)\approx U_\infty\left(\alpha-\frac{\mathrm d\bar z_c}{\mathrm d\bar x}\right).
$$

**NACA example.** $\bar z_c=0.24\,\bar x(1-\bar x)$, so $\mathrm d\bar z_c/\mathrm d\bar x=0.24(1-2\bar x)$; maximum camber $0.06c$ at mid-chord.

**Two segments** of length $c/2$: vortices at $c/8$ and $5c/8$, control points at $3c/8$ and $7c/8$.

| At | $\bar x$ | slope | upwash | from $\Gamma_1$ | from $\Gamma_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $T_1$ | $3/8$ | $0.24(1-\tfrac34)=0.06$ | $U_\infty(\alpha-0.06)$ | $+\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi(c/4)}$ | $-\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi(c/4)}$ (upwash) |
| $T_2$ | $7/8$ | $0.24(1-\tfrac74)=-0.18$ | $U_\infty(\alpha+0.18)$ | $+\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi(3c/4)}$ | $+\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi(c/4)}$ |

> [!warning] Typo in the notes' table (`Ch2_Notes_LVM_Cambered Aerofoil.pdf`)
> The $\Gamma_2$ entry at $T_2$ is printed as $\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi\frac{3c}{4}}$. The distance from $\Gamma_2$ ($5c/8$) to $T_2$ ($7c/8$) is $c/4$. With the printed $3c/4$ the equations give $\Gamma_1=\pi U_\infty c(\alpha+0.12)$ and $C_L=2\pi(1.5\alpha+0.27)$, which does not match the notes' own answer. With $c/4$ they give the notes' $\Gamma_1$, $\Gamma_2$ and $C_L$ exactly (verified symbolically). The answer is right; the table entry is the slip.

**Solve.** Multiply both rows by $\pi c/2$:

$$
\tfrac{\pi c}{2}U_\infty(\alpha-0.06)=\Gamma_1-\Gamma_2,\qquad\tfrac{\pi c}{2}U_\infty(\alpha+0.18)=\tfrac13\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2.
$$

Add them: $\tfrac{\pi c}{2}U_\infty(2\alpha+0.12)=\tfrac43\Gamma_1$, so

$$
\Gamma_1=\tfrac38\pi U_\infty c\,(2\alpha+0.12).
$$

Then $\Gamma_2=\Gamma_1-\tfrac{\pi c}{2}U_\infty(\alpha-0.06)=\pi U_\infty c\left[0.75\alpha+0.045-0.5\alpha+0.03\right]=\tfrac18\pi U_\infty c\,(2\alpha+0.6)$.

**Lift:**

$$
C_L=\frac{\rho U_\infty(\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2)}{\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2c}=2\pi\left[\tfrac38(2\alpha+0.12)+\tfrac18(2\alpha+0.6)\right]=2\pi\left[\alpha+0.045+0.075\right]=\boxed{2\pi(\alpha+0.12)}.
$$

**Compare with thin-aerofoil theory.** For a parabolic camber line $\bar z_c=4\bar h\,\bar x(1-\bar x)$ with $\bar h=0.06$, thin-aerofoil theory gives $\alpha_{L=0}=-2\bar h=-0.12$ rad $=-6.9^\circ$. The two-vortex model reproduces it **exactly**.

> [!note] Lectured Thu 15 and Fri 16 Oct (GE, ll. 242–296; PM1, ll. 1–63)
> The board worked exactly this table. Two small-angle simplifications were used, and the lecturer named both:
> 1. **Distances along the chord.** Because the camber slope $\beta$ is small, $\cos\beta\approx1$, so distances along each sloping segment are taken as their chordwise projections (GE, ll. 276–287).
> 2. **The local upwash.** It is normal to the **local segment**, not to the chord line: $U_\infty\sin(\alpha-\beta)\approx U_\infty(\alpha-\mathrm d\bar z_c/\mathrm d\bar x)$, with the slope from the camber line (PM1, ll. 15–34, 53–56).
>
> The results were *"$\Gamma_1$, $\Gamma_2$ …, lift coefficient … two pi"* with $\alpha_{L=0}=-0.12$ (PM1, ll. 42–62). The recording's numbers are garbled; the table above has them right.
>
> The lecturer called the result *"quite close to the actual results"* (PM1, ll. 87–88). For this parabolic camber line the lift is in fact **exact** at any $N$ (next subsection). What two segments miss is the pitching moment, and the shape of real camber lines.

> [!warning] Exam mistakes the lecturer warned about (PM1, ll. 77–84)
> - **Do not carry exact trigonometry.** With $\alpha$ and the segment angles small, set $\sin\approx\tan\approx$ the angle and $\cos\approx1$, and take distances along the segments as chordwise distances. Students who keep the exact sines and cosines end up with equations they cannot solve.
> - **Resolve the free stream normal to each segment**, not normal to the chord.

### More vortices: what converges and what does not

Panel (c) of the results figure and the table below (computed with $N$ equal segments at $\alpha=4^\circ$):

| $N$ | $C_L$ | $C_{M,c/4}$ | $\bar x_{cp}$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1.1926 | 0 | 0.250 |
| 2 | 1.1926 | $-0.1414$ | 0.369 |
| 4 | 1.1926 | $-0.1767$ | 0.398 |
| 16 | 1.1926 | $-0.1878$ | 0.407 |
| 64 | 1.1926 | $-0.1885$ | 0.408 |
| thin-aerofoil theory | 1.1926 | $-\pi\bar h=-0.1885$ | 0.408 |

- **Lift is exact for every $N$** for parabolic camber, even $N=1$ (which uses the slope at $3c/4$, $-0.12$).
- **The pitching moment converges as $1/N^2$** (the error drops by about 4 each time $N$ doubles). $N=1$ puts all the lift at $c/4$ and so cannot capture the camber moment at all.
- With more vortices, $\Gamma_j/\Delta x$ approaches the continuous thin-aerofoil sheet $\gamma(x)$, including its square-root singularity at the LE.

*Moment calculation (two vortices):* $C_{M,LE}=-\dfrac{2}{U_\infty c^2}\left(\Gamma_1\tfrac c8+\Gamma_2\tfrac{5c}{8}\right)=-\tfrac\pi2(\alpha+0.21)$, so $C_{M,c/4}=C_{M,LE}+\tfrac14C_L=-\tfrac\pi2(0.09)=-0.141$, independent of $\alpha$ (as it must be about the a.c.).

## 6. Limitations (slide 40)

- **2-D only.** No tip vortices, no induced drag (use lifting-line or vortex-lattice methods for wings).
- **No thickness.** Only the camber line is modelled, so it is valid for thin aerofoils at small $\alpha$.
- **Point singularities.** Near a lumped vortex the velocity is infinite. With many vortices close together the system becomes stiff and ill-conditioned. The lecture's explanation (PM1, ll. 95–109):
  - more segments mean more equations;
  - each control point gets closer to its **own** vortex, so the self-term ($\propto1/\text{distance}$) grows much larger than the cross terms;
  - one huge number among small ones costs accuracy, *"so instead of further improved accuracy"* the results can get worse.
- **Improvement:** distribute the loading over the actual surface, in 2-D or 3-D, which removes the singularity. That is the panel method ([[SESA3043 2.3 - Panel Methods|2.3]]).

## 7. Revision questions §2.2, fully worked

### Q4. Tandem in ground effect (s = c/2, h = c/2)

Four vortices: $\Gamma_1$, $\Gamma_2$ at height $h$, and images $-\Gamma_1$, $-\Gamma_2$ at $-h$. Downwash at $(x,h)$ from a clockwise vortex $\Gamma$ at $(x_v,y_v)$ is $\dfrac{\Gamma\,(x-x_v)}{2\pi[(x-x_v)^2+(h-y_v)^2]}$. Each image adds an upwash with $(h-y_v)=2h=c$.

| From \ at | $T_1$ | $T_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\Gamma_1$ and image | $\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{\pi c}\left[1-\dfrac{1/4}{1/4+1}\right]=\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{\pi c}\cdot\dfrac45$ | $\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi}\left[\dfrac{1}{2c}-\dfrac{2c}{4c^2+c^2}\right]=\dfrac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi c}\cdot\dfrac{1}{10}$ |
| $\Gamma_2$ and image | $\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi}\left[-\dfrac1c+\dfrac{c}{c^2+c^2}\right]=-\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{4\pi c}$ | $\dfrac{\Gamma_2}{\pi c}\cdot\dfrac45$ |

Solving (verified symbolically):

$$
\Gamma_1=\tfrac{140}{87}\Gamma_\infty=1.609\,\Gamma_\infty,\qquad\Gamma_2=\tfrac{100}{87}\Gamma_\infty=1.149\,\Gamma_\infty,\qquad\boxed{\frac{L_1}{L_2}=\frac75=1.4}.
$$

The ground raises both lifts (total $+38\%$) and **reduces** the front/rear imbalance from 2 to 1.4: the images partly cancel the mutual downwash and upwash.

### Q5. Rear aerofoil at extra incidence β = εα (s = c/2)

Only the right-hand side at $T_2$ changes, to $U_\infty\alpha(1+\varepsilon)$:

$$
\frac{\Gamma_1}{\Gamma_\infty}=\frac{4(\varepsilon+3)}{9},\qquad\frac{\Gamma_2}{\Gamma_\infty}=\frac{2(4\varepsilon+3)}{9},\qquad\boxed{\frac{L_1}{L_2}=\frac{2(\varepsilon+3)}{4\varepsilon+3}}.
$$

$\varepsilon=0$ recovers 2 ✓. The two aerofoils carry **equal lift when $\varepsilon=3/2$**: the rear one must be set $1.5\alpha$ steeper to make up for the front one's downwash.

### Q6. Biplane, vertical gap c/2

Upper vortex at $(c/4,c/2)$, lower at $(c/4,0)$; control points at $(3c/4,\cdot)$. From the upper vortex to the lower control point: $\Delta x=c/2$, $\Delta y=-c/2$, $r^2=c^2/2$. Downwash (vertical component) $=\dfrac{\Gamma\,\Delta x}{2\pi r^2}=\dfrac{\Gamma}{2\pi c}$. By symmetry the same holds the other way round, and $\Gamma_1=\Gamma_2=\Gamma$:

$$
U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi c}+\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi c}=\frac{3\Gamma}{2\pi c}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\Gamma=\tfrac23\Gamma_\infty}.
$$

The biplane's total lift is $\tfrac43$ of a **single** aerofoil (a 33% increase), but each wing produces only $\tfrac23$ of what it would alone: a third of the potential lift is lost to mutual interference. This is the classic biplane penalty, and why the gap was made as large as structurally possible.

### Challenge: tandem with unequal chords (PM1, ll. 67–73)

*"If you want to challenge yourself"*: a front plate of chord $c_1$ and a rear plate of chord $c_2$, both at $\alpha$, with gap $s$. This is a main element and a flap, where *"the main element [is] longer but the flap smaller"*.

**Positions.** Vortices at $c_1/4$ and $c_1+s+c_2/4$; control points at $3c_1/4$ and $c_1+s+3c_2/4$.

**Distances.** From vortex 2 back to $T_1$: $d_{21}=c_1/4+s+c_2/4$. Vortex 2 is downstream of $T_1$, so it induces **upwash** there. From vortex 1 to $T_2$: $d_{12}=3c_1/4+s+3c_2/4$, downwash.

**Tangency** at the two control points:

$$
U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma_1}{\pi c_1}-\frac{\Gamma_2}{2\pi d_{21}},\qquad U_\infty\alpha=\frac{\Gamma_1}{2\pi d_{12}}+\frac{\Gamma_2}{\pi c_2}.
$$

**Solution** (solved symbolically):

$$
\boxed{\Gamma_1=\pi U_\infty\alpha\,c_1\,\frac{3c_1+3c_2+4s}{3c_1+c_2+4s},\qquad\Gamma_2=\pi U_\infty\alpha\,c_2\,\frac{c_1+c_2+4s}{3c_1+c_2+4s}}
$$

**Checks.**
- $c_1=c_2=c$, $s=c/2$: $\Gamma_1=\tfrac86\pi U_\infty\alpha c=\tfrac43\Gamma_\infty$ and $\Gamma_2=\tfrac23\Gamma_\infty$ ✓ (§3).
- **The total is exactly** $\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2=\pi U_\infty\alpha(c_1+c_2)$. The numerator factorises as $(c_1+c_2)(3c_1+c_2+4s)$. So in this model the pair always lifts like **one plate of chord $c_1+c_2$**, whatever the gap or chord split. Interference only redistributes the lift.

| $c_2/c_1$ | $s/c_1$ | $\Gamma_1/(\pi U_\infty\alpha c_1)$ | $\Gamma_2/(\pi U_\infty\alpha c_1)$ | $L_1/L_2$ |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.5 | 1.333 | 0.667 | 2.00 |
| 0.5 | 0.5 | 1.182 | 0.318 | 3.71 |
| 0.5 | 0.1 | 1.256 | 0.244 | 5.16 |

A small flap close behind the main element carries little lift at the same angle. That is why a flap is set at a **higher** angle, as the lecturer said for the rear aerofoil (PM1, ll. 71–73; Q5 above). It is also why the normal at $T_2$ must be taken on the flap's own slope (PM1, ll. 73–76).
