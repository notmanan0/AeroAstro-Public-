# SESA3043 3.3 - Pohlhausen Method and Pressure Gradients

> [!abstract] Summary
> For a general edge velocity $U_e(x)$ there is no similarity solution, so Pohlhausen assumed a quartic profile in $\eta=y/\delta$ and fixed its coefficients from conditions at the wall and edge, including the momentum equation at the wall. That brings in one parameter, $\lambda=(\delta^2/\nu)\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$, comparing pressure and viscous forces: positive fills out the profile, negative empties it, and $\lambda=-12$ is separation. Separation is likelier with a thick layer, slow edge flow and a strong adverse gradient; turbulent layers resist it much better, which is why golf balls have dimples.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a laminar layer sees an arbitrary $U_e(x)$ and you want its profile or how close it is to separation.
> - **Assumptions:** laminar; quartic profile; $-12\le\lambda\le12$; an approximate method that predicts separation late.
> - **Method:** 1. $\lambda=\frac{\delta^2}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}$. 2. Profile $F(\eta)+\lambda G(\eta)$. 3. $\delta^*/\delta$, $\theta/\delta$ and $\tau_w$ from $\lambda$. 4. Separation at $\lambda=-12$. 5. Get $\delta(x)$ from the momentum integral equation (3.5).
> - **Common mistakes:** reading a Reynolds-number trend from $\lambda$ with $\delta$ held fixed (for similar laminar flow $\lambda$ is $Re$-independent); trusting Pohlhausen's separation point.
> - **Practise:** [[#6. Worked example: how close to separation? (suggested)]] · [[SESA3043 Ch3 Revision Questions - Worked Solutions#3.3 Pohlhausen and pressure gradient|Ch3 revision 3.3]]

Blasius and Falkner–Skan are exact, but their derivations are long and they need a special edge velocity, $U_e=cx^m$. A real aerofoil has an arbitrary $U_e(x)$, and Pohlhausen's method handles that case: a third, much simpler viscous solution. The method also gives a criterion for separation of the boundary layer.

The physical setting is a layer growing over a bump (after Leishman). Up the front, the outer flow accelerates ($\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx>0$), so the pressure falls downstream, which is a favourable pressure gradient: the pressure pushes every fluid particle forward, including the slow ones by the wall. Over the top and down the back the outer flow decelerates and the pressure rises, an adverse gradient. The pressure now pushes backwards on everything. Fast fluid near the edge has the momentum to coast through, but slow fluid near the wall does not, and somewhere downstream it is brought to rest with $\partial u/\partial y|_{wall}=0$. Beyond that point it flows backwards, the layer lifts off and a recirculating wake forms. This is separation.

The pressure gradient is imposed by the outer flow and pressed straight through the layer ($\partial p/\partial y=0$, 1.4). So the layer's fate is decided by $U_e(x)$, and by how much momentum the layer has near the wall to resist it.

The figure follows the same bump with the Pohlhausen profile of §2 drawn at five stations, each with a $\lambda$ of the sign the local gradient demands. Panels (b) and (c) are the outer-flow edge velocity and, through Bernoulli, the edge pressure $C_p=1-(U_e/U_\infty)^2$: wherever $U_e$ rises the pressure falls (favourable, $\lambda>0$), and down the back both reverse (adverse, $\lambda<0$). On the front face (station 2) the profile is fuller and the layer thinner than on a flat plate. Down the back (station 4) it empties out near the wall, thickens, and picks up an inflection point. At station 5 the wall slope has fallen to zero, which for the quartic happens at $\lambda=-12$ (§3), and downstream of that point the near-wall flow runs backwards in a recirculating region the quartic cannot describe. The heights and positions are schematic; the profile shapes are the exact quartics.

## 1. Pressure gradient terminology

At high Reynolds number $\partial p/\partial y=0$ across the layer (order-of-magnitude analysis, [[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers|1.4]]), so the static pressure is a function of $x$ only and equals its value at the edge, $p=p_e(x)$. The edge is in the potential flow, so Bernoulli holds there along the wall (steady, incompressible):

$$
p_e+\tfrac12\rho U_e^2=\text{const}.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $x$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}+\rho U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-\frac1\rho\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}.
$$

This is why the BL momentum equation carries $U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ where the pressure gradient used to be, and why that term should be read as the pressure gradient with its sign reversed:

| External flow | $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ | $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$ | Name | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| accelerating | $>0$ | $<0$ | favourable (FPG) | thins the layer, stabilises it |
| uniform | 0 | 0 | zero (ZPG) | flat plate |
| decelerating | $<0$ | $>0$ | adverse (APG) | thickens the layer, can separate it |

Under an adverse gradient the pressure keeps rising downstream, the fluid near the wall keeps slowing, and eventually the layer separates. The velocity profile at the **separation point** is a special one: it leaves the wall vertically, $\partial u/\partial y|_{y=0}=0$. Since $\tau_w=\mu\,\partial u/\partial y|_{y=0}$, the wall shear stress and $C_f=\tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho U_e^2)$ are both zero there. Downstream of it the flow next to the wall reverses and a wake forms, so $\tau_w$ changes sign at separation.

## 2. The Pohlhausen solution: derivation

The starting point is the 2D BL equations with the pressure gradient written through $U_e$:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,\qquad
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
$$

The sign of $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ says whether the gradient is favourable or adverse. Laminar profiles at different stations collapse onto one curve when plotted as $u/U_e$ against $y/\delta$: the layer has a similarity solution $u/U_e=f(y/\delta)$. Blasius and Falkner–Skan used that fact by changing variables and reducing the PDEs to an ODE. Pohlhausen's idea was cheaper: if the shape is universal, skip solving the BL equation and fit a polynomial to the normalised profile instead, then let the physics fix its coefficients.

### A quartic profile

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=a_0+a_1\eta+a_2\eta^2+a_3\eta^3+a_4\eta^4,\qquad\eta=\frac y\delta,\quad0\le\eta\le1.
$$

Above $\eta=1$, $u=U_e$. A fourth-order polynomial has five coefficients, $a_0$ to $a_4$, so five equations are needed.

> [!warning] A different η
> Here $\eta=y/\delta$, with $\delta$ a finite edge where the polynomial meets $U_e$. In 3.2, $\eta=y/\xi$ ran to infinity. Do not mix them.

The profile depends on $\eta$ alone, so its derivatives are ordinary ones:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\eta}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)=a_1+2a_2\eta+3a_3\eta^2+4a_4\eta^3,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)=2a_2+6a_3\eta+12a_4\eta^2.
$$

### Four boundary conditions

| Where | Condition | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| $\eta=0$ | no slip | $a_0=0$ (2) |
| $\eta=1$ | matched edge velocity | $a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4=1$ (3) |
| $\eta=1$ | smooth asymptote, zero slope | $a_1+2a_2+3a_3+4a_4=0$ (4) |
| $\eta=1$ | smooth asymptote, zero curvature | $2a_2+6a_3+12a_4=0$ (5) |

The first is the no-slip condition at the wall. The second says the profile reaches the edge velocity at $\eta=1$. The third and fourth say it meets the free stream smoothly, with no kink and no bend, so the first and second derivatives vanish there. That is four of the five equations; the fifth comes from the momentum equation itself.

### The BL equation at the wall

Textbooks call this condition the wall compatibility condition.

At the wall $u=v=0$, so both convective terms of the BL momentum equation vanish:

$$
\underbrace{u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{0}+\underbrace{v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}}_{0}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\left.\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}\right|_{y=0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
0=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\left.\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}\right|_{0}.
$$

Change variable, $u=U_e\cdot(u/U_e)$ and $y=\delta\eta$, so $\partial^2/\partial y^2=\delta^{-2}\,\mathrm d^2/\mathrm d\eta^2$:

$$
0=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{U_e}{\delta^2}\left.\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}.
$$

Divide by $\nu U_e/\delta^2$ and define $\lambda$:

$$
\boxed{\lambda\equiv\frac{\delta^2}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-\left.\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}=-2a_2}\qquad(6)
$$

The last equality uses the second derivative of the quartic at $\eta=0$, where the $6a_3\eta$ and $12a_4\eta^2$ terms vanish and leave $2a_2$. So $a_2=-\lambda/2$ (7), the fifth equation, and $\lambda$ is the **non-dimensional pressure gradient parameter**. Rearranged, this is revision Q2: $\dfrac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-\dfrac{\nu}{\delta^2}\left.\dfrac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\dfrac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}$.

So the curvature of the profile at the wall is set by the pressure gradient alone. An adverse gradient ($\lambda<0$) makes the profile curve the wrong way at the wall (concave towards $+u$), and since it has to curve the other way near the edge, the profile must have an inflection point. That inflection is why adverse gradients destabilise the layer ([[SESA3043 3.4 - Transition to Turbulence|3.4]]).

Once the coefficients are known (derived below) the inflection point can be located. The curvature is $2a_2+6a_3\eta+12a_4\eta^2$, a quadratic in $\eta$ that vanishes at $\eta=1$ by condition (5), so $\eta=1$ is one root. The product of the two roots is $2a_2/(12a_4)$, so the other root is

$$
\eta_i=\frac{a_2}{6a_4}=\frac{-\lambda/2}{6(1-\lambda/6)}=\frac{-\lambda}{2(6-\lambda)}.
$$

This lies inside the layer, $0<\eta_i<1$, only when $\lambda<0$: an adverse gradient gives $\eta_i=0.25$ at $\lambda=-6$ and $\eta_i=1/3$ at separation, the open circles in the bump figure above. A favourable gradient gives $\eta_i<0$, so the profile has no inflection point inside the layer.

### Solving the linear system

$$
(4)-(3):\quad a_2+2a_3+3a_4=-1\qquad(8)
$$

$$
(5)/2:\quad a_2+3a_3+6a_4=0\qquad(9)
$$

$$
(9)-(8):\quad a_3+3a_4=1\qquad(10)
$$

Substitute $3a_4=1-a_3$ from (10) into (8): $a_2+2a_3+(1-a_3)=-1$, i.e.

$$
a_2+a_3=-2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_3=-2-a_2=-2+\frac\lambda2.\qquad(11)
$$

From (10):

$$
a_4=\frac13(1-a_3)=\frac13\left(1+2-\frac\lambda2\right)=1-\frac\lambda6.\qquad(12)
$$

From (3):

$$
a_1=1-(a_2+a_3+a_4)=1-\left(-\frac\lambda2-2+\frac\lambda2+1-\frac\lambda6\right)=2+\frac\lambda6.\qquad(13)
$$

### The assembled profile

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=\left(2+\frac\lambda6\right)\eta-\frac\lambda2\eta^2+\left(\frac\lambda2-2\right)\eta^3+\left(1-\frac\lambda6\right)\eta^4.
$$

Separate the $\lambda$-free part from the $\lambda$ part:

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=\left(2\eta-2\eta^3+\eta^4\right)+\frac\lambda6\left(\eta-3\eta^2+3\eta^3-\eta^4\right).
$$

The bracket is $\eta(1-\eta)^3$, so

$$
\boxed{\frac{u}{U_e}=F(\eta)+\lambda\,G(\eta),\qquad F(\eta)=2\eta-2\eta^3+\eta^4,\qquad G(\eta)=\frac16\eta(1-\eta)^3.}
$$

*(Checked symbolically.)*

Written this way the profile is a superposition. $F(\eta)$, the first three terms, contains no $\lambda$: it is the flat-plate (zero pressure gradient) profile. $\lambda G(\eta)$ is the extra effect of the pressure gradient, and its size is set entirely by $\lambda$. With $\lambda=0$ the profile is the flat plate's.

## 3. Integral properties as functions of λ

The sign of $\lambda$ gives the type of pressure gradient and its magnitude the strength:

| $\lambda$ | Pressure gradient | Profile |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda=0$ | zero (ZPG) | flat plate, $u/U_e=F(\eta)$ |
| $\lambda>0$ | favourable (FPG) | fuller near the wall |
| $\lambda<0$ | adverse (APG) | emptier near the wall, with an inflection point |
| $\lambda=-12$ | adverse | laminar separation (revision Q5; derived in [[#Limits of the method]]) |

With the profile known as a function of $\lambda$, every integral quantity follows.

Integrate over $0\le\eta\le1$ (computed symbolically; $\mathrm dy=\delta\,\mathrm d\eta$):

$$
\frac{\delta^*}{\delta}=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac u{U_e}\right)\mathrm d\eta=\frac{3}{10}-\frac{\lambda}{120},
$$

$$
\frac{\theta}{\delta}=\int_0^1\frac u{U_e}\left(1-\frac u{U_e}\right)\mathrm d\eta=\frac{37}{315}-\frac{\lambda}{945}-\frac{\lambda^2}{9072},
$$

$$
\frac{\tau_w\delta}{\mu U_e}=\left.\frac{\mathrm d(u/U_e)}{\mathrm d\eta}\right|_0=a_1=2+\frac\lambda6.
$$

| $\lambda$ | $\delta^*/\delta$ | $\theta/\delta$ | $H$ | $\tau_w\delta/\mu U_e$ | state |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| −12 | 0.400 | 0.1143 | 3.50 | 0 | separation |
| −6 | 0.350 | 0.1198 | 2.92 | 1 | adverse |
| 0 | 0.300 | 0.1175 | 2.55 | 2 | flat plate |
| 6 | 0.250 | 0.1071 | 2.33 | 3 | favourable |
| 12 | 0.200 | 0.0889 | 2.25 | 4 | limit of validity |

The figure shows the profile for several values of $\lambda$, and the thicknesses and $H$ plotted against $\lambda$:

![[aa_pohlhausen_profiles.png|900]]

The shape factor $H=\delta^*/\theta$ is the column to watch, because it diagnoses the state of a layer ([[SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures|3.1]]). Pohlhausen's flat plate gives $H=2.55$, close to the exact Blasius 2.59. An adverse gradient raises $H$ above the zero-gradient value, all the way to 3.5 at separation, and a favourable one lowers it. So a measured or computed $H$ tells you whether a laminar layer is under zero, adverse or favourable pressure gradient.

The three laminar solutions of this chapter reach a profile in different ways. Blasius and Falkner–Skan introduce similarity variables and solve the boundary-layer equation exactly, with Falkner–Skan carrying the pressure gradient through $\beta$ and a tabulated solution for each $\beta$. Pohlhausen never solves the boundary-layer equation across the layer: it assumes a polynomial, fixes it with conditions at the wall and the edge, and carries the pressure gradient through $\lambda$.

### Limits of the method

Both ends of the range come out of the profile directly. Near the wall the linear term dominates, $u/U_e\approx a_1\eta=(2+\lambda/6)\eta$, so the wall slope falls to zero at $2+\lambda/6=0$, i.e. $\lambda=-12$: $\tau_w=0$, separation. This answers revision Q5. For $\lambda<-12$, $a_1<0$ and the quartic has reversed flow next to the wall (computed: $u/U_e$ is negative up to $\eta=0.07$ at $\lambda=-15$), which is no longer an attached layer.

At the other end, use the factorisations of the assembled profile. Since $1-F=1-2\eta+2\eta^3-\eta^4=(1-\eta)^3(1+\eta)$ and $G=\frac16\eta(1-\eta)^3$,

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}-1=-(1-F)+\lambda G=(1-\eta)^3\left[\frac{\lambda\eta}{6}-(1+\eta)\right].
$$

The factor $(1-\eta)^3$ is positive inside the layer, so $u$ exceeds $U_e$ wherever the bracket is positive. The bracket is largest at $\eta\to1$, where it equals $\lambda/6-2$, so an overshoot $u/U_e>1$ appears as soon as $\lambda>12$ (computed: a peak of 1.0005 at $\lambda=14$, 1.026 at 20). A velocity above the edge value inside a steady attached layer is unphysical, so the method is restricted to $-12\le\lambda\le12$.

The real separation point (from Falkner–Skan or exact solutions) occurs at a much milder gradient than $\lambda=-12$ suggests: the 4th-order polynomial is a rough shape, and Pohlhausen is known to predict separation late. Thwaites' method ([[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]) is more accurate.

> [!check] Flat plate by Pohlhausen + the momentum integral (extension)
> With $\lambda=0$, $\theta=\frac{37}{315}\delta$ and $\tau_w=2\mu U_e/\delta$. The flat-plate MIE (3.1, 3.5), $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=\tau_w/\rho U_e^2$, gives $\frac{37}{315}\delta\,\mathrm d\delta=\frac{2\nu}{U_e}\mathrm dx$, so $\delta^2=\frac{4\times315}{37}\frac{\nu x}{U_e}$:
>
> $$\frac{\delta}{x}=\frac{5.84}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad\frac{\delta^*}{x}=\frac{1.751}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad\frac{\theta}{x}=C_f=\frac{0.685}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad H=2.55,$$
>
> within 3.2% of Blasius (1.721, 0.664, 2.59). *(Computed.)*

## 4. What λ means

Since separation sits at $\lambda=-12$, with reversed flow and a wake downstream of it, $\lambda$ also says which conditions make a layer separate. $\lambda$ is dimensionless: $[\delta^2/\nu]=\text{m}^2/(\text{m}^2/\text{s})=\text{s}$ and $[\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx]=\text{s}^{-1}$.

Rewrite it with the Reynolds number based on the boundary-layer thickness, $Re_\delta=U_e\delta/\nu$, and with Bernoulli at the edge (§1), $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-\frac{1}{\rho U_e}\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$:

$$
\lambda=\frac{U_e\delta}{\nu}\cdot\frac{\delta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=Re_\delta\,\frac{\delta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-Re_\delta\,\frac{\delta}{\rho U_e^2}\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}.
$$

Physically, it is the ratio of the pressure force on the layer to the viscous force that holds the near-wall fluid to the outer flow:

$$
\lambda\sim\frac{\delta\cdot|\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx|}{\mu U_e/\delta}=\frac{\text{pressure-gradient force per unit area of wall}}{\text{viscous shear}}.
$$

### Which factors favour separation

Separation needs a large negative $\lambda$, so it is more likely whenever a factor in the last form makes $\lambda$ more negative. Each row changes one factor with the others held fixed at a given station:

| Separation is more likely with (others fixed) | Why, from $\lambda=-Re_\delta\frac{\delta}{\rho U_e^2}\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}$ |
|---|---|
| higher $Re$ | $Re_\delta$ multiplies $\lambda$: viscosity is relatively weaker at re-energising the wall fluid |
| thicker boundary layer | $\delta$ appears squared overall: the gradient acts over more fluid that viscosity must drag along |
| slower freestream $U_e$ | $1/U_e^2$: less dynamic pressure to resist the same pressure rise |
| stronger APG ($\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx>0$) | larger positive $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$ |

The freestream $U_e$ in the third row is the velocity at the edge of the layer.

> [!warning] Higher Re favours separation? Only with δ held fixed
> In a real flow the factors are not independent: $\delta$ itself depends on $\nu$. For a laminar layer on a body of length $L$ in a stream $U_\infty$, with $U_e=U_\infty f(x/L)$, the laminar growth law ([[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions|3.2]], and the momentum integral in 3.5) gives
>
> $$\delta^2=\frac{\nu L}{U_\infty}\,g\!\left(\frac xL\right).$$
>
> The gradient is
>
> $$\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{U_\infty}{L}f'\!\left(\frac xL\right).$$
>
> Substitute both into $\lambda=\delta^2U_e'/\nu$:
>
> $$\lambda=\frac{\nu L}{U_\infty}g\cdot\frac{U_\infty}{L}f'\cdot\frac1\nu=g\!\left(\frac xL\right)f'\!\left(\frac xL\right).$$
>
> $\nu$ and $U_\infty$ cancel. For geometrically similar laminar flow, $\lambda$ at a given $x/L$ is independent of Reynolds number, so the laminar separation point does not move with $Re$. The "higher $Re$" row is true only at fixed $\delta$, e.g. comparing two layers that happen to have the same thickness. The real Reynolds-number effect on separation comes from transition: at higher $Re$ the layer turns turbulent first, and a turbulent layer separates much later (§5). This is the drag crisis.

## 5. Laminar vs turbulent separation

In aeronautics the flow is usually wanted attached for as long as possible, and making the layer turbulent delays separation. In smoke pictures of the same curved surface the laminar layer separates near the crest, while a layer tripped near the leading edge stays attached much further round. Turbulent eddies mix the flow across the layer, carrying high-momentum fluid from the edge of the layer down to the slow-moving fluid near the wall. The near-wall fluid then has more momentum to push against the adverse gradient, and the turbulent profile is fuller (lower $H$, [[SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures|3.1]]).

On a bluff body this changes the drag. Measured from the front stagnation point, a laminar layer separates at about 80° and a turbulent one at about 130°. The turbulent layer leaves a smaller separation area and a narrower wake, and so a lower pressure drag. The same profile plot shows the trade-off: the turbulent profile has a much steeper velocity gradient at the wall, so its skin-friction drag is higher. The two effects compete, and on a bluff body the fall in pressure drag is much the bigger, so the total drag falls. On a streamlined body, where skin friction dominates, the balance can go the other way.

Sport uses this. The dimples on a golf ball are roughness that trips the layer, so transition comes early, separation is delayed and the pressure drag drops, which increases the range. The dimpled ball's drag coefficient falls from about 0.5 to about 0.25 between $Re\approx4\times10^4$ and $7\times10^4$, while a smooth sphere keeps $C_D\approx0.5$ until its own drag crisis at about $3.5\times10^5$. A 42.7 mm ball at 50–70 m/s in air ($\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s) has $Re=1.4$–$2.0\times10^5$ (computed): past the dimpled ball's drop, but short of the smooth sphere's.

A cricket ball is bowled with its seam at a small angle to the flight path, held there by spinning the ball. On one side the seam trips the layer, which becomes turbulent and separates late. The other side, kept polished, stays laminar and separates sooner. The flow is asymmetric, with the larger area of low pressure on the turbulent side, and that gives a normal force with respect to the flight path: swing. Roughening one side of the ball by hand does the same job and is against the laws of the game; the 2018 Cape Town Test is an example.

### Ways to delay separation

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| shaping (gentler pressure recovery) | reduces $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$, so $\vert \lambda\vert$ is smaller |
| trips, turbulators, dimples | early transition: fuller turbulent profile |
| vortex generators | small vanes make streamwise vortices that pump edge fluid to the wall |
| boundary-layer suction | removes the slow near-wall fluid (thins $\delta$, fills the profile) |
| tangential blowing, slats and slotted flaps | inject high-momentum fluid near the wall; each slot starts a fresh, thin layer |
| moving walls, active flow control | add momentum to the wall fluid directly |

## 6. Worked example: how close to separation? (suggested)

A laminar layer on a wing: $\delta=2$ mm, $U_e=30$ m/s, $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-20$ s⁻¹, $\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s, $\rho=1.225$ kg/m³.

1. $\lambda=\dfrac{(0.002)^2}{1.5\times10^{-5}}(-20)=\dfrac{4\times10^{-6}}{1.5\times10^{-5}}(-20)=-5.33$: adverse, still attached.
2. From §3: $\delta^*/\delta=0.3+5.33/120=0.344$, $\theta/\delta=0.1175+0.0056-0.0031=0.1200$, so $H=2.87$ (up from 2.55 on a flat plate).
3. The deceleration that would separate it: $\lambda=-12$ gives $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-12\nu/\delta^2=-12\times1.5\times10^{-5}/(4\times10^{-6})=-45$ s⁻¹.
4. The corresponding pressure gradient: $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx=-\rho U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=1.225\times30\times45=1650$ Pa/m. That is only 3% of the dynamic pressure (551 Pa) per centimetre of chord: laminar layers really are fragile.

## 7. Common mistakes

1. Using the Blasius $\eta=y/\xi$ in the Pohlhausen profile.
2. Forgetting the BL equation at the wall (the textbooks' compatibility condition) and trying to close the polynomial with a fifth edge condition.
3. Getting the sign of $\lambda$ backwards: negative is adverse.
4. Saying turbulence "reduces drag" without qualification: it reduces pressure drag on bluff bodies but increases skin friction.
