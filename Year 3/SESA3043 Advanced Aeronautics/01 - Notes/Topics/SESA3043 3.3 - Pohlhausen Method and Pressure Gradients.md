# SESA3043 3.3 - Pohlhausen Method and Pressure Gradients

> [!abstract] Summary
> Similarity solutions need $U_e=cx^m$. For a general $U_e(x)$, Pohlhausen (1921) **assumed** the profile shape: a quartic in $\eta=y/\delta$ with five coefficients. Four come from conditions at the wall and edge. The fifth comes from the BL momentum equation evaluated **at the wall**, where $u=v=0$ leaves $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial y^2|_0=-U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$. That brings in the single parameter
>
> $$\lambda=\frac{\delta^2}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx},\qquad\frac{u}{U_e}=F(\eta)+\lambda G(\eta),\quad F=2\eta-2\eta^3+\eta^4,\ G=\tfrac16\eta(1-\eta)^3.$$
>
> $\lambda$ compares the pressure force with the viscous force across the layer. $\lambda>0$ (favourable gradient) fills out the profile; $\lambda<0$ (adverse) empties it, and at $\lambda=-12$ the wall shear is zero: **separation**. Separation is more likely at high $Re$, with a thick layer, slow edge flow and a strong adverse gradient. Turbulent layers resist it much better, which is why golf balls have dimples and cricket balls swing.

## The topic in one physical picture

Follow the flow over a bump (slide 5, after Leishman).

1. Up the front, the outer flow **accelerates** ($\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx>0$), so the pressure **falls** downstream: a favourable pressure gradient. The pressure pushes every fluid particle forward, including the slow ones by the wall.
2. Over the top and down the back, the outer flow **decelerates** and the pressure **rises**: an adverse gradient. The pressure now pushes backwards on everything. Fast fluid near the edge has the momentum to coast through. Slow fluid near the wall does not.
3. Somewhere downstream the near-wall fluid is brought to rest: $\partial u/\partial y|_{wall}=0$. Beyond that it flows backwards, the layer lifts off and a recirculating wake forms. This is **separation**.

The pressure gradient is imposed by the outer flow and pressed straight through the layer ($\partial p/\partial y=0$, 1.4). So the layer's fate is decided by $U_e(x)$, and by how much momentum the layer has near the wall to resist it.

## 1. Pressure gradient terminology (slide 5)

From Bernoulli at the edge (1.4), $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx=-\rho U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$:

| External flow | $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ | $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$ | Name | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| accelerating | $>0$ | $<0$ | **favourable** (FPG) | thins the layer, stabilises it |
| uniform | 0 | 0 | zero (ZPG) | flat plate |
| decelerating | $<0$ | $>0$ | **adverse** (APG) | thickens the layer, can separate it |

**At separation**, $\partial u/\partial y|_{y=0}=0$. So $\tau_w=0$ and $C_f=0$ (revision Q1): the wall shear changes sign there, because downstream the flow near the wall is reversed.

## 2. The Pohlhausen quartic: derivation (slides 9–23; Notes Eqs. 1–14)

### Step 1: assume a profile

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=a_0+a_1\eta+a_2\eta^2+a_3\eta^3+a_4\eta^4,\qquad\eta=\frac y\delta,\quad0\le\eta\le1.
$$

Above $\eta=1$, $u=U_e$.

> [!warning] A different η
> Here $\eta=y/\delta$, with $\delta$ a finite edge where the polynomial meets $U_e$. In 3.2, $\eta=y/\xi$ ran to infinity. Do not mix them (slide 9).

Its derivatives:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\eta}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)=a_1+2a_2\eta+3a_3\eta^2+4a_4\eta^3,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)=2a_2+6a_3\eta+12a_4\eta^2.
$$

### Step 2: four boundary conditions

| Where | Condition | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| $\eta=0$ | no slip | $a_0=0$ (2) |
| $\eta=1$ | matched edge velocity | $a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4=1$ (3) |
| $\eta=1$ | smooth asymptote, zero slope | $a_1+2a_2+3a_3+4a_4=0$ (4) |
| $\eta=1$ | smooth asymptote, zero curvature | $2a_2+6a_3+12a_4=0$ (5) |

That is "4/5 equations found" (slides 12–17).

### Step 3: the wall compatibility condition (slide 18)

At the wall $u=v=0$, so both convective terms of the BL momentum equation vanish:

$$
\underbrace{u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{0}+\underbrace{v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}}_{0}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\left.\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}\right|_{y=0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
0=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\left.\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}\right|_{0}.
$$

Change variable, $u=U_e\cdot(u/U_e)$ and $y=\delta\eta$, so $\partial^2/\partial y^2=\delta^{-2}\,\mathrm d^2/\mathrm d\eta^2$:

$$
0=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{U_e}{\delta^2}\left.\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}.
$$

Divide by $\nu U_e/\delta^2$ and define $\lambda$:

$$
\boxed{\lambda\equiv\frac{\delta^2}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-\left.\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}=-2a_2}\qquad(6)
$$

so $a_2=-\lambda/2$ (7). Rearranged, this is revision **Q2**: $\dfrac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-\dfrac{\nu}{\delta^2}\left.\dfrac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm d\eta^2}\left(\dfrac{u}{U_e}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}$.

> [!tip] What the wall condition says
> The **curvature** of the profile at the wall is set by the pressure gradient alone. Adverse gradient ($\lambda<0$) makes the profile curve the "wrong" way at the wall (concave towards $+u$), and since it must curve the other way near the edge, the profile has an **inflection point**. That inflection is why adverse gradients destabilise the layer ([[SESA3043 3.4 - Transition to Turbulence|3.4]]).

### Step 4: solve the linear system (Notes Eqs. 8–13)

One algebraic move per line:

$$
(4)-(3):\quad a_2+2a_3+3a_4=-1\qquad(8)
$$

$$
(5)/2:\quad a_2+3a_3+6a_4=0\qquad(9)
$$

$$
(9)-(8):\quad a_3+3a_4=1\qquad(10)
$$

Substitute $3a_4=1-a_3$ from (10) into (8): $a_2+2a_3+(1-a_3)=-1$, i.e.

$$
a_2+a_3=-2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_3=-2-a_2=-2+\frac\lambda2.\qquad(11)
$$

From (10):

$$
a_4=\frac13(1-a_3)=\frac13\left(1+2-\frac\lambda2\right)=1-\frac\lambda6.\qquad(12)
$$

From (3):

$$
a_1=1-(a_2+a_3+a_4)=1-\left(-\frac\lambda2-2+\frac\lambda2+1-\frac\lambda6\right)=2+\frac\lambda6.\qquad(13)
$$

### Step 5: assemble (Notes Eq. 14)

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=\left(2+\frac\lambda6\right)\eta-\frac\lambda2\eta^2+\left(\frac\lambda2-2\right)\eta^3+\left(1-\frac\lambda6\right)\eta^4.
$$

Separate the $\lambda$-free part from the $\lambda$ part:

$$
\frac{u}{U_e}=\left(2\eta-2\eta^3+\eta^4\right)+\frac\lambda6\left(\eta-3\eta^2+3\eta^3-\eta^4\right).
$$

The bracket is $\eta(1-\eta)^3$, so

$$
\boxed{\frac{u}{U_e}=F(\eta)+\lambda\,G(\eta),\qquad F(\eta)=2\eta-2\eta^3+\eta^4,\qquad G(\eta)=\frac16\eta(1-\eta)^3.}
$$

*(Checked symbolically.)*

> [!warning] Sign slip in `Ch3_Notes_Pohlhausen.pdf`, p. 2
> The expanded bracket is printed $\frac\lambda6\eta(1-3\eta+3\eta^2+\eta^3)$. The last term should be $-\eta^3$, since $(1-\eta)^3=1-3\eta+3\eta^2-\eta^3$. The boxed result $G=\frac16\eta(1-\eta)^3$ and slide 23 are correct.

## 3. Integral properties as functions of λ

Integrate over $0\le\eta\le1$ (computed symbolically; $\mathrm dy=\delta\,\mathrm d\eta$):

$$
\frac{\delta^*}{\delta}=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac u{U_e}\right)\mathrm d\eta=\frac{3}{10}-\frac{\lambda}{120},
$$

$$
\frac{\theta}{\delta}=\int_0^1\frac u{U_e}\left(1-\frac u{U_e}\right)\mathrm d\eta=\frac{37}{315}-\frac{\lambda}{945}-\frac{\lambda^2}{9072},
$$

$$
\frac{\tau_w\delta}{\mu U_e}=\left.\frac{\mathrm d(u/U_e)}{\mathrm d\eta}\right|_0=a_1=2+\frac\lambda6.
$$

| $\lambda$ | $\delta^*/\delta$ | $\theta/\delta$ | $H$ | $\tau_w\delta/\mu U_e$ | state |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| −12 | 0.400 | 0.1143 | 3.50 | 0 | **separation** |
| −6 | 0.350 | 0.1198 | 2.92 | 1 | adverse |
| 0 | 0.300 | 0.1175 | 2.55 | 2 | flat plate |
| 6 | 0.250 | 0.1071 | 2.33 | 3 | favourable |
| 12 | 0.200 | 0.0889 | 2.25 | 4 | limit of validity |

This reproduces slide 26's right-hand plot.

### Limits of the method (slides 26–27, revision Q5)

- **Separation, $\lambda=-12$:** $\tau_w=0$ when $2+\lambda/6=0$. This answers revision Q5 (and is what slide 26 points to).
- **$\lambda>12$:** the profile overshoots, $u/U_e>1$ inside the layer (computed: 1.0005 at $\lambda=14$, 1.026 at 20), which is unphysical for a steady attached layer. So $-12\le\lambda\le12$.
- The real separation point (from Falkner–Skan or exact solutions) occurs at a much milder gradient than $\lambda=-12$ suggests: the quartic is a rough shape, and Pohlhausen is known to predict separation late. Thwaites' method ([[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]) is more accurate.

> [!check] Flat plate by Pohlhausen + the momentum integral (extension)
> With $\lambda=0$, $\theta=\frac{37}{315}\delta$ and $\tau_w=2\mu U_e/\delta$. The flat-plate MIE (3.1, 3.5), $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=\tau_w/\rho U_e^2$, gives $\frac{37}{315}\delta\,\mathrm d\delta=\frac{2\nu}{U_e}\mathrm dx$, so $\delta^2=\frac{4\times315}{37}\frac{\nu x}{U_e}$:
>
> $$\frac{\delta}{x}=\frac{5.84}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad\frac{\delta^*}{x}=\frac{1.751}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad\frac{\theta}{x}=C_f=\frac{0.685}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\quad H=2.55,$$
>
> within 3% of Blasius (1.721, 0.664, 2.59). *(Computed.)*

## 4. What λ means (revision Q3; slides 28–31)

$\lambda$ is dimensionless: $[\delta^2/\nu]=\text{m}^2/(\text{m}^2/\text{s})=\text{s}$ and $[\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx]=\text{s}^{-1}$.

Rewrite it with $Re_\delta=U_e\delta/\nu$ and Bernoulli, $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-\frac{1}{\rho U_e}\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$:

$$
\lambda=\frac{U_e\delta}{\nu}\cdot\frac{\delta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=Re_\delta\,\frac{\delta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=-Re_\delta\,\frac{\delta}{\rho U_e^2}\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}.
$$

Physically, it is the ratio of the **pressure force** on the layer to the **viscous force** that holds the near-wall fluid to the outer flow:

$$
\lambda\sim\frac{\delta\cdot|\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx|}{\mu U_e/\delta}=\frac{\text{pressure-gradient force per unit area of wall}}{\text{viscous shear}}.
$$

### Which factors favour separation (slides 29–31)

For an adverse gradient ($\lambda<0$), $|\lambda|$ grows, and separation becomes more likely, with:

| Factor | Why, from $\lambda=-Re_\delta\frac{\delta}{\rho U_e^2}\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}$ |
|---|---|
| **higher $Re$** | $Re_\delta$ multiplies $\lambda$: viscosity is relatively weaker at re-energising the wall fluid |
| **thicker layer** | $\delta$ appears squared overall: the gradient acts over more fluid that viscosity must drag along |
| **slower edge flow** | $1/U_e^2$: less dynamic pressure to resist the same pressure rise |
| **stronger APG** | larger $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx>0$ |

> [!tip] Higher Re favours separation?
> Only for a **laminar** layer with a fixed gradient. In practice higher $Re$ also brings transition, and a turbulent layer separates much later (next section). That is the drag crisis.

## 5. Laminar vs turbulent separation (slides 32–37)

- **Turbulence delays separation** (slide 33). Turbulent eddies mix high-momentum fluid from the edge down to the slow fluid near the wall. The near-wall flow has more momentum to push against the adverse gradient. The turbulent profile is **fuller** (slide 35's profile plot; lower $H$).
- **Bluff bodies** (slides 34–35): on a sphere, a laminar layer separates at about **80°** from the front stagnation point, a turbulent one at about **130°**. The turbulent wake is much narrower, so the **pressure drag** falls a lot. The skin-friction drag rises (larger wall gradient), but for a bluff body pressure drag dominates.
- **Golf ball** (slide 36): dimples trip the layer to turbulence early. The drag coefficient drops from about 0.5 to about 0.25 at $Re\approx4\times10^4$, well inside the speed range of a drive, rather than at $Re\approx3\times10^5$ for a smooth sphere. The ball goes further.
- **Cricket ball** (slide 37): the seam is held at a small angle to the flight path. On one side it trips the layer (turbulent, later separation); the other side, polished, stays laminar (earlier separation). The asymmetric wake gives a sideways force: **swing**. (Roughening one side by hand works too, and is illegal.)

### Ways to delay separation (revision Q6)

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| shaping (gentler pressure recovery) | reduces $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx$, so $|\lambda|$ is smaller |
| trips, turbulators, dimples | early transition: fuller turbulent profile |
| vortex generators | small vanes make streamwise vortices that pump edge fluid to the wall |
| boundary-layer suction | removes the slow near-wall fluid (thins $\delta$, fills the profile) |
| tangential blowing, slats and slotted flaps | inject high-momentum fluid near the wall; each slot starts a fresh, thin layer |
| moving walls, active flow control | add momentum to the wall fluid directly |

## 6. Worked example: how close to separation? (suggested)

A laminar layer on a wing: $\delta=2$ mm, $U_e=30$ m/s, $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-20$ s⁻¹, $\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s, $\rho=1.225$ kg/m³.

1. $\lambda=\dfrac{(0.002)^2}{1.5\times10^{-5}}(-20)=\dfrac{4\times10^{-6}}{1.5\times10^{-5}}(-20)=-5.33$: adverse, still attached.
2. From §3: $\delta^*/\delta=0.3+5.33/120=0.344$, $\theta/\delta=0.1175+0.0056-0.0031=0.1200$, so $H=2.87$ (up from 2.55 on a flat plate).
3. **Deceleration that would separate it:** $\lambda=-12$ gives $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=-12\nu/\delta^2=-12\times1.5\times10^{-5}/4\times10^{-6}=-45$ s⁻¹.
4. The corresponding pressure gradient: $\mathrm dp_e/\mathrm dx=-\rho U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=1.225\times30\times45=1650$ Pa/m. That is only 3% of the dynamic pressure (551 Pa) per centimetre of chord: laminar layers really are fragile.

## 7. Common mistakes

1. Using the Blasius $\eta=y/\xi$ in the Pohlhausen profile.
2. Forgetting the wall compatibility condition and trying to close the quartic with a fifth edge condition.
3. Getting the sign of $\lambda$ backwards: **negative** is adverse.
4. Saying turbulence "reduces drag" without qualification: it reduces **pressure** drag on bluff bodies but increases **skin friction**.
