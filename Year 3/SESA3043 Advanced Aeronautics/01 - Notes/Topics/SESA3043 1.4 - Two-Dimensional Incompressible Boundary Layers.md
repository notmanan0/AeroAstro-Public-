# SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers

> [!abstract] Summary
> At high Reynolds number, viscosity matters only in a **thin layer** next to a solid surface; outside it the flow is the potential flow of 1.3. Prandtl's insight was to treat the two regions separately. Inside the layer the streamwise length scale is $L$ but the cross-stream scale is the thickness $\delta\ll L$. An **order-of-magnitude analysis** of the 2-D steady incompressible Navier–Stokes equations then shows: continuity forces $v\sim U_e\delta/L$; in $x$-momentum the streamwise diffusion $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ is negligible, and balancing inertia with cross-stream diffusion gives $\delta/L\sim Re^{-1/2}$; in $y$-momentum every term is $O(Re^{-1})$ small, so $\partial p/\partial y=0$ and the pressure is imposed from outside, $-\tfrac1\rho\,\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx=U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$. The result is two equations for two unknowns,
>
> $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,\qquad u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2},$$
>
> which are **parabolic**: they can be marched downstream from an inflow profile, unlike the elliptic Navier–Stokes equations. The exact flat-plate (Blasius) solution confirms the scaling: $\delta_{99}=4.91\,x\,Re_x^{-1/2}$.

## The whole topic in one physical picture

Watch air flow along a wing at 50 m/s. A millimetre from the surface it is still doing 50 m/s; at the surface it is stopped dead (no slip). All of that velocity change is squeezed into a layer a few millimetres thick.

1. **Outside the layer** viscosity has nothing to work on: velocity gradients are gentle, so viscous stresses are tiny. This is the potential flow of 1.3, and it fixes the **pressure** and the **edge speed** $U_e(x)$.
2. **Inside the layer** gradients across the layer are huge, so viscosity matters, but gradients **along** it are as gentle as outside.
3. **The layer is too thin to support a pressure difference across it.** The outer flow's pressure is pressed straight through to the wall. That is why a pressure tapping on a wing measures the potential-flow pressure even though it sits under the boundary layer.
4. **The layer grows only by diffusion.** In time $t$ vorticity diffuses a distance $\sqrt{\nu t}$, and a parcel takes $t\sim x/U$ to reach station $x$. So $\delta\sim\sqrt{\nu x/U}$, i.e. $\delta/x\sim Re_x^{-1/2}$. The rest of the chapter makes this argument rigorous.

## 1. Prandtl's thin boundary-layer idea (slides 4–7)

**Assumptions:**

- high Reynolds number, so the viscous region is a **thin** layer on a solid surface;
- outside the layer: **potential flow** (1.3), negligible change of $u$ with $y$, $\partial u/\partial y\approx0$;
- at the wall: **no slip**, $u(y=0)=0$ (and no penetration, $v=0$);
- inside the layer: **rapid** change of $u$ with $y$, $\partial u/\partial y\neq0$.

**Notation** (slide 11, used again in Chapter 3):

- $U_\infty$: the **free-stream** velocity, far upstream of the body;
- $U_e(x)$: the velocity at the **edge** of the boundary layer, i.e. the potential-flow velocity just outside it. On a flat plate $U_e=U_\infty$; on a wing it varies with $x$.

> [!tip] Why the lecturer insists on two symbols (L5, ll. 197–208)
> *"If you use other textbooks, you might find that $U_\infty$ and $U_e$ are interchangeable. But in this case, I'm going to use this to differentiate, because $U_e$ might be different than $U_\infty$, and you will see this later on in chapter 3."* In Chapter 3 the Falkner–Skan flows have $U_e=cx^m$, so the distinction matters there.

The slides' sketch (laminar → transition → turbulent, with viscous sublayer and buffer zone) is a reminder that real layers become turbulent. The equations derived here are for the **laminar**, steady layer (turbulent layers are time-averaged versions with an extra Reynolds stress).

## 2. The starting equations (slides 9–10)

Incompressible continuity and Navier–Stokes in index form (from [[SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations|1.2]]):

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}+u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}=f_i-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}.
$$

Cross out $\partial u_i/\partial t$ (**steady**) and $f_i$ (**body force neglected**), and restrict to **2-D** ($u_1=u$, $u_2=v$, $w=0$, no $z$-dependence):

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,
$$

$$
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+\nu\left(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}\right),
$$

$$
u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}+\nu\left(\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial y^2}\right).
$$

Three equations, three unknowns $(u,v,p)$, elliptic. The goal is to throw away whatever is small inside a thin layer.

## 3. Order-of-magnitude analysis, step by step

![[aa_bl_order_of_magnitude_derivation.png|900]]

### The scales (slide 13)

$\mathcal O(\cdot)$ means "of the order of": the typical size, ignoring factors of 2 or $\pi$. *"It doesn't mean that something is equal to something else… The actual magnitude, no, but the order of magnitude is the same"* (L5, ll. 219–222). Signs are irrelevant too (ll. 253–254). Inside the layer (ll. 222–228):

$$
\mathcal O(x)=\mathcal O(L),\qquad\mathcal O(y)=\mathcal O(\delta),\qquad\mathcal O(u)=\mathcal O(U_e),\qquad\mathcal O(p)=\mathcal O(\rho U_e^2),\qquad\frac{\delta}{L}\ll1.
$$

A derivative is estimated as "change over distance": $\partial u/\partial x\sim U_e/L$ (u changes by about $U_e$ over a length $L$), but $\partial u/\partial y\sim U_e/\delta$ (the same change happens across the much shorter $\delta$).

> [!tip] Why $p\sim\rho U_e^2$
> From Bernoulli outside the layer, pressure changes are of the size of the dynamic pressure $\tfrac12\rho U_e^2$. It is the pressure **changes** that drive the flow, so this is the right scale for $\partial p/\partial x$.

### Step 1: continuity fixes v (slides 14–16)

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,\qquad
\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)=\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{U_e}{L}\right),\qquad
\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)=\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{v}{\delta}\right).
$$

Two terms must cancel to give zero, so they must be the **same size** (if one were much larger, nothing could balance it; L5, ll. 239–243):

$$
\frac{v}{\delta}\sim\frac{U_e}{L}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\boxed{\mathcal O(v)=\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{U_e\,\delta}{L}\right)\ll U_e}.
$$

The flow in the layer is nearly parallel to the wall.

### Step 2: x-momentum, term by term (slides 17–24)

| Term | Estimate | Order |
|---|---|---|
| $u\,\partial u/\partial x$ | $U_e\cdot U_e/L$ | $U_e^2/L$ |
| $v\,\partial u/\partial y$ | $(U_e\delta/L)\cdot(U_e/\delta)$ | $U_e^2/L$ |
| $\frac1\rho\,\partial p/\partial x$ | $\rho U_e^2/(\rho L)$ | $U_e^2/L$ |
| $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ | $\nu U_e/L^2$ | $\nu U_e/L^2$ |
| $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial y^2$ | $\nu U_e/\delta^2$ | $\nu U_e/\delta^2$ |

Notice the second row: $v$ is small, but $\partial u/\partial y$ is large, and the two effects cancel exactly. **Both convective terms are the same size.**

**Compare the two viscous terms:**

$$
\frac{\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial x^2}{\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial y^2}\sim\frac{\nu U_e/L^2}{\nu U_e/\delta^2}=\left(\frac{\delta}{L}\right)^2\ll1.
$$

So $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ is dropped: *"variation in $y$ is much greater than in $x$"* (slide 21).

**The surviving viscous term must matter.** If it were negligible too, there would be no viscous effect at all and no way to satisfy no-slip. So it must be the same order as the inertia terms (slide 24):

$$
\frac{U_e^2}{L}\sim\frac{\nu U_e}{\delta^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\delta^2}{L^2}\sim\frac{\nu}{U_eL}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{\delta}{L}\right)=\mathcal O\!\left(Re^{-1/2}\right),\qquad Re=\frac{U_eL}{\nu}}.
$$

This is revision §1.4 Q5, which the lecture points to: *"if you rearrange this equation a bit, in fact, one of the revision questions… asks you to do this"* (L5, ll. 291–295). It also tells us exactly how small the dropped term was: $(\delta/L)^2\sim Re^{-1}$.

> [!question] Lecture Q&A: why does $v\,\partial u/\partial y$ survive? (L5, ll. 286–289)
> A student asked how the second term ends up as $U_e^2/L$. *"$v$ is the order of $U_e\delta/L$. $\partial u/\partial y$ is $U_e/\delta$, the $\delta$ term cancels."* Small $v$ times large gradient: both convective terms are the same size.

**Result:**

$$
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
$$

### Step 3: y-momentum (slides 25–31)

Using $v\sim U_e\delta/L$ and $y\sim\delta$. The lecturer writes each term as $(U_e^2/\delta)\times(\text{something})$ on purpose, *"multiply this by delta on delta… You'll see the reason in a minute"* (L5, ll. 304–306, 317–319), so that the $Re^{-1}$ factors line up:

| Term | Estimate | In units of $U_e^2/\delta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $u\,\partial v/\partial x$ | $U_e\cdot\dfrac{U_e\delta}{L^2}$ | $\left(\dfrac{\delta}{L}\right)^2=Re^{-1}$ |
| $v\,\partial v/\partial y$ | $\dfrac{U_e\delta}{L}\cdot\dfrac{U_e\delta/L}{\delta}$ | $\left(\dfrac{\delta}{L}\right)^2=Re^{-1}$ |
| $\nu\,\partial^2v/\partial y^2$ | $\nu\dfrac{U_e\delta/L}{\delta^2}=\dfrac{\nu U_e}{L\delta}$ | $\dfrac{\nu}{U_eL}=Re^{-1}$ |
| $\nu\,\partial^2v/\partial x^2$ | $\nu\dfrac{U_e\delta}{L^3}$ | $Re^{-2}$ |
| $\frac1\rho\,\partial p/\partial y$ | $\dfrac{\Delta p_y}{\rho\,\delta}$ | ? |

The slides fill the last row by assuming the largest conceivable value, $\Delta p_y\sim\rho U_e^2$, which gives $U_e^2/\delta$, i.e. $\mathcal O(1)$ in the last column. Everything else in the equation is $\mathcal O(Re^{-1})$: at high $Re$ there is nothing left that could balance a pressure gradient that large. The only consistent conclusion is that it is **not** that large. The pressure gradient can be at most the size of the other terms:

$$
\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}\sim\frac{U_e^2\delta}{L^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Delta p_{\text{across}}\sim\rho U_e^2\left(\frac{\delta}{L}\right)^2=\rho U_e^2\,Re^{-1}.
$$

That is negligible compared with the streamwise pressure changes $\sim\rho U_e^2$. To leading order,

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p=p_e(x)}.
$$

> [!important] The physical impact of the BL $y$-momentum equation (revision §1.4 Q3)
> - The pressure is **constant across** the layer at each $x$ (slide 31: *"the pressure is constant in the BL… solely a function of $x$"*).
> - So the pressure in the layer is **imposed** by the outer potential flow; the layer cannot generate its own pressure field.
> - That turns $p$ from an unknown into a known function, leaving 2 equations for 2 unknowns.
> - It is why wall pressure tappings measure the inviscid pressure distribution, and why potential-flow $C_p$ is useful even though the real flow is viscous (until separation).
> - It fails where the wall is strongly curved (radius of curvature comparable to $\delta$) or where the layer separates.

### Step 4: Bernoulli at the edge (slides 33–37; revision §1.4 Q4)

*Lectured at L5, ll. 359–376; the derivation is "part of your revision question, so you can do the derivation yourself at home"* (ll. 368–369).

Just outside the layer the flow is inviscid and irrotational, so steady Bernoulli holds:

$$
p_e+\tfrac12\rho U_e^2=\text{const}.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $x$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dp_e}{\mathrm dx}+\rho U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=0.
$$

Inside the layer $p=p_e(x)$, so the pressure gradient in the $x$-momentum equation is the edge one, and it is an **ordinary** derivative:

$$
\boxed{-\frac1\rho\frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dx}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}}.
$$

- $U_e$ increasing (accelerating outer flow, $\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx<0$): **favourable** pressure gradient, the layer stays thin and attached.
- $U_e$ decreasing ($\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx>0$): **adverse** gradient; the slow fluid near the wall can be brought to rest and reversed: [[Boundary Layer Separation]].

> [!tip] Why write it with $U_e$ rather than $p$ (L5, ll. 377–388)
> *"It's more likely that $U_e$ is known rather than $p$."* In an experiment or a simulation (e.g. your individual project), $U_e$ is what you **set**: the tunnel speed, or a boundary condition. The pressure and velocity fields are what you want to **get**. With $U_e(x)$ known, the BL equations have two unknowns, $u$ and $v$, and two equations, so they can be solved.

> [!question] Lecture Q&A: how can potential flow and a viscous boundary layer coexist? (L5, ll. 403–420)
> Inside the layer $\partial u/\partial y$ is very large, so the viscous stress matters. From the edge outwards the gradient is negligible, so the flow is effectively inviscid and potential flow applies. *"Where exactly the boundaries are between viscous and inviscid? Chapter three."* See [[SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures]] for the thickness definitions.

## 4. The boundary-layer equations

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,\qquad
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}+\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}}
$$

**Boundary conditions:**

- wall: $u(x,0)=0$ and $v(x,0)=0$;
- edge: $u(x,y)\to U_e(x)$ as $y/\delta\to\infty$;
- inflow: a known profile $u(x_0,y)$ at the starting station.

**What changed compared with Navier–Stokes** (slides 37–40):

| | 2-D Navier–Stokes | 2-D boundary layer |
|---|---|---|
| unknowns | $u,v,p$ | $u,v$ ($p$ supplied by potential flow) |
| equations | continuity + 2 momentum | continuity + $x$-momentum |
| streamwise diffusion | $\nu\,\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ present | absent |
| PDE type | elliptic | **parabolic** |
| how to solve | the whole domain at once, with conditions on every boundary | **march** downstream from an inflow profile |

### Why parabolic matters (revision §1.4 Q6)

Without $\partial^2/\partial x^2$ terms, nothing downstream can influence the solution upstream: information travels only downstream, like heat along a time axis. A numerical solver can therefore start from the inflow profile and step in $x$, solving a 1-D problem in $y$ at each station. That costs a tiny fraction of a full 2-D Navier–Stokes solution, which must be solved iteratively over the whole domain with conditions on every boundary.

> [!warning] Slide and lecture wording: "Matching Technique"
> Slide 37, and the lecture (L5, ll. 392–393: *"they can be solved with something called matching technique. So we start from a known inflow condition and then you match downstream condition"*), say the BL equations *"can be solved using **Matching Technique**, starting from a known inflow condition, matching downstream"*. The standard term, and the meaning described, is a **marching** technique: march downstream station by station. (Matching, as in "matched asymptotic expansions", is a different idea: joining the inner boundary-layer solution to the outer potential flow, which here is done through $U_e(x)$.)

### The hierarchy of governing equations (slide 40)

| Flow model | Unknowns | Equations | Type | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-D compressible N–S | $\rho,u,v,w,p,E_T$ | 5 PDEs (mass, 3 momentum, energy) | elliptic (steady, subsonic) | hyperbolic character for supersonic flow (see [[SESA3029 Aerothermodynamics Hub\|SESA3029]]) |
| 3-D incompressible N–S | $u,v,w,p$ | 4 PDEs | elliptic | $\rho=$ const |
| 2-D incompressible N–S | $u,v,p$ | 3 PDEs | elliptic | $w=0$ |
| 2-D incompressible BL | $u,v$ | 2 PDEs | **parabolic** | $p$ from potential flow |
| potential flow | $\phi,p$ | Laplace for $\phi$, then Bernoulli | elliptic | irrotational |

Read down the table as a chain of approximations, each with its stated assumption. Potential flow and the BL equations are partners: potential flow gives $U_e(x)$, the BL equations give the viscous layer underneath (viscous–inviscid interaction).

## 5. Check: the exact flat-plate solution (Blasius)

*(Extension, beyond the slides. It confirms that the order-of-magnitude result is right, and gives the constant.)*

On a flat plate $U_e=U$ constant, so $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=0$. The scaling suggests the profile shape depends only on $y/\delta(x)$ with $\delta\propto\sqrt{\nu x/U}$. Try a **similarity** variable and streamfunction:

$$
\eta=y\sqrt{\frac{U}{\nu x}},\qquad\psi=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f(\eta).
$$

**Velocities.** With $\partial\eta/\partial y=\sqrt{U/\nu x}$ and $\partial\eta/\partial x=-\eta/(2x)$:

$$
u=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f'\sqrt{\frac{U}{\nu x}}=Uf',
$$

$$
v=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=-\left[\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu U}{x}}\,f+\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f'\left(-\frac{\eta}{2x}\right)\right]=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu U}{x}}\left(\eta f'-f\right).
$$

Continuity is satisfied automatically, because $u$ and $v$ come from a streamfunction.

**Derivatives of $u$:**

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=Uf''\left(-\frac{\eta}{2x}\right),\qquad
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=Uf''\sqrt{\frac{U}{\nu x}},\qquad
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=Uf'''\frac{U}{\nu x}.
$$

**Substitute into $x$-momentum** ($u\,u_x+v\,u_y=\nu u_{yy}$):

$$
Uf'\left(-\frac{U\eta f''}{2x}\right)+\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu U}{x}}\left(\eta f'-f\right)Uf''\sqrt{\frac{U}{\nu x}}=\nu\,\frac{U^2f'''}{\nu x}.
$$

The middle factor simplifies, $\sqrt{\nu U/x}\sqrt{U/\nu x}=U/x$:

$$
-\frac{U^2}{2x}\eta f'f''+\frac{U^2}{2x}\left(\eta f'f''-ff''\right)=\frac{U^2}{x}f'''.
$$

The $\eta f'f''$ terms cancel. Multiply by $x/U^2$:

$$
\boxed{f'''+\tfrac12ff''=0},\qquad f(0)=f'(0)=0,\quad f'(\infty)=1.
$$

$x$ and $y$ have disappeared: the profiles at all stations collapse onto one curve. *(Verified symbolically.)* This ODE is solved by the shooting method of [[Shooting Method and the Blasius Solution|SESA2029]]: guess $f''(0)$, integrate, adjust until $f'(\infty)=1$.

![[aa_bl_scaling_check.png|900]]

**Results** (computed for this note, with RK45 and bisection on $f''(0)$):

| Quantity | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $f''(0)$ | 0.33206 | wall shear: $C_f=\dfrac{\tau_w}{\frac12\rho U^2}=\dfrac{2f''(0)}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\dfrac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}}$ |
| $\eta$ where $f'=0.99$ | 4.91 | $\delta_{99}=4.91\,x\,Re_x^{-1/2}$ |
| $\int_0^\infty(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta$ | 1.7208 | displacement thickness $\delta^*=1.721\,x\,Re_x^{-1/2}$ |
| $\int_0^\infty f'(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta$ | 0.6641 | momentum thickness $\theta=0.664\,x\,Re_x^{-1/2}$ |
| $\delta^*/\theta$ | 2.59 | shape factor $H$ (laminar flat plate) |
| $\tfrac12(\eta f'-f)$ as $\eta\to\infty$ | 0.8604 | edge outflow $v_e=0.860\,U\,Re_x^{-1/2}$ |

The exponent $-\tfrac12$ is exactly the order-of-magnitude prediction; the analysis cannot give the constant (4.91), only the scaling. See [[Displacement and Momentum Thickness]].

> [!check] $v$ really is of order $U\delta/L$
> The table gives $v_e/U=0.860\,Re_x^{-1/2}$ and $\delta_{99}/x=4.91\,Re_x^{-1/2}$. The ratio $v_e/(U\delta/x)=0.175$: the same order, as Step 1 predicted. The non-zero $v_e$ is the displacement effect: the layer pushes the outer flow away from the wall.

## 6. Worked examples

### 6.1 How thin is "thin"? (suggested)

Air at $U=50$ m/s over a plate of length $L=1$ m, $\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s.

- $Re_L=\dfrac{50\times1}{1.5\times10^{-5}}=3.33\times10^6$.
- Order estimate: $\delta/L\sim Re^{-1/2}=5.5\times10^{-4}$, i.e. about 0.5 mm.
- Blasius: $\delta_{99}=4.91\times1/\sqrt{3.33\times10^6}=2.69$ mm (if the layer stayed laminar; at this $Re$ it would in fact have become turbulent part-way).

So $\delta/L\approx0.003$: very thin indeed.

### 6.2 How small is the pressure change across the layer? (suggested)

Same flow. $\Delta p_{\text{across}}\sim\rho U^2(\delta/L)^2=1.225(50^2)(0.00269)^2=0.022$ Pa, against a dynamic pressure $\tfrac12\rho U^2=1531$ Pa. The ratio is $1.4\times10^{-5}$: $\partial p/\partial y=0$ is an excellent approximation.

### 6.3 Where does the theory fail? (suggested)

- **Low $Re$:** at $Re\sim1$, $\delta\sim L$: there is no thin layer and the full equations are needed (e.g. microfluidics, insects).
- **Near the leading edge:** $Re_x=Ux/\nu\to0$ as $x\to0$, so the BL equations are not valid within a distance of order $\nu/U$ ($\sim$ microns at 50 m/s) of the edge.
- **Separation:** where $\partial u/\partial y|_{wall}=0$ the layer leaves the wall, $v$ is no longer small and the outer flow is changed; the parabolic marching breaks down (the Goldstein singularity).
- **Strong curvature** and **shocks** (SESA3029) also violate the assumptions.

## 7. Common mistakes

1. Estimating $\partial u/\partial y$ as $U_e/L$ instead of $U_e/\delta$.
2. Concluding that $v\,\partial u/\partial y$ is negligible because $v$ is small: it is not, because $\partial u/\partial y$ is large.
3. Writing $\partial p/\partial x$ as an unknown; in the BL equations it is a known function, $-\rho U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$.
4. Using $U_\infty$ where $U_e(x)$ is meant (they differ everywhere except on a flat plate).
5. Calling the BL equations elliptic, or describing their solution as "matching" rather than marching.
