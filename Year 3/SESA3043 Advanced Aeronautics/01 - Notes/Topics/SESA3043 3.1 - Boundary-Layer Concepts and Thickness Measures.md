# SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures

> [!abstract] Summary
> A boundary layer is described by a few thickness measures. The edge thickness $\delta_{99}$ is a convention; the integral thicknesses are better defined. The displacement thickness $\delta^*$ is how far the outer flow is pushed away, giving the effective body, and the momentum thickness $\theta$ measures the momentum lost, which on a flat plate gives the drag, $D=\rho U_e^2\theta$ and $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$. Their ratio, the shape factor $H$, indicates the state of the layer.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** you need $\delta_{99}$, $\delta^*$, $\theta$, $H$ or $C_f$ from a profile, or drag from $\theta$.
> - **Assumptions:** 2-D incompressible boundary layer; $D'=\rho U_e^2\theta$ and $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$ only for a flat plate with zero pressure gradient.
> - **Method:** 1. Write $u/U_e$ in $\eta=y/\delta$. 2. $\delta^*=\delta\int(1-u/U_e)\,\mathrm d\eta$. 3. $\theta=\delta\int\frac u{U_e}(1-\frac u{U_e})\,\mathrm d\eta$. 4. $H=\delta^*/\theta$. 5. $\tau_w=\mu\,\partial u/\partial y|_0$ → $C_f$; drag per side $\rho U_e^2\theta$.
> - **Common mistakes:** swapping the $\delta^*$ and $\theta$ integrands; using $D'=\rho U_e^2\theta$ with a pressure gradient.
> - **Practise:** [[#7. Worked example: the linear profile]] · [[#8. Worked example: a laminar plate in numbers (suggested)]] · [[SESA3043 Ch3 Revision Questions - Worked Solutions#3.1 Introduction|Ch3 revision 3.1]]

---

A stream of smoke released at some height at the leading edge of a plate in a wind drifts upwards downstream: the slowed air near the wall takes up more room, so the outer streamlines are pushed away from the surface. The outer flow behaves as if the plate had grown thicker by $\delta^*$. That is the displacement effect.

At the same time, the plate is pulling momentum out of the air. Air that left the leading edge at $U_e$ arrives at the trailing edge slower. The momentum the plate has removed is its drag. If you measure how much momentum flux is missing compared with a uniform stream, expressed as an equivalent thickness, you get $\theta$, and the drag follows directly.

## 1. Prandtl's thin boundary-layer theory, revisited

The governing equations simplify in steps:

| Flow type | Unknowns | Equations | Type | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-D compressible | $\rho,u,v,w,p,E_T$ | 5 PDEs: continuity, 3 momentum, energy | elliptic | hyperbolic for supersonic flow |
| 3-D incompressible | $u,v,w,p$ | 4 PDEs: continuity, 3 momentum | elliptic | $\rho=$ const. |
| 2-D incompressible | $u,v,p$ | 3 PDEs: continuity, 2 momentum | elliptic | $w=0$ |
| 2-D incompressible boundary layer | $u,v$ | 2 PDEs: continuity, $x$-momentum | parabolic | $p$ from the potential flow |
| potential flow | $\phi,p$ | Laplace for $\phi$, then Bernoulli | elliptic (Laplace) | inviscid, irrotational |

The methods of Chapter 2 belong to the bottom row. The fourth row, the boundary-layer equations derived in 1.4, is solved in Chapter 3. The two rows fit together through Prandtl's picture. At high Reynolds number the layer is thin, $\delta\ll x$ (1.4: $\delta/L\sim Re^{-1/2}$). Outside the layer and on its edge the flow is inviscid and irrotational, so the potential-flow methods of 1.3 and Chapter 2, with Bernoulli, give $U_e(x)$ and the pressure. Inside the layer the flow is viscous and obeys the reduced boundary-layer equations of 1.4, with that pressure imposed from outside ($\partial p/\partial y=0$). The boundary-layer row is parabolic, so a solution can be marched downstream from the leading edge, which is what makes the similarity and integral methods of this chapter possible.

A flat-plate layer starts laminar at the leading edge, passes through a transition region and becomes turbulent, with a viscous sublayer and buffer zone near the wall. Laminar layers are treated in 3.2–3.3, transition in 3.4, integral methods valid for both in 3.5 and the coupling with the outer flow in 3.6. Turbulent layers in depth are SESA6061 Turbulence, an optional fourth-year module.

## 2. Boundary-layer thickness δ99

$u\to U_e$ only asymptotically, so the "edge" is a convention:

$$
\boxed{\delta\equiv\delta_{99}:\quad u(\delta_{99})=0.99\,U_e.}
$$

It is easy to picture but awkward to measure (the profile is nearly flat there, so a tiny velocity error moves $\delta_{99}$ a lot), and it is not what the force or the outer flow depends on. The integral thicknesses are better on both counts.

Strictly, $\delta$ is where $u=U_e$, but $u$ only reaches $U_e$ asymptotically, and in a turbulent layer, whose edge fluctuates, the point is even harder to pin down. The 99 % rule is a practical convention, and the choice of 99 rather than, say, 99.5 is arbitrary: for Blasius, $\eta_{99}=3.47$ but $\eta_{99.5}=3.73$, a 7 % change in "the" thickness.

Plotting $u/U_e$ against $y/\delta$ puts layers of any size on the same axes, so laminar and turbulent layers can be compared directly. The turbulent profile is **fuller**: it gets close to $U_e$ much nearer the wall, so its wall gradient $\partial u/\partial y|_0$ is steeper. Since $\tau_w=\mu\,\partial u/\partial y|_0$ (§5), a turbulent layer has more friction drag.

> [!check] How much fuller (computed)
> At the same $y/\delta_{99}$, Blasius against the 1/7 power law:
>
> | $y/\delta_{99}$ | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.5 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | laminar (Blasius) $u/U_e$ | 0.16 | 0.32 | 0.74 |
> | turbulent ($(y/\delta)^{1/7}$) $u/U_e$ | 0.72 | 0.79 | 0.91 |
>
> The laminar wall slope is $\mathrm d(u/U_e)/\mathrm d(y/\delta_{99})=f''(0)\,\eta_{99}=0.4696\times3.47=1.63$. The power law is infinitely steep at the wall: it describes the outer turbulent layer only, not the viscous sublayer.

## 3. Displacement thickness

### Derivation from the mass-flow defect

Compare the real layer with a hypothetical uniform flow $U_e$ right down to the wall. Per unit span, the mass-flow defect (area $A$ in panel b) is

$$
A=\int_0^\infty\rho\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

The displacement thickness $\delta^*$ is the distance the wall must be moved into the uniform stream to lose the same mass flow. A slab of height $\delta^*$ carries $\rho U_e\delta^*$:

$$
\rho U_e\delta^*=\int_0^\infty\rho\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

Divide by $\rho U_e$ (incompressible):

$$
\boxed{\delta^*=\int_0^\infty\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.}
$$

### Physical meaning: the effective body

Adding $\delta^*$ to the surface gives the apparent (effective) body seen by the outer flow. On a flat plate, the growth of $\delta^*$ is what pushes the outer streamlines away: the edge outflow is $v_e=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx$ (1.4 Blasius check: $v_e=0.860\,U_eRe_x^{-1/2}$).

This also locates the boundary between the potential-flow region and the viscous region: it is the displaced surface. Thicken the body by $\delta^*$, apply the potential-flow methods of Chapter 2 outside it, and use a viscous solution inside it. This is the basis of viscous–inviscid interaction ([[SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction|3.6]]). The procedure is circular: $\delta^*$ itself has to come from a viscous solution, which needs $U_e(x)$ from the outer flow. Chapter 3 provides the viscous solutions, and 3.6 closes the loop by iterating between the two.

> [!check] Blasius numbers
> For the laminar flat plate, $\delta^*=1.721\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}$ and $\delta_{99}=4.91\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}$, so $\delta^*\approx0.35\,\delta_{99}$ (panel b, computed). The wall would need to move out about a third of the layer thickness.

## 4. Momentum thickness θ: the control-volume derivation

Assumptions: flat plate; zero pressure gradient ($U_e=U_\infty$, and $\partial p/\partial y=0$, so the pressure is uniform on the whole control volume and contributes no net force); incompressible; steady.

**Control volume** (panel d): inlet 1 upstream of the plate, uniform $U_e$ over height $h_1$; outlet 2 at the trailing edge, profile $u(y)$ over height $h_2$; the top follows a streamline, so no mass crosses it; the bottom is the plate, which exerts the drag $D$ (per unit span) on the fluid in the $-x$ direction.

**Mass balance.**

$$
\dot m_1=\dot m_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho U_eh_1=\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy.
$$

Multiply both sides by $U_e$ (this will be needed to eliminate $h_1$):

$$
\rho U_e^2h_1=U_e\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy.\qquad(1)
$$

**$x$-momentum balance.** The net momentum outflow equals the force on the fluid, which is the plate's $-D$:

$$
\dot M_2-\dot M_1=-D
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy-\rho U_e^2h_1=-D.
$$

Rearrange:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2h_1-\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy.
$$

**Eliminate $h_1$ with (1).**

$$
D=U_e\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy-\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy=\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

**Non-dimensionalise.** Take $\rho U_e^2$ outside:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2\int_0^{h_2}\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.
$$

**Let $h_2\to\infty$.** Above the layer $u=U_e$, so the integrand is zero there and extending the limit changes nothing:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy\equiv\rho U_e^2\,\theta,
\qquad
\boxed{\theta=\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.}
$$

The integrand $\rho u(U_e-u)$ is the momentum deficit carried by the fluid that actually passes through: a mass flux $\rho u$, each unit of which has lost speed $U_e-u$. $\theta$ is the thickness of a uniform stream whose momentum flux equals that deficit, and the momentum the fluid loses is the momentum given to the plate, which is the drag. Equivalently, $\theta$ is how far the wall would have to move up, in a uniform stream $U_e$, to lose the same momentum flow, just as $\delta^*$ does for mass flow. A memory aid: displacement thickness goes with the effective body, momentum thickness with drag.

> [!warning] Why $u(U_e-u)$ and not $U_e(U_e-u)$
> Using $U_e(U_e-u)$ would count the defect of fluid that never entered the control volume. Mass conservation is what turns the $h_1$ term into $\int\rho uU_e$ and makes the weighting $u$, not $U_e$. A common exam slip is to leave out the mass balance and write $D=\rho\int(U_e^2-u^2)\,\mathrm dy$, which over-counts.

## 5. Wall shear, skin friction and the drag link

$$
\tau_w=\mu\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0},
\qquad
\boxed{C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U_e^2}.}
$$

Link to $\theta$: the drag on a plate of length $x$ is the integral of the local wall shear, $D(x)=\int_0^x\tau_w\,\mathrm dx'$. Differentiate both expressions for $D$ with respect to $x$ ($U_e$ constant):

$$
\frac{\mathrm dD}{\mathrm dx}=\tau_w=\rho U_e^2\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_e^2}=\frac{C_f}{2}.}
$$

This is the flat-plate form of the momentum integral equation of [[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]. It says the layer thickens (in momentum terms) exactly as fast as the wall drags on it.

This holds for a flat plate only: the general result is derived more rigorously as the momentum integral equation in 3.5. The same result also comes out of the Blasius solution ([[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions#Skin friction|3.2 §5]]).

With a pressure gradient the definitions of $\delta^*$, $\theta$, $H$ and $C_f$ are unchanged, since they are integrals of whatever profile is there, but the control-volume argument of §4 breaks. The pressure is no longer uniform on the control volume, so the plate's friction is not the only force removing momentum, and $U_e$ changes along $x$, so the momentum flux of the "uniform stream" being compared against changes too. The general balance ([[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]) is

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}+(2+H)\frac{\theta}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{C_f}{2},
$$

which reduces to the boxed result above when $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=0$. In an adverse gradient the second term is negative, so $\theta$ grows faster than friction alone would make it, and the shape factor rises (§6).

## 6. Shape factor H

$$
\boxed{H=\frac{\delta^*}{\theta}}
$$

Because $u/U_e\le1$, $\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})\le1-\frac{u}{U_e}$, so $\theta<\delta^*$ and $H>1$ always.

| Layer | $H$ | Source |
|---|---|---|
| laminar flat plate (Blasius) | 2.59 | [[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions\|3.2]] |
| laminar stagnation point ($\beta=1$) | 2.22 | 3.2 |
| laminar, at separation (Falkner–Skan) | 4.03 | 3.2 |
| turbulent flat plate (1/7 power law) | 1.29 | [[SESA2022 T2 - Boundary Layers\|SESA2022]] |

A fuller profile (more momentum near the wall: turbulent, or accelerating) has a lower $H$; an emptier one (decelerating, near separation) has a higher $H$. $H$ is therefore used as an indicator of the state of the boundary layer and as a closure in integral methods (3.5–3.6).

Although $H$ is a single number, it tells you whether a layer has zero or non-zero pressure gradient, and whether it is laminar or turbulent. The laminar flat-plate value is about 2.59, which follows from Blasius: $H=1.2168/0.4696=2.591$ ([[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions#Integral type B|3.2 §5]]).

## 7. Worked example: the linear profile

Profile: $u/U_e=y/\delta$ for $y\le\delta$, and $1$ above. Let $\eta=y/\delta$, $\mathrm dy=\delta\,\mathrm d\eta$. The integrands vanish for $y>\delta$.

**Displacement thickness:**

$$
\delta^*=\delta\int_0^1(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta=\delta\left[\eta-\frac{\eta^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac{\delta}{2}.
$$

**Momentum thickness:**

$$
\theta=\delta\int_0^1\eta(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta=\delta\left[\frac{\eta^2}{2}-\frac{\eta^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac{\delta}{6}.
$$

**Shape factor:** $H=\dfrac{\delta/2}{\delta/6}=3$.

**Skin friction:** $\tau_w=\mu U_e/\delta$, so

$$
C_f=\frac{\mu U_e/\delta}{\tfrac12\rho U_e^2}=\frac{2\nu}{U_e\delta}=\frac{2}{Re_\delta}.
$$

So $\delta^*\propto\delta$, $\theta\propto\delta$, $C_f\propto1/\delta$: the thicker the layer, the gentler the wall gradient and the lower the friction.

**Going further** (using $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$): $\frac{1}{6}\frac{\mathrm d\delta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\nu}{U_e\delta}$, so $\delta\,\mathrm d\delta=\frac{6\nu}{U_e}\mathrm dx$; integrate from $\delta(0)=0$: $\delta^2=12\nu x/U_e$, i.e.

$$
\frac{\delta}{x}=\frac{3.46}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad\frac{\theta}{x}=\frac{0.577}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad C_f=\frac{0.577}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.
$$

Against Blasius (0.664) the crude straight line is only 13% low in $C_f$, because $\theta$ is an integral and averages out the profile error. *(All checked numerically.)* The same calculation is done in [[SESA2022 T2 - Boundary Layers|SESA2022 T2 §4]]; Chapter 3 makes the $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$ step rigorous.

## 8. Worked example: a laminar plate in numbers (suggested)

Air at $U_e=10$ m/s flows along a smooth flat plate of length $L=0.6$ m, with $\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m²/s and $\rho=1.225$ kg/m³. Find the thicknesses at the trailing edge and the drag per unit span on one side. The Blasius constants come from [[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions|3.2]].

The Reynolds number decides whether the layer is laminar:

$$
Re_L=\frac{U_eL}{\nu}=\frac{10\times0.6}{1.5\times10^{-5}}=4.0\times10^5,\qquad\sqrt{Re_L}=632.5.
$$

That is below the usual flat-plate transition value of about $5\times10^5$ (3.4), so the layer is laminar all the way. The thicknesses at $x=L$ follow from the Blasius results:

$$
\delta_{99}=\frac{4.91L}{\sqrt{Re_L}}=\frac{4.91\times0.6}{632.5}=4.66\text{ mm},
$$

$$
\delta^*=\frac{1.721L}{\sqrt{Re_L}}=1.63\text{ mm},\qquad\theta=\frac{0.664L}{\sqrt{Re_L}}=0.630\text{ mm},\qquad H=\frac{1.63}{0.630}=2.59.
$$

So the outer flow at the trailing edge sees a body 1.6 mm thicker than the plate. The local skin friction there is

$$
C_f=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_L}}=1.05\times10^{-3},\qquad\tau_w=C_f\cdot\tfrac12\rho U_e^2=1.05\times10^{-3}\times61.25=0.0643\text{ Pa}.
$$

The drag per unit span on one side comes straight from §4:

$$
D'=\rho U_e^2\theta(L)=1.225\times10^2\times0.630\times10^{-3}=0.0772\text{ N/m}.
$$

> [!check] The same drag from the wall shear
> Integrate the shear instead. For Blasius $\tau_w\propto x^{-1/2}$, so $\int_0^L\tau_w\,\mathrm dx=2L\,\tau_w(L)=2\times0.6\times0.0643=0.0772$ N/m, the same as $\rho U_e^2\theta$, as §5 requires. The mean drag coefficient is $C_D=D'/(\tfrac12\rho U_e^2L)=0.0772/(61.25\times0.6)=2.10\times10^{-3}$, which is $1.328/\sqrt{Re_L}$, i.e. twice the local $C_f$ at the trailing edge. *(All values computed.)*

The outflow at the edge of the layer is $v_e=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx=0.860\,U_e/\sqrt{Re_L}=0.0136$ m/s, about 0.14 % of $U_e$: small, but it is the whole displacement effect.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. Using $U_\infty$ for $U_e$ when the outer flow is not uniform (1.4 notation: $U_e(x)$ is the potential-flow speed at the edge).
2. Writing $\theta=\int(1-u/U_e)^2\mathrm dy$ or $\int(1-u^2/U_e^2)\mathrm dy$: the weighting is $\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})$.
3. Forgetting the mass balance in the control-volume derivation (see the warning in §4).
4. Calling $H<1$ possible: it is not, since $\theta<\delta^*$.
