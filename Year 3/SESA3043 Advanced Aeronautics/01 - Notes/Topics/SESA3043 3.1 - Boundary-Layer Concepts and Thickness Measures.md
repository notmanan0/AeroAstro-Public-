# SESA3043 3.1 - Boundary-Layer Concepts and Thickness Measures

> [!abstract] Summary
> Chapter 3 is about **methods** for solving the boundary-layer (BL) equations of 1.4. Before any method, we need numbers that describe a layer. The edge of a BL is asymptotic, so its thickness is defined by convention: $\delta_{99}$, where $u=0.99U_e$. Two **integral** thicknesses are better defined and more useful:
> - the **displacement thickness** $\delta^*=\int_0^\infty(1-u/U_e)\,\mathrm dy$ is how far the wall would have to move outward, in a uniform flow, to carry the same **mass** flow. Adding it to the body gives the *effective* body seen by the outer potential flow;
> - the **momentum thickness** $\theta=\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})\,\mathrm dy$ is the same idea for **momentum** flow. A control volume shows that the drag of a flat plate is $D=\rho U_e^2\theta$, so $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$.
>
> Their ratio, the **shape factor** $H=\delta^*/\theta$, diagnoses the state of the layer: 2.59 for a laminar flat plate, lower for turbulent and accelerating layers, higher approaching separation.

## The topic in one physical picture

Imagine standing on the leading edge of a plate in a wind, holding a stream of smoke at some height. Downstream, the smoke line drifts **upwards**: the slowed air near the wall takes up more room, so the outer streamlines are pushed away from the surface. The outer flow behaves as if the plate had grown thicker by $\delta^*$. That is the displacement effect.

At the same time, the plate is pulling momentum out of the air. Air that left the leading edge at $U_e$ arrives at the trailing edge slower. The momentum the plate has removed is its drag. If you measure how much momentum flux is missing compared with a uniform stream, expressed as an equivalent thickness, you get $\theta$, and the drag follows directly.

## 1. Prandtl's thin boundary-layer theory, revisited (slides 6–7)

- High $Re$, thin layer: $\delta\ll x$ (1.4: $\delta/L\sim Re^{-1/2}$).
- **Outside and on the edge** of the layer: inviscid, irrotational flow, solved by potential flow and Bernoulli (Ch 1.3, Ch 2).
- **Inside**: viscous flow, governed by the reduced BL equations of 1.4.

The slide sketch traces a flat-plate layer from the leading edge: **laminar**, a **transition** region, then **turbulent**, with a viscous sublayer and buffer zone near the wall. Chapter 3 deals with laminar layers (3.2–3.3), transition (3.4), integral methods valid for both (3.5) and the coupling with the outer flow (3.6). Turbulent layers in depth are SESA6061.

## 2. Boundary-layer thickness δ99 (slides 8–9)

$u\to U_e$ only asymptotically, so the "edge" is a convention:

$$
\boxed{\delta\equiv\delta_{99}:\quad u(\delta_{99})=0.99\,U_e.}
$$

It is easy to picture but awkward to measure (the profile is nearly flat there, so a tiny velocity error moves $\delta_{99}$ a lot), and it is not what the force or the outer flow depends on. The integral thicknesses are better on both counts.

## 3. Displacement thickness (slides 11–17)

### Derivation from the mass-flow defect

Compare the real layer with a hypothetical uniform flow $U_e$ right down to the wall. Per unit span, the **mass-flow defect** (area $A$ in panel b) is

$$
A=\int_0^\infty\rho\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

Ask: by what distance $\delta^*$ must the wall be moved into the uniform stream to lose the same mass flow? A slab of height $\delta^*$ carries $\rho U_e\delta^*$:

$$
\rho U_e\delta^*=\int_0^\infty\rho\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

Divide by $\rho U_e$ (incompressible):

$$
\boxed{\delta^*=\int_0^\infty\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.}
$$

### Physical meaning: the effective body (slide 17)

- Adding $\delta^*$ to the surface gives the **apparent (effective) body** seen by the outer flow.
- So the potential-flow methods of Chapter 2 can be applied to the **displaced** surface, and the viscous solution used inside it. This is the basis of viscous–inviscid interaction ([[SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction|3.6]]).
- On a flat plate, the growth of $\delta^*$ is exactly what pushes the outer streamlines away: the edge outflow is $v_e=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx$ (1.4 Blasius check: $v_e=0.860\,U_eRe_x^{-1/2}$).

> [!check] Blasius numbers
> For the laminar flat plate, $\delta^*=1.721\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}$ and $\delta_{99}=4.91\,x/\sqrt{Re_x}$, so $\delta^*\approx0.35\,\delta_{99}$ (panel b, computed). The wall would need to move out about a third of the layer thickness.

## 4. Momentum thickness θ: the control-volume derivation (slides 19–29)

This is the derivation the slides build up over eleven animation frames. **Assumptions**: flat plate; zero pressure gradient ($U_e=U_\infty$, and $\partial p/\partial y=0$, so the pressure is uniform on the whole control volume and contributes no net force); incompressible; steady.

**Control volume** (panel d): inlet 1 upstream of the plate, uniform $U_e$ over height $h_1$; outlet 2 at the trailing edge, profile $u(y)$ over height $h_2$; the top **follows a streamline**, so no mass crosses it; the bottom is the plate, which exerts the drag $D$ (per unit span) on the fluid in the $-x$ direction.

**Step 1: mass.**

$$
\dot m_1=\dot m_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho U_eh_1=\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy.
$$

Multiply both sides by $U_e$ (this will be needed to eliminate $h_1$):

$$
\rho U_e^2h_1=U_e\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy.\qquad(1)
$$

**Step 2: $x$-momentum.** The net momentum outflow equals the force on the fluid, which is the plate's $-D$:

$$
\dot M_2-\dot M_1=-D
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy-\rho U_e^2h_1=-D.
$$

Rearrange:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2h_1-\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy.
$$

**Step 3: eliminate $h_1$ with (1).**

$$
D=U_e\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,\mathrm dy-\int_0^{h_2}\rho u^2\,\mathrm dy=\int_0^{h_2}\rho u\,(U_e-u)\,\mathrm dy.
$$

**Step 4: non-dimensionalise.** Take $\rho U_e^2$ outside:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2\int_0^{h_2}\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.
$$

**Step 5: let $h_2\to\infty$.** Above the layer $u=U_e$, so the integrand is zero there and extending the limit changes nothing:

$$
D=\rho U_e^2\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy\equiv\rho U_e^2\,\theta,
\qquad
\boxed{\theta=\int_0^\infty\frac{u}{U_e}\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy.}
$$

> [!tip] Interpretation
> $\rho u(U_e-u)$ is the momentum **deficit** carried by the fluid that actually passes through: mass flux $\rho u$, each unit of which has lost speed $U_e-u$. $\theta$ is the thickness of a uniform stream whose momentum flux equals that deficit. Momentum lost by the fluid is momentum given to the plate: drag.

> [!warning] Why $u(U_e-u)$ and not $U_e(U_e-u)$
> Using $U_e(U_e-u)$ would count the defect of fluid that never entered the control volume. Mass conservation (Step 1) is what turns the $h_1$ term into $\int\rho uU_e$ and makes the weighting $u$, not $U_e$. A common exam slip is to forget Step 1 and write $D=\rho\int(U_e^2-u^2)\,\mathrm dy$, which over-counts.

## 5. Wall shear, skin friction and the drag link (slide 32)

$$
\tau_w=\mu\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0},
\qquad
\boxed{C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U_e^2}.}
$$

Link to $\theta$ (revision Q3): the drag on a plate of length $x$ is the integral of the local wall shear, $D(x)=\int_0^x\tau_w\,\mathrm dx'$. Differentiate both expressions for $D$ with respect to $x$ ($U_e$ constant):

$$
\frac{\mathrm dD}{\mathrm dx}=\tau_w=\rho U_e^2\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_e^2}=\frac{C_f}{2}.}
$$

This is the flat-plate form of the momentum integral equation of [[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]. It says the layer thickens (in momentum terms) exactly as fast as the wall drags on it.

## 6. Shape factor H (slide 31)

$$
\boxed{H=\frac{\delta^*}{\theta}}
$$

Because $u/U_e\le1$, $\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})\le1-\frac{u}{U_e}$, so $\theta<\delta^*$ and $H>1$ always.

| Layer | $H$ | Source |
|---|---|---|
| laminar flat plate (Blasius) | 2.59 | [[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions\|3.2]] |
| laminar stagnation point ($\beta=1$) | 2.22 | 3.2 |
| laminar, at separation (Falkner–Skan) | 4.03 | 3.2 |
| turbulent flat plate (1/7 power law) | 1.29 | [[SESA2022 T2 - Boundary Layers\|SESA2022]] |

A **fuller** profile (more momentum near the wall: turbulent, or accelerating) has a lower $H$; an emptier one (decelerating, near separation) has a higher $H$. That is why $H$ is used to "indicate the state of the boundary layer" (slide 31) and as a closure in integral methods (3.5–3.6).

## 7. Worked example: the linear profile (revision 3.1 Q4)

Profile: $u/U_e=y/\delta$ for $y\le\delta$, and $1$ above. Let $\eta=y/\delta$, $\mathrm dy=\delta\,\mathrm d\eta$. The integrands vanish for $y>\delta$.

**Displacement thickness:**

$$
\delta^*=\delta\int_0^1(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta=\delta\left[\eta-\frac{\eta^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac{\delta}{2}.
$$

**Momentum thickness:**

$$
\theta=\delta\int_0^1\eta(1-\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta=\delta\left[\frac{\eta^2}{2}-\frac{\eta^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac{\delta}{6}.
$$

**Shape factor:** $H=\dfrac{\delta/2}{\delta/6}=3$.

**Skin friction:** $\tau_w=\mu U_e/\delta$, so

$$
C_f=\frac{\mu U_e/\delta}{\tfrac12\rho U_e^2}=\frac{2\nu}{U_e\delta}=\frac{2}{Re_\delta}.
$$

So $\delta^*\propto\delta$, $\theta\propto\delta$, $C_f\propto1/\delta$: the thicker the layer, the gentler the wall gradient and the lower the friction.

**Going further** (using $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$): $\frac{1}{6}\frac{\mathrm d\delta}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\nu}{U_e\delta}$, so $\delta\,\mathrm d\delta=\frac{6\nu}{U_e}\mathrm dx$; integrate from $\delta(0)=0$: $\delta^2=12\nu x/U_e$, i.e.

$$
\frac{\delta}{x}=\frac{3.46}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad\frac{\theta}{x}=\frac{0.577}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad C_f=\frac{0.577}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.
$$

Against Blasius (0.664) the crude straight line is only 13% low in $C_f$, because $\theta$ is an integral and averages out the profile error. *(All checked numerically.)* The same calculation is done in [[SESA2022 T2 - Boundary Layers|SESA2022 T2 §4]]; Chapter 3 now makes the $\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dx=C_f/2$ step rigorous.

## 8. Common mistakes

1. Using $U_\infty$ for $U_e$ when the outer flow is not uniform (1.4 notation: $U_e(x)$ is the potential-flow speed at the edge).
2. Writing $\theta=\int(1-u/U_e)^2\mathrm dy$ or $\int(1-u^2/U_e^2)\mathrm dy$: the weighting is $\frac{u}{U_e}(1-\frac{u}{U_e})$.
3. Forgetting the mass balance in the control-volume derivation (see the warning in §4).
4. Calling $H<1$ possible: it is not, since $\theta<\delta^*$.
