# SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction

> [!abstract] Summary
> A panel method gives the inviscid flow, $U_e(x)$ and $C_L$, but no drag; a boundary-layer method gives $\delta^*(x)$ and the drag but needs $U_e(x)$. Viscous–inviscid interaction iterates between them until $\delta^*$ stops changing, feeding the layer back either by moving the panels out by $\delta^*$ (surface displacement) or by blowing through the original panels at $v_s=\mathrm d(U_e\delta^*)/\mathrm dx$ (surface transpiration, which XFOIL uses). The loop is reliable while the layers stay attached and degrades once they separate, which the coursework's post-stall runs in XFOIL or XFLR5 show.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** the boundary layer's displacement changes the outer flow, as in XFOIL or a wind-tunnel blockage estimate.
> - **Assumptions:** thin, attached layers; the iteration converges; the boundary-layer closure is approximate.
> - **Method:** 1. Inviscid solution → $U_e(x)$. 2. Boundary-layer method → $\delta^*(x)$. 3. SDM (move the wall out by $\delta^*$) or STM ($v_s=\mathrm d(U_e\delta^*)/\mathrm dx$). 4. Re-solve the inviscid flow. 5. Repeat to convergence, and state the closure used.
> - **Common mistakes:** $v_s=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx$ when $U_e$ varies; presenting a converged loop as the exact physical answer.
> - **Practise:** [[#5. Worked example: revision 3.6 Q3, a wind-tunnel test section]] · [[SESA3043 Ch3 Revision Questions - Worked Solutions#3.6 Viscous–inviscid interaction|Ch3 revision 3.6]]

---

The outer flow does not see the real aerofoil. It sees the aerofoil plus its boundary layer, thickened by $\delta^*$ everywhere, plus a wake trailing off the trailing edge. That "effective body" is slightly fatter and slightly less cambered at the rear (the upper-surface layer is thicker), so the real aerofoil makes a little less lift than potential theory says, and its pressure no longer fully recovers at the trailing edge, which is pressure (form) drag.

But the layer's thickness depends on the pressure distribution, and the pressure distribution depends on the effective body. Neither can be found first, so the method guesses (no layer), solves, corrects, and repeats until the two agree. That loop is VII.

![[aa_vii_methods.png|900]]

## 1. Why VII is needed

Falkner–Skan, the MIE and Thwaites all give integral quantities, and adding $\delta^*$ to the surface gives the effective body. That works analytically or with tables for simple bodies such as the wedge of 3.2, but not for arbitrary bodies. A panel method gives the inviscid solution for arbitrary bodies: for each panel it gives the tangential velocity $u_i$, which is $U_e(x)$ at that panel. Being inviscid, it has $U_e$ uniform in $y$, and only $C_L$ can be obtained from it.

In reality a layer forms on the body, and a viscous solution applied inside the layer gives $C_D$. So the panel method supplies $C_L$ and $U_e(x)$, the viscous solution supplies $\delta^*(x)$ and $C_D$, and each needs the other's output. The boundary between the viscous and inviscid regions is not known in advance; VII finds it iteratively.

## 2. The VII algorithm

1. Input: panels, $U_\infty$, $\alpha$.
2. Inviscid solution (panel method): $U_e(x)$ on every panel, $C_L$.
3. Viscous solution (MIE with a closure, e.g. Thwaites; transition prediction; turbulent closure): $\theta(x)$, $\delta^*(x)$, $C_f(x)$, hence $C_D$.
4. Convergence check: has $\delta^*$ (or $v_s$) changed by less than $\varepsilon$ since the last iteration?
5. If not: recalculate, repanel (SDM) or update the blowing (STM), and return to 2.
6. Output: final $C_L$, $C_D$.

## 3. Surface displacement method

The viscous solution gives $\delta^*(x)=\int_0^\infty(1-u/U_e)\,\mathrm dy$ at each panel. The iteration has converged when $\left|\delta^{*(n+1)}-\delta^{*(n)}\right|<\varepsilon$; if it has not, the body is repanelled, shifting each panel out by $\delta^{*(n)}$ for iteration $n+1$.

The drawback is that the panel method must be applied to a new body at every iteration, with a new geometry and a new influence matrix. That can be numerically impractical, especially near the trailing edge and in the wake.

## 4. Surface transpiration method

### The idea

The displaced panels of SDM must be streamlines of the outer flow, because the panel method's boundary condition is no flow through a panel. STM instead keeps the original panels and imposes an imaginary blowing/suction velocity $v_s(x)$ through each one, chosen so that the outer streamlines take the same shape as the SDM panels. The blowing is not physical, but its effect on the outer flow is the same as repanelling by $\delta^*$: the effective body is reproduced.

### Deriving vₛ

A "tunnel" control volume between two stations $x$ and $x+\Delta x$, from the original wall up to the displacement surface (red dashed box in panel b), carries incompressible flow.

Mass enters through the left face, as the equivalent inviscid flow through a gap of height $\delta^*(x)$, i.e. $U_e(x)\,\delta^*(x)$ per unit span, and through the bottom (the original wall) as the blowing, $v_s(x)\,\Delta x$. It leaves through the right face, $U_e(x+\Delta x)\,\delta^*(x+\Delta x)$; none crosses the top, which is the displacement streamline. The balance $\sum\dot m_{in}=\sum\dot m_{out}$ is

$$
U_e(x)\delta^*(x)+v_s(x)\Delta x=U_e(x+\Delta x)\delta^*(x+\Delta x).
$$

Rearrange:

$$
v_s(x)=\frac{U_e(x+\Delta x)\delta^*(x+\Delta x)-U_e(x)\delta^*(x)}{\Delta x}.
$$

Let $\Delta x\to0$:

$$
\boxed{v_s(x)=\frac{\mathrm d\left(U_e\delta^*\right)}{\mathrm dx}.}
$$

Physically, the layer's mass-flow deficit is $\rho U_e\delta^*$, and where it grows the layer is pushing more fluid outwards; the blowing supplies exactly that outward flux. On a flat plate ($U_e$ constant), $v_s=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx$, which is the Blasius edge outflow $v_e=0.860\,U_e/\sqrt{Re_x}$ of 1.4, so the blowing reproduces the real displacement effect.

### The STM loop

$v_s$ is easy to specify as a (Dirichlet) normal-velocity boundary condition in the panel code: the same influence matrix, a new right-hand side. The loop has converged when $\left|v_s^{(n+1)}-v_s^{(n)}\right|<\varepsilon$; if not, $v_s$ is updated and the inviscid flow re-solved.

### XFOIL

XFOIL uses VII with STM rather than SDM, so it never repanels. Its viscous solution is an integral method of the same family as the momentum integral equation and Thwaites' method of [[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]: it solves the MIE together with a second integral equation, for the kinetic-energy shape parameter, in a compressible form with the Mach number in it, which is why it differs from the incompressible MIE of 3.5. The compressible form comes from the transonic code ISES, for which the method was written. XFOIL's own panel solution carries only a Kármán–Tsien compressibility correction, so its results are reliable while the flow stays subsonic everywhere and degrade once a supersonic region appears. The MIE has three unknowns and needs closures, and XFOIL uses different ones on either side of transition: Falkner–Skan profiles for the laminar layer, and a separate turbulent closure. Transition between them is predicted by the $e^n$ method of [[SESA3043 3.4 - Transition to Turbulence|3.4]], with $n_{crit}$ ("Ncrit", default 9) as the user setting. The method is described in Drela and Giles (1987), listed under Sources.

### Separation and stall

Every closure in this chapter stops at separation. The Falkner–Skan family ends at $\beta=-0.1988$, where $f''(0)=0$ ([[SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions|3.2]]), and the Pohlhausen profile at $\lambda=-12$ ([[SESA3043 3.3 - Pohlhausen Method and Pressure Gradients|3.3]]). Beyond those points the profiles have reverse flow near the wall, and the thin-layer assumptions behind the boundary-layer equations, and so behind the MIE, no longer hold. When $U_e(x)$ is prescribed by the inviscid solution and the layer is marched downstream, the integral equations also become singular at the separation point itself (the Goldstein singularity), so the march cannot continue.

VII inherits both limits. It is accurate for attached layers, it copes with small regions of separation such as a laminar separation bubble or mild trailing-edge separation, and it becomes unreliable once a large part of the upper surface has separated. Near and beyond $C_{L,\max}$ an XFOIL polar should be read as a trend rather than a prediction: the iteration may fail to converge, and a converged $C_L$ or $C_D$ after stall is less trustworthy than the attached-flow values. The coursework's sweep from $\alpha=-5^\circ$ to post-stall exposes this.

### SDM against STM

| | SDM | STM |
|---|---|---|
| what changes each iteration | the geometry (panels moved by $\delta^*$) | a boundary condition ($v_s$ on each panel) |
| influence matrix | rebuilt every iteration | built once |
| robustness | awkward near the trailing edge and with thick layers or separation | handles the wake and thick layers more easily |
| physical? | yes, the effective body | no, imaginary blowing, but equivalent outer flow |
| used in | conceptual and simple codes | XFOIL, most practical codes |

## 5. Worked example: revision 3.6 Q3, a wind-tunnel test section

> A test section 2 m long, $15\times15$ cm square. $U_0=15$ m/s at the start, where turbulent layers on the walls begin. $\theta/x=0.036\,Re_x^{-1/5}$, $H=9/7$, $\nu=1.46\times10^{-5}$ m²/s. Perform two VII iterations to estimate the centre-line velocity at the exit.

The correlation $\theta/x=0.036Re_x^{-1/5}$ is the 1/7-power-law turbulent result from the MIE, and $H=9/7$ is the 1/7 power law's shape factor ([[SESA2022 T2 - Boundary Layers|SESA2022 T2 §5]]).

The core is inviscid. The four wall layers each displace the core flow inward by $\delta^*$, so the effective area at the exit is $(a-2\delta^*)^2$, $a=0.15$ m (corners ignored). Mass conservation for the core:

$$
U_0a^2=U_e(a-2\delta^*)^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad U_e=U_0\frac{a^2}{(a-2\delta^*)^2}.
$$

The core speed depends on $\delta^*$; $\delta^*$ depends on $Re_x=U_ex/\nu$, i.e. on the core speed. Hence the iteration.

Iteration 1 starts from the inviscid guess, $U_e=15$ m/s:

$$
Re_L=\frac{15\times2}{1.46\times10^{-5}}=2.055\times10^6,
\qquad
Re_L^{-1/5}=0.05463,
$$

$$
\theta=0.036\times2\times0.05463=3.934\ \text{mm},
\qquad
\delta^*=\frac97\theta=5.057\ \text{mm},
$$

$$
U_e=15\times\frac{0.15^2}{(0.15-0.010115)^2}=15\times\frac{0.0225}{0.019568}=\boxed{17.25\ \text{m/s}}.
$$

Iteration 2 uses $U_e=17.248$ m/s:

$$
Re_L=2.363\times10^6,\qquad\theta=3.825\ \text{mm},\qquad\delta^*=4.918\ \text{mm},
$$

$$
U_e=15\times\frac{0.0225}{(0.15-0.009836)^2}=\boxed{17.18\ \text{m/s}}.
$$

> [!check] Sense of scale
> The wall layers block $1-(0.14016/0.15)^2=12.7\%$ of the exit area, accelerating the core by 14.5%. Real tunnels have slightly diverging walls to compensate, keeping the test-section speed (and static pressure) constant along its length.

Iterations 3–5 converge to 17.181 m/s. Two iterations are already within 0.002 m/s: $\delta^*\propto Re^{-1/5}$ depends only weakly on $U_e$, so the loop converges fast. *(All computed.)*

> [!warning] Converged is not the same as exact
> This answers the question as set (two VII iterations with the supplied correlation), but the closure is approximate. The correlation $\theta/x=0.036Re_x^{-1/5}$ is a zero-pressure-gradient flat-plate result, evaluated here with the exit velocity as if the layer had grown at that speed all along. In the tunnel the core accelerates from 15 to about 17.2 m/s, so the layer actually grows in a favourable pressure gradient and carries the history of the lower upstream speeds. A consistent calculation would march the momentum integral equation ([[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]]) along $x$ with the local $U_e(x)$ and $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$. Quote 17.2 m/s as the result of this approximate closure: the loop converging only shows that the closure is self-consistent, not that the physics is.

## 6. Coursework: thickness and order of accuracy

The coursework (due Thu 3 Dec 2026, 4 pm; brief and weights in the [[SESA3043 Advanced Aeronautics Hub#Assessment and deadlines|hub]]) uses the Blackboard aerofoil generator and doublet panel code of [[SESA3043 2.3 - Panel Methods#10. The Blackboard coursework code (dpan and ktreff)|2.3 §10]], then XFOIL or XFLR5. Two of its calculations need a method rather than just a code run. Both are shown here on the 2.3 validation aerofoil ($\varepsilon=0.1$, $\beta=0.05$, $\tau=0.1$, $\alpha=4^\circ$), not on a coursework aerofoil.

### Thickness normal to the camber line

The generator returns points round the surface. Pairing an upper and a lower point at the same $x$ gives a camber point midway between them and a vertical thickness, but the vertical line is not normal to the camber line, so the thickness should be measured along the camber-line normal instead. At each camber point, construct the normal, intersect it with the upper and lower surfaces, and take the distance between the two intersections; the maximum of that distance is the maximum thickness. Maximum camber and its position are measured from the chord line.

> [!check] How much the normal changes the answer (Python, 400 panels)
> Maximum camber $0.02467c$ at $x/c=0.509$. Vertical thickness $0.13674c$ at $x/c=0.288$; normal thickness $0.13661c$ at $x/c=0.285$. With 2.5 % camber the camber-line slope is small, so the two differ by only $0.013\,\%c$. The difference grows with camber and with the camber-line slope at the thickest point, so state which definition was used, and the error against the closest NACA section.

### Order of accuracy

Once $N$ is large enough, the error in $C_L$ is assumed to fall as a power of the panel number $N$:

$$
\varepsilon(N)=\frac{\lvert C_{L,\text{panel}}(N)-C_{L,\text{exact}}\rvert}{C_{L,\text{exact}}}\approx K\,N^{-n},
$$

where $C_{L,\text{exact}}$ is the conformal-mapping value and $K$ a constant. Take the logarithm of both sides:

$$
\log\varepsilon=\log K+\log N^{-n}.
$$

Bring the power down:

$$
\log\varepsilon=\log K-n\log N.
$$

So $\log\varepsilon$ against $\log N$ is a straight line of slope $-n$, and a linear fit to the points gives the order of accuracy $n$. From two runs alone, $n=\ln(\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2)/\ln(N_2/N_1)$.

![[aa_panel_order_of_accuracy.png|900]]

| $N$ | 25 | 50 | 100 | 200 | 400 | 800 | 1600 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $C_{L,\text{panel}}$ | 1.11236 | 0.93478 | 0.86103 | 0.83830 | 0.83362 | 0.83304 | 0.83301 |
| $\varepsilon$ (signed) | $+3.4\times10^{-1}$ | $+1.2\times10^{-1}$ | $+3.4\times10^{-2}$ | $+6.3\times10^{-3}$ | $+7.1\times10^{-4}$ | $+2.3\times10^{-5}$ | $-1.0\times10^{-5}$ |
| local $n$ (from the previous column) | | 1.46 | 1.86 | 2.41 | 3.15 | 4.94 | 1.15 |

The exact value is $C_L=0.833023$. The fitted order depends on the range of $N$: 1.90 over $N=25$–200, 2.78 over 100–400 and 3.27 over 200–1600. The local order rises with $N$ because the coarse runs are not yet in the asymptotic range, and at $N=1600$ the error has changed sign, so $\lvert\varepsilon\rvert$ passed through zero between 800 and 1600 and the last local value means nothing. The fit should be restricted to the range where the points lie on one straight line, and the report should state that range. About 100 panels is usually enough for decent accuracy, but this thin trailing edge ($\tau=0.1$ rad, about $5.7^\circ$) still has a 3.4 % error at $N=100$ and needs 200–400 panels; the coursework aerofoil's own convergence decides. Errors of order $10^{-5}$ and their logarithms need double precision, which MATLAB and NumPy use by default.

## 7. Summary of governing equations

The Chapter 3 table ends with VII: potential flow plus boundary layer, coupled. It sits on top of the similarity solution and the MIE (the viscous half) and potential flow (the inviscid half).

The key formula is the blowing/suction velocity in STM, $v_s=\mathrm d(U_e\delta^*)/\mathrm dx$.

## 8. Common mistakes

1. Using $v_s=U_e\,\mathrm d\delta^*/\mathrm dx$ when $U_e$ varies: the product must be differentiated.
2. In the tunnel problem, displacing by $\delta^*$ on only one wall, or by $\theta$ instead of $\delta^*$.
3. Using $U_0$ instead of the updated $U_e$ in $Re_x$ for the second iteration.
4. Claiming STM is physical blowing.
5. Quoting post-stall XFOIL values with the same confidence as attached-flow ones.
6. Fitting the order of accuracy over every panel count, including points outside the straight-line range or past a sign change in the error.
