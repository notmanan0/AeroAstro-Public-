# SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight

> [!abstract] Summary
> Actuator disc theory for a rotor climbing at $V_C$ or descending at $V_D$ along its axis gives the same wake result as hover, $v_2=2v_i$, and a quadratic for the induced velocity. Normalised by the hover value $v_0=\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$ it reads $\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i-1=0$ in climb and $\bar v_i^2-\bar V_D\bar v_i+1=0$ in descent; one root of each is physical, and the descent equation has real roots only for $\bar V_D\geq2$. Between hover and $\bar V_D=2$ no slipstream exists: the rotor passes through the vortex ring and turbulent wake states, and autorotation (zero power) sits there, at $\bar V_D\approx1.7$–$1.8$. The power is $P=T(V_C+v_i)$ in climb and $P=T(-V_D+v_i)$ in descent.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a rotor of known thrust, disc area and density climbs or descends vertically and the induced velocity, wake velocity or power is wanted, or the flow state has to be named.
> - **Assumptions:** the hover actuator-disc assumptions (steady, incompressible, uniform $v_i$ over the disc, no swirl, one streamtube with $p_\infty$ at both ends); axial flight only; $v_i$ positive downward through the disc; profile power left out.
> - **Method:** 1. $v_0=\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$ with $T=W$. 2. $\bar V_C=V_C/v_0$ or $\bar V_D=V_D/v_0$. 3. Climb: $\bar v_i=-\bar V_C/2+\sqrt{\bar V_C^2/4+1}$; descent with $\bar V_D\geq2$: $\bar v_i=\bar V_D/2-\sqrt{\bar V_D^2/4-1}$; $0<\bar V_D<2$: no momentum solution, name the state. 4. $P=T(V_C+v_i)$ or $T(-V_D+v_i)$. 5. Check the far-wake velocity has the same sign as the far-upstream one.
> - **Common mistakes:** taking the larger descent root (it reverses the wake); using the momentum equations between hover and $\bar V_D=2$, where they have no meaning.
> - **Practise:** [[#Example 1: Lynx in a 5 m/s vertical climb]] · [[#The roots, when they exist and which is physical]] (which descent root is physical, and why)

A hovering rotor pulls air down through its disc and accelerates it to twice the disc velocity in the far wake ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]). When the rotor climbs vertically, the air approaching from above already has a velocity $V_C$ relative to the disc, so the same thrust needs less extra velocity, and the induced velocity falls. The rotor is then doing work in two ways: lifting the aircraft ($TV_C$) and accelerating the air ($Tv_i$).

Descent reverses the free stream. Relative to the rotor, air now approaches from below at $V_D$, while the rotor still pushes it downward to make thrust. At high descent rates the rotor slows the upward stream, like a windmill or a brake, and extracts energy from it; the slipstream is then well defined again and momentum theory works. At low descent rates the rotor's own downwash and the upward free stream are of similar size and oppose each other. Air goes down through the disc, back up outside it and down again: the tip vortices pile up into a ring around the disc. There is no single streamtube with a definite direction, and actuator disc theory does not apply there.

![[aa_4_2_climb_descent_control_volumes.png|900]]

Hover is the manoeuvre that sets rotorcraft apart, but a rotorcraft also has to go straight up and down. Axial flight reuses the hover model unchanged in its essentials, a disc of area $A$ carrying thrust $T$ and a streamtube through its edge, and the whole analysis rests on drawing that streamtube correctly for each case, with the velocity and pressure at every station. Once the picture is right, Bernoulli on each side of the disc and the momentum balance over the streamtube give everything else.

## 1. Notation and sign conventions

The quantities are those of [[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]: disc area $A=\pi R^2$, wake area $A_2$, induced velocity at the disc $v_i$, far-wake induced velocity $v_2$, ambient pressure $p_\infty$, pressures just above and just below the disc $p_u$ and $p_l$, constant density $\rho$. Two new speeds are added:

- the climb velocity $V_C$, the speed at which the rotor rises, so that relative to the rotor the free stream comes **down** at $V_C$;
- the descent velocity $V_D$, the speed at which the rotor sinks, so that relative to the rotor the free stream comes **up** at $V_D$.

Both are relative velocities: the frame is fixed to the rotor, so a rotor climbing at $V_C$ sees the air far above coming down at it at $V_C$. In a steady climb or descent the whole streamtube moves with the rotor, and that is why the hover analysis carries over.

$V_C$ and $V_D$ are both written positive, and $v_i$ and $v_2$ are always measured positive **downward** through the disc, in the direction the rotor pushes the air. In climb all three velocities add; in descent the induced velocities subtract from the upward stream. When one curve covers both cases, descent is written as a negative climb velocity, $V_C=-V_D$.

The hover induced velocity of the same rotor at the same thrust is

$$
v_0=\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}},
$$

which is the hover result of [[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]. Every velocity in axial flight is normalised by it:

$$
\bar v_i=\frac{v_i}{v_0},\qquad \bar V_C=\frac{V_C}{v_0},\qquad \bar V_D=\frac{V_D}{v_0}.
$$

The tip-speed ratios of 4.1 are also used: $\lambda_i=v_i/V_T$, and in axial flight $\mu_z=V_C/V_T$, with $V_T=\Omega R$.

## 2. Climb

### Thrust from the pressure jump

The rotor still produces an upward thrust $T$. In a steady climb it balances the weight; a larger thrust accelerates the aircraft upwards, and the steady analysis below is then a quasi-steady approximation. The control volume is the streamtube through the disc edge (left panel above), from far upstream (above the disc, velocity $V_C$, pressure $p_\infty$) to the far wake (below, velocity $V_C+v_2$, area $A_2$, pressure $p_\infty$). The flow is steady, incompressible and loss-free on each side of the disc, so Bernoulli holds along a streamline above the disc and separately below it, but not across it, where the rotor adds energy.

The thrust is the pressure jump times the disc area:

$$
T=A(p_l-p_u).
$$

Bernoulli from far upstream to just above the disc:

$$
p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho V_C^2=p_u+\tfrac12\rho(V_C+v_i)^2.
$$

Bernoulli from just below the disc to the far wake:

$$
p_l+\tfrac12\rho(V_C+v_i)^2=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho(V_C+v_2)^2.
$$

The velocity is continuous through the disc (assumption 4 of 4.1), so the $(V_C+v_i)^2$ terms are equal. Subtract the first equation from the second:

$$
p_l-p_u=\tfrac12\rho(V_C+v_2)^2-\tfrac12\rho V_C^2.
$$

Expand the square:

$$
p_l-p_u=\tfrac12\rho\left(V_C^2+2V_Cv_2+v_2^2-V_C^2\right)=\rho\left(v_2V_C+\tfrac12v_2^2\right).
$$

Multiply by $A$:

$$
T=\rho A\left(v_2V_C+\tfrac12v_2^2\right).
$$

At $V_C=0$ this is the hover result $T=\tfrac12\rho Av_2^2$.

### Thrust from the momentum balance

The mass flow through the disc is

$$
\dot m=\rho A(V_C+v_i).
$$

The pressure on the whole control-volume boundary is $p_\infty$ (far up, far down and, by assumption, along the sides), so it exerts no net force, and the only axial force on the air is the rotor's. The air enters with downward momentum flux $\dot mV_C$ and leaves with $\dot m(V_C+v_2)$. The rotor pushes the air down with force equal to the increase in momentum flux, and the air pushes the rotor up with the equal and opposite thrust:

$$
T=\dot m(V_C+v_2)-\dot mV_C=\dot mv_2=\rho A(V_C+v_i)v_2.
$$

### The wake velocity

Equate the two thrust expressions:

$$
\rho A(V_C+v_i)v_2=\rho A\left(v_2V_C+\tfrac12v_2^2\right).
$$

Divide by $\rho Av_2$ (non-zero for a rotor making thrust):

$$
V_C+v_i=V_C+\tfrac12v_2.
$$

Cancel $V_C$:

$$
\boxed{v_2=2v_i,}
$$

the same as hover: half the velocity increment is added above the disc and half below, whatever the climb rate.

### The induced-velocity equation

Put $v_2=2v_i$ into the momentum thrust:

$$
T=\rho A(V_C+v_i)\,2v_i=2\rho A\left(v_i^2+V_Cv_i\right).
$$

Divide by $2\rho A$ and move everything to one side:

$$
v_i^2+V_Cv_i-\frac{T}{2\rho A}=0.
$$

Replace $T/2\rho A$ by $v_0^2$:

$$
v_i^2+V_Cv_i-v_0^2=0.
$$

Divide by $v_0^2$:

$$
\boxed{\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i-1=0.}
$$

### Solving it, and which root is physical

The quadratic formula with coefficients $1$, $\bar V_C$, $-1$:

$$
\bar v_i=\frac{-\bar V_C\pm\sqrt{\bar V_C^2+4}}{2}.
$$

Take the 2 inside the root:

$$
\bar v_i=-\frac{\bar V_C}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_C^2}{4}+1}.
$$

The product of the two roots is $-1$, so for every $\bar V_C$ one root is positive and one negative. The rotor makes its upward thrust by pushing the air down, so the induced velocity must point down through the disc, $v_i>0$, and the negative root is rejected. It also fails a second test, since it is not just a negative induced velocity: it is smaller than $-\bar V_C$, since $\sqrt{\bar V_C^2/4+1}>\bar V_C/2$, so $V_C+v_i<0$ and the mass flow $\rho A(V_C+v_i)$ would be upward through a disc pushing air down. Then $T=\dot mv_2<0$, which contradicts the positive thrust the equation was built for. The physical root is the positive one:

$$
\boxed{\bar v_i=-\frac{\bar V_C}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_C^2}{4}+1},\qquad \bar V_C\geq0.}
$$

Two limits check it. At $\bar V_C=0$ it gives $\bar v_i=1$, i.e. $v_i=v_0$, hover. As the climb velocity rises from zero, $\bar v_i$ falls steadily below 1. For a fast climb, factor out $\bar V_C/2$:

$$
\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_C^2}{4}+1}=\frac{\bar V_C}{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{4}{\bar V_C^2}}\approx\frac{\bar V_C}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{\bar V_C^2}\right)=\frac{\bar V_C}{2}+\frac{1}{\bar V_C},
$$

so $\bar v_i\approx1/\bar V_C$: the faster the climb, the more mass flows through the disc each second and the smaller the velocity increment needed for the same thrust. At $\bar V_C=4$ the exact root is $0.2361$ against $1/\bar V_C=0.25$.

> [!check] Values of the climb root (computed)
> $\bar V_C=0,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 2,\ 4$ give $\bar v_i=1,\ 0.7808,\ 0.6180,\ 0.4142,\ 0.2361$, and each satisfies $\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i-1=0$ to round-off.

## 3. Power in climb

The rotor's thrust acts on air that passes through the disc at $V_C+v_i$, so the power is

$$
P=T(V_C+v_i).
$$

An energy balance on the same control volume confirms it. The kinetic-energy flux rises from $\tfrac12\dot mV_C^2$ to $\tfrac12\dot m(V_C+v_2)^2$:

$$
\Delta\dot E=\tfrac12\dot m\left[(V_C+v_2)^2-V_C^2\right]=\dot mv_2\left(V_C+\tfrac12v_2\right).
$$

With $\dot mv_2=T$ and $\tfrac12v_2=v_i$:

$$
\Delta\dot E=T(V_C+v_i)=P.
$$

The power splits into a climb power and an induced power:

$$
P=\underbrace{TV_C}_{P_{climb}}+\underbrace{Tv_i}_{P_i}.
$$

The climb power is the rate of increase of potential energy, $WV_C$ with $T=W$. The induced power is the energy left behind in the wake; it falls as the climb rate rises, because $v_i$ falls.

Normalising by the hover induced power $Tv_0$:

$$
\frac{P}{Tv_0}=\bar V_C+\bar v_i.
$$

For a slow climb, expand the root for small $\bar V_C$, using $\sqrt{1+x}\approx1+x/2$:

$$
\bar v_i\approx-\frac{\bar V_C}{2}+1+\frac{\bar V_C^2}{8},
$$

$$
\frac{P}{Tv_0}\approx1+\frac{\bar V_C}{2}+\frac{\bar V_C^2}{8}.
$$

So a slow climb costs only about half the climb power $TV_C$ on top of hover: the other half is paid for by the drop in induced power. In a fast climb $\bar v_i\to0$ and nearly all the power goes into climbing.

![[aa_4_2_power_axial.png|900]]

In coefficient form, divide $P=T(V_C+v_i)$ by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A$ and use $C_T=T/(\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A)$:

$$
C_P=\frac{T(V_C+v_i)}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A}=C_T\,\frac{V_C+v_i}{V_T},
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{C_P}{C_T}=\mu_z+\lambda_i,\qquad \mu_z=\frac{V_C}{V_T},\ \lambda_i=\frac{v_i}{V_T}.}
$$

Here $\mu_z$ is the non-dimensional climb velocity. It is normalised by the tip velocity $V_T$, like $\lambda_i$, whereas $\bar V_C$ and $\bar v_i$ are normalised by the hover induced velocity $v_0$; the two sets of ratios differ by the factor $v_0/V_T=\lambda_{i,\text{hover}}$.

The rotor efficiency, thrust per unit power, is $T/P=1/(V_C+v_i)$: in hover it is $1/v_i$ ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]), and in climb the whole inflow $V_C+v_i$ sets it.

## 4. Descent at high rate: the windmill brake slipstream

### Control volume and directions

In a descent the relative flow comes up from below at $V_D$ (right panel of the control-volume figure), so the inlet of the streamtube is now at the bottom. Even in a steady descent the rotor still has to carry the weight, so the thrust still points upwards, and to make an upward thrust the rotor must still push the air downwards: the induced velocity at the disc points down, against the incoming stream. The rotor therefore slows the upward stream: the velocity is $V_D$ far below (now upstream), $V_D-v_i$ at the disc and $V_D-v_2$ far above (now the wake). The far-wake velocity is the smaller of the two because $v_2>v_i$. All three are upward, which needs $V_D-v_2>0$, and the streamtube widens upward because the flow decelerates. Climb is the easier case because the induced velocity and the climb velocity both point down at the disc and add; in descent they oppose each other, and that opposition is the source of every complication in §§4–7.

### Thrust from the pressure jump

Bernoulli from far upstream (below) to just below the disc:

$$
p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho V_D^2=p_l+\tfrac12\rho(V_D-v_i)^2.
$$

Bernoulli from just above the disc to the wake (above):

$$
p_u+\tfrac12\rho(V_D-v_i)^2=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho(V_D-v_2)^2.
$$

Subtract the second from the first; the disc terms cancel:

$$
p_l-p_u=\tfrac12\rho V_D^2-\tfrac12\rho(V_D-v_2)^2.
$$

Expand:

$$
p_l-p_u=\tfrac12\rho\left(2V_Dv_2-v_2^2\right)=\rho\left(v_2V_D-\tfrac12v_2^2\right).
$$

So

$$
T=A(p_l-p_u)=\rho A\left(v_2V_D-\tfrac12v_2^2\right).
$$

### Thrust from the momentum balance

The mass flow is upward:

$$
\dot m=\rho A(V_D-v_i).
$$

Upward momentum flux enters at the bottom, $\dot mV_D$, and leaves at the top, $\dot m(V_D-v_2)$. The air loses upward momentum at the rate $\dot mv_2$, so the rotor pushes it down with that force and the air pushes the rotor up with

$$
T=\dot mV_D-\dot m(V_D-v_2)=\dot mv_2=\rho A(V_D-v_i)v_2.
$$

### Wake velocity and the induced-velocity equation

Equate the two thrusts and divide by $\rho Av_2$:

$$
V_D-v_i=V_D-\tfrac12v_2.
$$

Cancel $V_D$:

$$
\boxed{v_2=2v_i,}
$$

as in hover and climb. Substitute into the momentum thrust:

$$
T=\rho A(V_D-v_i)\,2v_i=2\rho A\left(V_Dv_i-v_i^2\right).
$$

Divide by $2\rho A$ and rearrange:

$$
v_i^2-V_Dv_i+\frac{T}{2\rho A}=0.
$$

Replace $T/2\rho A$ by $v_0^2$:

$$
v_i^2-V_Dv_i+v_0^2=0.
$$

Divide by $v_0^2$:

$$
\boxed{\bar v_i^2-\bar V_D\bar v_i+1=0.}
$$

### The roots, when they exist and which is physical

The quadratic formula with coefficients $1$, $-\bar V_D$, $1$:

$$
\bar v_i=\frac{\bar V_D\pm\sqrt{\bar V_D^2-4}}{2}=\frac{\bar V_D}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_D^2}{4}-1}.
$$

The roots are real only when $\bar V_D^2\geq4$:

$$
\boxed{\bar V_D\geq2,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad V_D\geq2v_0.}
$$

Hover ($\bar V_D=0$) is therefore not a limit of the descent equation: putting $\bar V_D=0$ gives $\bar v_i^2+1=0$, which has no real solution.

For $\bar V_D>2$ both roots are positive (their product is 1 and their sum $\bar V_D$), so positivity does not choose between them. The wake condition does. The model assumed upward flow everywhere, and in particular in the far wake:

$$
V_D-v_2=V_D-2v_i\geq0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bar v_i\leq\frac{\bar V_D}{2}.
$$

The larger root exceeds $\bar V_D/2$ by $\sqrt{\bar V_D^2/4-1}$, so its far-wake velocity is $-2v_0\sqrt{\bar V_D^2/4-1}<0$: air would leave the top of the streamtube going down while entering the bottom going up, which no single streamtube can do. The smaller root is the physical one:

$$
\boxed{\bar v_i=\frac{\bar V_D}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_D^2}{4}-1},\qquad \bar V_D\geq2.}
$$

At $\bar V_D=2$ the two roots meet at $\bar v_i=1$, and the far-wake velocity is $V_D-2v_i=0$: the wake has been brought to rest relative to the rotor. For a fast descent, the same expansion as in climb gives $\bar v_i\approx1/\bar V_D$.

### One equation for climb and descent

With $V_C=-V_D$ the descent equation becomes

$$
\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i+1=0,\qquad \bar V_C=-\bar V_D\leq-2,
$$

with roots

$$
\bar v_i=-\frac{\bar V_C}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_C^2}{4}-1},
$$

so climb and descent differ only in the sign of the constant term. Plotting every root of both equations against $\bar V_C$ (below) shows four mathematical branches; only two are physical.

## 5. Where momentum theory fails

The climb equation applies only for $\bar V_C\geq0$. Extended to small descents ($\bar V_C<0$), its positive root still gives a downward flow at the disc and in the wake, $V_C+v_i>0$ and $V_C+2v_i>0$, but far above the disc the free stream $V_C$ is now upward. The streamtube would carry air down at one end and up at the other, so the model's flow picture is wrong, even though the algebra still returns a number (the dashed blue curve).

The descent equation applies only for $\bar V_D\geq2$, where its roots are real.

Between them,

$$
-2<\bar V_C<0\qquad(0<V_D<2v_0),
$$

momentum theory has no valid solution. Physically, this is the range where the rotor's downwash ($v_i\sim v_0$) and the upward relative wind ($V_D$) are of the same order and oppose each other, so no clean slipstream forms. For a hovering helicopter this band is a real operating hazard: for the Lynx of the worked examples, $2v_0=25.8$ m/s, so every vertical descent slower than about 25 m/s is in it.

A simple bridge across the gap keeps the hover value, $\bar v_i=1$ (the green dashed line). Measured, averaged data show that the real downwash is much larger: it rises from $\bar v_i=1$ at hover to a peak of about 2.4 near $\bar V_C\approx-1.5$ and falls back to the windmill-brake branch near $\bar V_C=-2$. The power plot shows the consequence. With the simple model, power falls linearly from $Tv_0$ at hover to $-Tv_0$ at $\bar V_D=2$; with the measured downwash it stays near $+Tv_0$ over roughly $-1.7<\bar V_C<-0.5$ before dropping steeply. A rotor descending slowly needs about as much power as in hover, not less.

## 6. Rotor working states in descent

The states are named by the flow pattern through and around the disc.

![[aa_4_2_working_states.png|900]]

The **normal working state** covers hover and climb: air comes down from above, accelerates through the disc and leaves as a downward wake. Actuator disc theory with the climb equation describes it.

In **low speed descent** the upward relative wind starts to push the edge of the wake back up, and the tip vortices are no longer swept cleanly away below the disc; small recirculating regions form at the tips.

The **vortex ring state** follows at higher descent rates, roughly $-1.5<\bar V_C<0$ on the measured curve. The tip vortices collect into a doughnut-shaped ring around the disc edge. Air goes down through the inner disc, up outside the tips and back in from above, so it is recirculated rather than left behind. The flow is unsteady, the thrust fluctuates, the downwash is largest (about $2.4v_0$) and the rotor needs roughly hover power while descending. Raising collective to stop the descent only strengthens the ring, which is why a helicopter in this state can keep sinking.

In the **turbulent wake state**, roughly $-2<\bar V_C<-1.5$, the net flow through the disc has reversed to upward, and the rotor behaves like a bluff plate with a turbulent wake above it, with recirculating vortices sitting above the disc. Ideal autorotation falls in this band.

In the **windmill brake state**, $\bar V_C\leq-2$, the flow is upward everywhere, the rotor decelerates it and extracts power from it, and the descent equation of §4 describes it. The idealised slipstream that the descent equation assumes, upward flow everywhere with a uniform deceleration through the disc, is the **modelled descent**.

These boundaries are not sharp and are read approximately from averaged test data, so they should be quoted as ranges.

## 7. Power for axial motion of the disc

### The two expressions

In climb, from §3,

$$
P=T(V_C+v_i).
$$

In descent the air passes up through the disc at $V_D-v_i$ while the thrust acts upward on the rotor and so downward on the air, against the flow. The energy balance of §4's control volume gives the rate at which the air loses kinetic energy:

$$
\tfrac12\dot mV_D^2-\tfrac12\dot m(V_D-v_2)^2=\dot mv_2\left(V_D-\tfrac12v_2\right)=T(V_D-v_i).
$$

That energy goes into the rotor, so the power supplied to the rotor is its negative:

$$
\boxed{P=T(-V_D+v_i).}
$$

Its parts are a descent power $P_{desc}=-TV_D$ (the rate at which the aircraft's potential energy is released into the rotor) and the induced power $P_i=Tv_i$. Both formulas are the single expression $P=T(V_C+v_i)$ with $V_C=-V_D$. Normalised,

$$
\frac{P}{Tv_0}=-\bar V_D+\bar v_i.
$$

On the windmill brake branch, put in the physical root:

$$
\frac{P}{Tv_0}=-\frac{\bar V_D}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\bar V_D^2}{4}-1}\leq-1,
$$

so a rotor in the windmill brake state always extracts power, at least $Tv_0$.

### Autorotation

Autorotation is descent with zero total power: the rotor turns without an engine, driven by the upward air. With profile power left out, ideal autorotation needs

$$
P=T(-V_D+v_i)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i=V_D,\qquad \bar v_i=\bar V_D,
$$

which is the dash-dot line $\bar v_i=-\bar V_C$ on the induced-velocity plot.

> [!warning] Ideal autorotation is outside momentum theory
> Put $\bar v_i=\bar V_D$ into the descent equation: $\bar V_D^2-\bar V_D^2+1=1\neq0$. Put it into the climb equation with $\bar V_C=-\bar V_D$: $\bar V_D^2-\bar V_D^2-1=-1\neq0$. Neither equation has a zero-power solution. Zero net flow through the disc ($V_D-v_i=0$) is exactly the condition under which no slipstream exists, so autorotation always lies in the gap $0<\bar V_D<2$ and its descent rate has to come from measurement. With the simple model $\bar v_i=1$ it would be $\bar V_D=1$; the measured downwash crosses the line $\bar v_i=\bar V_D$ at about $\bar V_D=1.7$.

In practice the values are $\bar V_D\approx1.7$ for ideal autorotation and $\bar V_D\approx1.8$ for real autorotation. A real rotor also has to supply its profile power $P_{profile}$ ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]) from the air, so its net induced-plus-descent power must be negative:

$$
T(-V_D+v_i)=-P_{profile}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_D-v_i=\frac{P_{profile}}{T}>0,
$$

and it has to descend a little faster than ideal.

A second estimate treats the autorotating rotor as a drag-producing plate (a parachute) of area $A$ and drag coefficient $C_D$, whose drag balances the weight, i.e. equals the thrust:

$$
T=D=\tfrac12\rho V_D^2AC_D.
$$

From the definition of $v_0$, $T=2\rho Av_0^2$. Equate:

$$
2\rho Av_0^2=\tfrac12\rho V_D^2AC_D.
$$

Cancel $\rho A$ and rearrange:

$$
\frac{V_D^2}{v_0^2}=\frac{4}{C_D}.
$$

Take the root:

$$
\boxed{\bar V_D=\frac{2}{\sqrt{C_D}}.}
$$

A disc behaves like a bluff plate with $C_D$ of order 1.2–1.4: $C_D=1.23$ gives $\bar V_D=1.80$ and $C_D=1.38$ gives $1.70$, matching the measured range.

## 8. Worked examples

These use the Westland Lynx data of the hover example ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]): maximum take-off mass 5330 kg, $R=6.4$ m, $V_T=218.69$ m/s, $g=9.81$ m/s², $\rho=1.2256$ kg/m³. In axial flight $T=W$ (fuselage drag in vertical flight is left out).

$$
T=5330\times9.81=52\,287\ \text{N},\qquad A=\pi\times6.4^2=128.68\ \text{m}^2,
$$

$$
v_0=\sqrt{\frac{52\,287}{2\times1.2256\times128.68}}=12.875\ \text{m/s},\qquad Tv_0=673.2\ \text{kW}.
$$

### Example 1: Lynx in a 5 m/s vertical climb

Normalise:

$$
\bar V_C=\frac{5}{12.875}=0.3883.
$$

Physical climb root:

$$
\bar v_i=-0.1942+\sqrt{0.03770+1}=-0.1942+1.0187=0.8245,\qquad v_i=0.8245\times12.875=10.62\ \text{m/s}.
$$

Power:

$$
P=T(V_C+v_i)=52\,287\times(5+10.616)=816.5\ \text{kW},
$$

made of $P_{climb}=TV_C=261.4$ kW and $P_i=Tv_i=555.1$ kW. Against hover (673.2 kW) the climb costs 143.3 kW extra, close to the small-$\bar V_C$ estimate $\tfrac12TV_C=130.7$ kW; the second-order term $\bar V_C^2/8$ accounts for most of the difference ($1+0.1942+0.0189=1.213$ against the exact $1.2129$).

In coefficients: $\mu_z=5/218.69=0.02286$, $\lambda_i=10.616/218.69=0.04854$, so $C_P/C_T=0.07141$.

### Example 2: Lynx descending vertically at 30 m/s

$$
\bar V_D=\frac{30}{12.875}=2.330\geq2,
$$

so the windmill brake slipstream exists. The two roots:

$$
\bar v_i=1.1650\mp\sqrt{1.3573-1}=1.1650\mp0.5977=0.5673\ \text{or}\ 1.7627.
$$

The far wake decides. The smaller root gives $v_i=7.30$ m/s and a wake velocity $V_D-2v_i=15.39$ m/s upward, consistent. The larger gives $v_i=22.70$ m/s and $V_D-2v_i=-15.39$ m/s, a reversed wake, so it is rejected. The power:

$$
P=T(-V_D+v_i)=52\,287\times(-30+7.304)=-1187\ \text{kW},
$$

so the air drives the rotor with about 1.2 MW, and the excess has to be absorbed (by increasing blade pitch and so thrust, or by braking the rotor) if the descent is to be held steady.

### Example 3: Lynx descending vertically at 15 m/s

$$
\bar V_D=\frac{15}{12.875}=1.165<2.
$$

The descent equation has no real root, and the climb equation does not apply to a descent: the rotor is in the vortex ring state. The simple model $v_i=v_0$ would give $P=52\,287\times(-15+12.875)=-111$ kW, i.e. power extracted, whereas the measured downwash puts the power near $+Tv_0\approx670$ kW. The simple model gets even the sign wrong here; only test data give the power in this band.

### Example 4: vertical autorotation rate of the Lynx

With $\bar V_D\approx1.8$:

$$
V_D\approx1.8\times12.875=23.2\ \text{m/s}\ (\approx4560\ \text{ft/min}),
$$

and the equivalent drag coefficient is $C_D=4/\bar V_D^2=4/1.8^2=1.23$. A purely vertical autorotation is fast; real autorotative descents are flown with forward speed, which brings the rotor out of axial flight and into [[SESA3043 4.3 - Rotor in Forward Flight|4.3]].

## 9. Summary of results

| Quantity | Climb ($V_C\geq0$) | Descent ($V_D\geq2v_0$) |
|---|---|---|
| mass flow | $\rho A(V_C+v_i)$ | $\rho A(V_D-v_i)$ |
| thrust | $\rho A(v_2V_C+\tfrac12v_2^2)=\rho A(V_C+v_i)v_2$ | $\rho A(v_2V_D-\tfrac12v_2^2)=\rho A(V_D-v_i)v_2$ |
| wake | $v_2=2v_i$ | $v_2=2v_i$ |
| equation | $\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i-1=0$ | $\bar v_i^2-\bar V_D\bar v_i+1=0$ |
| physical root | $-\bar V_C/2+\sqrt{\bar V_C^2/4+1}$ | $\bar V_D/2-\sqrt{\bar V_D^2/4-1}$ |
| power | $T(V_C+v_i)$ | $T(-V_D+v_i)$ |

For $0<V_D<2v_0$ no row applies.
