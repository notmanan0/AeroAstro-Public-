# SESA3043 4.3 - Rotor in Forward Flight

> [!abstract] Summary
> In forward flight the rotor disc is tilted forward by the disc tilt $\gamma$, so that the thrust has a forward component to balance the drag. The free stream $V$ splits into $V_x=V\cos\gamma$ along the disc and $V_z=V\sin\gamma$ through it, and actuator disc theory with mass flow $\rho AV'$ gives the implicit induced-velocity equation $v_i=(T/2\rho A)\big/\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2}$, solved by Newton–Raphson; at high speed $v_i\approx T/(2\rho AV)$. The power is $P=Tv_i+TV_z$ plus the profile power of blade element theory: induced power falls with speed, parasite power ($TV_z=DV$ in level flight) rises as $V^3$, so the total has a minimum at an intermediate speed.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a rotor of known weight, disc area and drag flies forward at speed $V$ and the disc tilt, induced velocity or power is wanted.
> - **Assumptions:** steady level flight; uniform induced velocity normal to the disc; mass flow $\rho AV'$ through the disc and far-wake increment $2v_i$; thrust normal to the disc; fuselage drag horizontal; angles in radians in any series expansion.
> - **Method:** 1. $D$ from the drag data; $\tan\gamma=D/W$, $T=\sqrt{W^2+D^2}$. 2. $V_x=V\cos\gamma$, $V_z=V\sin\gamma$. 3. Solve $v_i=(T/2\rho A)/\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2}$ by Newton–Raphson from $v_i=v_0$, or use $v_i\approx T/(2\rho AV)$ when $V\gg v_0$. 4. $P_i=Tv_i$, $P_{parasite}=TV_z$, $P_{profile}=\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D$. 5. Sum, and check against hover.
> - **Common mistakes:** forgetting $V_z$ inside the root (it adds to $v_i$, not to $V_x$); using $T=W$ when the drag is not small, or quoting the high-speed formula at low speed, where it overestimates $v_i$ without bound.
> - **Practise:** [[#8. Worked example: Lynx at 60 m/s]] · [[#Self-study tasks]] (no lecturer-set exercise yet)

A helicopter moves forward by tilting its rotor. With the disc tilted nose-down, the thrust, which acts normal to the disc, gains a forward component that pulls the aircraft along and balances the fuselage drag, while its vertical component carries the weight. The tilt is set with the cyclic pitch, which varies the blade pitch once per revolution; the collective changes the pitch of all blades together and so the size of the thrust.

Forward speed changes the flow in two ways. First, the blades no longer see the same air speed all the way round: a blade moving into the wind (the advancing blade) sees its rotational speed plus the flight speed, and a blade moving with the wind (the retreating blade) sees the difference, so near the hub on the retreating side the air flows over the blade backwards. Second, the disc now meets a large mass of fresh air every second, coming mostly from ahead, rather than only the air it draws down itself. A rotor flying fast is like a wing of span $2R$: it makes its thrust by giving a small downward velocity to a large mass flow, so the induced velocity, and with it the induced power, falls as the speed rises. Smoke filaments around a helicopter at 53 mph show this picture: the streams pass through the disc, are deflected slightly downward behind it and roll up at the disc edges, much like the trailing vortices of a wing.

## 1. Co-ordinate system and disc tilt

The axes are attached to the rotor disc. The $x$ axis lies in the disc plane and points forward, the $y$ axis lies in the disc plane at right angles to it (sideways), and the $z$ axis is normal to the disc, upward along the thrust. The **disc tilt** $\gamma$ is the angle by which the disc is tilted forward from the horizontal, which is also the angle between the thrust and the vertical.

The incoming velocity relative to the rotor is the flight speed $V$, horizontal and from the front. Resolve it in disc axes:

- along the disc plane, the **flow velocity parallel to the disc plane** is
$$
V_x=V\cos\gamma;
$$
- normal to the disc, the **flow velocity perpendicular to the disc plane** is
$$
V_z=V\sin\gamma.
$$

Because the front of the disc is lower than the rear, the oncoming air meets the upper surface of the disc, so $V_z$ passes down through the disc, in the same direction as the induced velocity $v_i$. A forward-tilted rotor is therefore partly in a climb relative to its own axis: $V_z$ plays the role of $V_C$ in [[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight|4.2]]. With $\gamma$ positive forward, both $V_z$ and $v_i$ are positive downward through the disc.

At the disc the induced velocity adds to $V_z$, so the velocity components are $V_x$ along the disc and $V_z+v_i$ normal to it, and the **overall inflow velocity** is their resultant,

$$
V'=\sqrt{V_x^2+\left(V_z+v_i\right)^2}.
$$

Expanding it with $V_x^2+V_z^2=V^2$ shows how the induced velocity enters:

$$
V'^2=V^2\cos^2\gamma+V^2\sin^2\gamma+2Vv_i\sin\gamma+v_i^2=V^2+2Vv_i\sin\gamma+v_i^2.
$$

The velocity ratios are formed with the tip speed $V_T=\Omega R$, as in hover:

$$
\mu=\frac{V}{V_T},\qquad \mu_x=\frac{V_x}{V_T},\qquad \mu_z=\frac{V_z}{V_T},\qquad \lambda_i=\frac{v_i}{V_T},\qquad \mu_{zD}=\frac{V_z+v_i}{V_T}=\mu_z+\lambda_i.
$$

$\mu$ is the usual advance ratio of rotorcraft texts (SESA2023's propeller advance ratio $J=V_\infty/nD$ is a different quantity), and $\mu_{zD}$ is the total normal inflow through the disc.

## 2. Advancing and retreating blades

The velocity seen by a blade element at radius $r$ normal to the blade, in the plane of the disc, is its rotational speed $\Omega r$ plus the component of $V_x$ along the direction of blade motion. Measure the blade azimuth $\psi$ from the downstream (tail) position in the direction of rotation (the usual textbook convention). At $\psi$ the blade moves at right angles to its own span, and the free-stream component against that motion is $V_x\sin\psi$, so

$$
U_T=\Omega r+V_x\sin\psi.
$$

At $\psi=90^\circ$ the blade advances into the wind and its tip sees $\Omega R+V_x=V_T(1+\mu_x)$; at $\psi=270^\circ$ it retreats and its tip sees $V_T(1-\mu_x)$. In hover ($\mu_x=0$) the velocity is the same at every azimuth, growing linearly from hub to tip.

![[aa_4_3_advancing_retreating.png|900]]

On the retreating side $\sin\psi<0$, and near the hub $\Omega r$ is small, so $U_T$ can become negative: the air flows over the blade from the trailing edge to the leading edge. Set $U_T<0$:

$$
\Omega r<-V_x\sin\psi,
$$

$$
r<-\mu_xR\sin\psi.
$$

The boundary $r=-\mu_xR\sin\psi$ is a circle. Write it in disc co-ordinates $x'=r\cos\psi$, $y'=r\sin\psi$ by multiplying by $r$:

$$
r^2=-\mu_xR\,r\sin\psi,
$$

$$
x'^2+y'^2+\mu_xRy'=0,
$$

$$
x'^2+\left(y'+\frac{\mu_xR}{2}\right)^2=\left(\frac{\mu_xR}{2}\right)^2.
$$

The **reverse-flow region** is a circle of diameter $\mu_xR$ on the retreating side, touching the hub. It is small at moderate speed (1.75 m across for the Lynx at 60 m/s, out of a 12.8 m disc) but grows linearly with speed, and together with retreating-blade stall and advancing-tip compressibility it limits the top speed of a conventional helicopter.

The lift asymmetry between the two sides is removed by flapping and cyclic pitch, which the actuator disc ignores: it treats the disc as a uniform pressure jump, so it can predict the induced velocity and power averaged over the disc, but not this azimuthal variation.

## 3. Actuator disc theory in forward flight

### Mass flow and momentum

The streamtube through the disc is now inclined: the air arrives at $V$ from ahead, passes through the tilted disc and leaves as a wake deflected downward and backward. The momentum argument keeps the hover and axial-flight ingredients and changes only the mass flow.

The mass flow through the disc is taken as the density times the disc area times the overall inflow velocity:

$$
\dot m=\rho AV'.
$$

This is an assumption, the key one of forward-flight momentum theory (Glauert's). It reduces to the axial mass flow $\rho A(V_z+v_i)$ when $V_x=0$, and at high speed to $\rho AV$, the mass flow through a circle of area $A$ facing the free stream, which is the mass of air a wing of span $2R$ influences.

The disc adds momentum only normal to itself (the thrust is normal to the disc), raising the normal velocity by $v_i$ at the disc and by $v_2$ in the far wake. The momentum created is the thrust:

$$
T=\dot mv_2=\rho AV'v_2.
$$

### The wake velocity

The power the rotor puts into the air is the thrust times the normal velocity through the disc, as in [[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight|4.2]]:

$$
P=T\left(V_z+v_i\right).
$$

The air arrives with velocity components $(V_x,V_z)$ in disc axes and leaves the far wake with $(V_x,V_z+v_2)$. The rise in kinetic-energy flux is

$$
\Delta\dot E=\tfrac12\dot m\left[V_x^2+(V_z+v_2)^2\right]-\tfrac12\dot m\left[V_x^2+V_z^2\right],
$$

$$
\Delta\dot E=\tfrac12\dot m\left(2V_zv_2+v_2^2\right)=\dot mv_2\left(V_z+\tfrac12v_2\right)=T\left(V_z+\tfrac12v_2\right).
$$

Set $\Delta\dot E=P$:

$$
T\left(V_z+\tfrac12v_2\right)=T\left(V_z+v_i\right),
$$

$$
\boxed{v_2=2v_i,}
$$

the same as hover and axial flight. Then

$$
T=\rho AV'\cdot2v_i.
$$

### The induced velocity

Solve for $v_i$:

$$
v_i=\frac{T}{2\rho AV'},
$$

and write out $V'$:

$$
\boxed{v_i=\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_x^2+\left(V_z+v_i\right)^2}}.}
$$

The unknown $v_i$ appears on both sides, so the equation is implicit. With $T/2\rho A=v_0^2$ and every velocity divided by $v_0$ it reads

$$
\bar v_i=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar V_x^2+\left(\bar V_z+\bar v_i\right)^2}}.
$$

Three limits check it.

In hover, $V_x=V_z=0$:

$$
v_i=\frac{v_0^2}{v_i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i^2=v_0^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i=v_0,
$$

the hover result of [[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]].

In axial flight, $V_x=0$ and $V_z=V_C$:

$$
v_i\left(V_C+v_i\right)=v_0^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bar v_i^2+\bar V_C\bar v_i-1=0,
$$

the climb equation of [[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight|4.2]].

At high speed, $V\gg v_i$. Then $V'^2=V^2+2Vv_i\sin\gamma+v_i^2\approx V^2$, and

$$
\boxed{v_i\approx\frac{T}{2\rho AV}=\frac{v_0^2}{V},\qquad \bar v_i\approx\frac{1}{\bar V}.}
$$

This is the induced downwash of an elliptically loaded wing of span $b=2R$ carrying lift $T$: with $A=\pi b^2/4$, $v_i=2T/(\rho\pi b^2V)$, the uniform downwash of lifting-line theory ([[Downwash and Induced Drag]]). The high-speed rotor is a circular wing.

### Zero disc tilt in closed form

With $\gamma=0$, $V_z=0$ and $V_x=V$, square the normalised equation:

$$
\bar v_i^2\left(\bar V^2+\bar v_i^2\right)=1,
$$

$$
\bar v_i^4+\bar V^2\bar v_i^2-1=0.
$$

This is a quadratic in $\bar v_i^2$. Its positive root (the product of the two roots in $\bar v_i^2$ is $-1$, so only one is positive):

$$
\bar v_i^2=\frac{-\bar V^2+\sqrt{\bar V^4+4}}{2}.
$$

At $\bar V=1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 5$ this gives $\bar v_i=0.786,\ 0.486,\ 0.331,\ 0.200$, against $1/\bar V=1,\ 0.5,\ 0.333,\ 0.2$: the high-speed limit is already within 3 % at $\bar V=2$ and useless below $\bar V\approx1$.

![[aa_4_3_induced_velocity_forward.png|900]]

Forward tilt adds $V_z$ to the normal inflow and so lowers $\bar v_i$ a little at moderate speed; the axial climb of 4.2 ($\gamma=90^\circ$) is the extreme case. At high speed every tilt collapses onto $1/\bar V$, because $V'\to V$ whatever the direction.

### Non-dimensional form

Divide $v_i=(T/2\rho A)/V'$ by $V_T$, multiplying and dividing the right-hand side by $V_T$:

$$
\lambda_i=\frac{T}{2\rho AV_T^2}\cdot\frac{1}{V'/V_T}.
$$

With $C_T=T/(\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A)$,

$$
\frac{T}{2\rho AV_T^2}=\frac{C_T\cdot\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A}{2\rho AV_T^2}=\frac{C_T}{4},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{\lambda_i=\frac{C_T}{4}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_x^2+\left(\mu_z+\lambda_i\right)^2}}.}
$$

In hover it gives $\lambda_i^2=C_T/4$, i.e. $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$, as in 4.1.

## 4. Solving for the induced velocity by Newton–Raphson

Newton–Raphson solves $f(x)=0$ by following the tangent at the current guess to the axis ([[Direct Substitution and Newton-Raphson]]):

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$

Recast the induced-velocity equation as $f(v_i)=0$, with $k=T/2\rho A$:

$$
f(v_i)=v_i-k\left[V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2\right]^{-1/2}.
$$

Differentiate with the chain rule. The derivative of the bracket is $2(V_z+v_i)$:

$$
f'(v_i)=1-k\left(-\tfrac12\right)\left[V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2\right]^{-3/2}\cdot2\left(V_z+v_i\right),
$$

$$
f'(v_i)=1+k\,\frac{V_z+v_i}{\left[V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2\right]^{3/2}}.
$$

So the iteration is

$$
\boxed{v_{i,n+1}=v_{i,n}-\frac{v_{i,n}-\left(\dfrac{T}{2\rho A}\right)\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_{i,n})^2}}}{1+\left(\dfrac{T}{2\rho A}\right)\dfrac{V_z+v_{i,n}}{\left[\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_{i,n})^2}\right]^3}},}
$$

and, in the same way, the non-dimensional form

$$
\lambda_{i,n+1}=\lambda_{i,n}-\frac{\lambda_{i,n}-\dfrac{C_T}{4}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_x^2+(\mu_z+\lambda_{i,n})^2}}}{1+\dfrac{C_T}{4}\dfrac{\mu_z+\lambda_{i,n}}{\left[\sqrt{\mu_x^2+(\mu_z+\lambda_{i,n})^2}\right]^3}}.
$$

The hover value $v_0$ (or $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$) is a safe starting guess: $f$ is increasing wherever $V_z+v_i>0$, and the iteration converges in three or four steps (worked example below). At high speed the starting guess $T/(2\rho AV)$ is already within a fraction of a per cent.

## 5. Disc tilt from the force balance

In steady, level, unaccelerated flight take the thrust $T$ normal to the disc, the weight $W$ vertical, and the fuselage drag $D$ horizontal and rearward; the tail rotor, fuselage lift and any drag of the rotor hub are left out. Resolve horizontally and vertically:

$$
T\sin\gamma=D,
$$

$$
T\cos\gamma=W.
$$

Divide the first by the second:

$$
\boxed{\tan\gamma=\frac{D}{W}.}
$$

Square and add:

$$
T^2\left(\sin^2\gamma+\cos^2\gamma\right)=D^2+W^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{T=\sqrt{W^2+D^2}.}
$$

The drag is written $D=\tfrac12\rho V^2f$, with $f$ the equivalent flat-plate area of the fuselage (a textbook drag measure, not a course symbol). Since $D\propto V^2$, the disc tilts further forward as the speed rises, $V_z=V\sin\gamma$ grows rapidly, and at low speed $T\approx W$ and $\gamma\approx0$.

## 6. Power consumption

### The three parts

The power is

$$
\text{Power}=\text{Induced power}+\text{Parasite power}+\text{Profile power}.
$$

The rotor's thrust acts on air crossing the disc at the normal velocity $V_z+v_i$, which gives the first two terms:

$$
P=T(V_z+v_i)=Tv_i+TV_z.
$$

The **induced power** $Tv_i$ is the energy left in the wake to make the thrust. It is largest in hover and falls roughly as $1/V$ at speed, since $v_i\approx T/(2\rho AV)$:

$$
P_i\approx\frac{T^2}{2\rho AV}.
$$

The **parasite power** $TV_z$ is the work done against the fuselage drag. In level flight, multiply $T\sin\gamma=D$ by $V$:

$$
TV_z=TV\sin\gamma=DV=\tfrac12\rho V^3f,
$$

so it grows as $V^3$. In a climb with forward speed $TV_z$ would also contain the climb power, as $TV_C$ did in 4.2.

The **profile power** is the power to drive the blade sections through the air against their own drag. Actuator disc theory has no blades, so it misses this term; blade element theory supplies it ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]):

$$
P_{profile}=\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D,
$$

for $N$ blades of constant chord $c$ and constant drag coefficient. This is its hover value; in forward flight the advancing blade sees a higher speed than the retreating blade, and the azimuthal average rises roughly as $(1+3\mu^2)$ in the simplest blade-element estimate (an extension, not used in the example below).

In coefficients, dividing by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A$ and using solidity $s=Nc/\pi R$,

$$
C_P=C_T\left(\lambda_i+\mu_z\right)+\tfrac14sC_{D0}=C_T\,\mu_{zD}+\tfrac14sC_{D0},
$$

which is the hover $C_P=C_T\lambda_i+\tfrac14sC_{D0}$ with the extra normal inflow $\mu_z$ added, and the same form as $C_P/C_T=\mu_z+\lambda_i$ in axial flight.

### The power curve

![[aa_4_3_power_breakdown.png|900]]

Falling induced power and rising parasite power give the power-required curve its bucket. For the Lynx with an assumed $f=1.5$ m², the total falls from 838 kW in hover to a minimum of about 438 kW near 42 m/s and rises again beyond. The minimum-power speed gives the longest endurance and the lowest rate of descent in forward-flight autorotation, and the hover needs more power than any speed up to about 85 m/s, which is why a helicopter can often fly at speeds where it could not hover at the same weight, altitude and temperature.

## 7. Comparison with axial flight

The forward-flight equation contains 4.2 as a special case. Setting $V_x=0$ turns $V'$ into $V_z+v_i$ and the equation into the climb quadratic; the extra $V_x^2$ under the root can only increase $V'$, so at the same thrust and normal inflow a rotor in forward flight always has a lower induced velocity than in a vertical climb at $V_C=V_z$. Forward flight also removes the descent problem of 4.2: as long as $V_x$ is large, $V'$ stays well away from zero even when $V_z+v_i$ is small or negative, so the vortex ring state is a hazard of steep, slow descents, not of normal forward flight.

## 8. Worked example: Lynx at 60 m/s

The rotor data are those of the hover example ([[SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover|4.1]]): mass 5330 kg, $R=6.4$ m, $N=4$, $V_T=218.69$ m/s, $\rho=1.2256$ kg/m³, $g=9.81$ m/s², so $W=52\,287$ N and $A=128.68$ m². The drag area $f=1.5$ m² is an assumed value for illustration, not course data; the blade data are those assumed in the blade-element example of 4.1, solidity $s=0.08$ (so $c=s\pi R/N=0.402$ m) and $C_{D0}=0.010$.

**Drag and disc tilt.**

$$
D=\tfrac12\times1.2256\times60^2\times1.5=3309\ \text{N},
$$

$$
\gamma=\tan^{-1}\frac{3309}{52\,287}=3.62^\circ,\qquad T=\sqrt{52\,287^2+3309^2}=52\,392\ \text{N}.
$$

**Velocity components.**

$$
V_x=60\cos3.62^\circ=59.88\ \text{m/s},\qquad V_z=60\sin3.62^\circ=3.790\ \text{m/s}.
$$

**Induced velocity.** $k=T/2\rho A=166.10$ m²/s², so the hover-like starting guess is $v_{i,0}=\sqrt k=12.888$ m/s. Newton–Raphson gives

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $v_{i,n}$ (m/s) | 12.888 | 2.7887 | 2.75748 | 2.757477 |

so $v_i=2.757$ m/s and $V'=\sqrt{59.88^2+(3.790+2.757)^2}=60.24$ m/s. The high-speed estimate $T/(2\rho AV)=2.768$ m/s is 0.4 % high.

**Non-dimensional check.** $\mu=0.2744$, $\mu_x=0.2738$, $\mu_z=0.01733$, $C_T=0.013892$. Starting from $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2=0.05893$, the iteration gives $0.012752$, $0.0126091$, $0.0126091$, so $\lambda_i=0.012609$, which is $2.757/218.69$; $\mu_{zD}=0.02994$.

**Power.**

$$
P_i=Tv_i=52\,392\times2.757=144.5\ \text{kW},
$$

$$
P_{parasite}=TV_z=52\,392\times3.790=198.5\ \text{kW}\quad(=DV=3309\times60),
$$

$$
P_{profile}=\tfrac18\times1.2256\times218.69^3\times4\times0.4021\times6.4\times0.010=164.9\ \text{kW},
$$

$$
P=144.5+198.5+164.9=508\ \text{kW}.
$$

In hover the same rotor needs $673.2+164.9=838$ kW, so cruising at 60 m/s takes 39 % less power than hovering. The advancing tip sees $218.7+59.9=278.6$ m/s and the retreating tip $158.8$ m/s, and the reverse-flow circle is $\mu_xR=1.75$ m across.

> [!check] Induced power at speed
> The induced power at 60 m/s is 21 % of the hover value ($144.5/673.2$), while $v_0/V'=12.888/60.24=0.214$: at speed $P_i/P_{i,hover}\approx v_0/V$, as $v_i\approx v_0^2/V$ predicts.

## 9. Summary of results

| Quantity | Formula | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| velocity components | $V_x=V\cos\gamma$, $V_z=V\sin\gamma$ | disc axes; $\gamma$ forward tilt |
| overall inflow | $V'=\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2}$ | uniform $v_i$ normal to the disc |
| thrust | $T=\rho AV'\cdot2v_i$ | mass flow $\rho AV'$; $v_2=2v_i$ |
| induced velocity | $v_i=(T/2\rho A)/V'$; $\lambda_i=(C_T/4)/\sqrt{\mu_x^2+(\mu_z+\lambda_i)^2}$ | implicit; Newton–Raphson |
| high speed | $v_i\approx T/(2\rho AV)$ | $V\gg v_0$ |
| disc tilt | $\tan\gamma=D/W$, $T=\sqrt{W^2+D^2}$ | steady level flight, $D$ horizontal |
| power | $P=Tv_i+TV_z+\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D$ | $TV_z=DV$ in level flight; hover profile power |

## Self-study tasks

No exercise is set on slides 69–90. The tasks below are suggested, not lecturer-set.

### Core

- [ ] Derive $v_i=(T/2\rho A)/\sqrt{V_x^2+(V_z+v_i)^2}$ and check that it reduces to hover, to the 4.2 climb equation and to $T/(2\rho AV)$ (suggested, not lecturer-set; CH4 slides 81–85). → [[#3. Actuator disc theory in forward flight]]
- [ ] Code the Newton–Raphson iteration and reproduce the Lynx power breakdown at 60 m/s, then sweep $V$ to find the minimum-power speed (suggested, not lecturer-set; CH4 slides 86–88). → [[#8. Worked example: Lynx at 60 m/s]]

### Further practice

- [ ] Show that the reverse-flow region is a circle of diameter $\mu_xR$ and find its size for the Lynx at 80 m/s (answer 2.33 m with the example's drag area) (suggested, not lecturer-set; CH4 slide 73). → [[#2. Advancing and retreating blades]]
