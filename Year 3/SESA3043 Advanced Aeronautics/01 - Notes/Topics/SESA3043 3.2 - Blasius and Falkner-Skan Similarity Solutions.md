# SESA3043 3.2 - Blasius and Falkner-Skan Similarity Solutions

> [!abstract] Summary
> Laminar profiles on a flat plate are stretched copies of one another, so a similarity variable $\eta=y/\xi(x)$ turns the boundary-layer PDEs into one ODE, the Blasius equation $f'''+ff''=0$. Solving it by shooting gives all the flat-plate results, including $\delta_{99}/x=4.91/\sqrt{Re_x}$ and $C_f=0.664/\sqrt{Re_x}$. The same idea works for any power-law edge velocity $U_e=cx^m$ (wedge flow), giving the Falkner–Skan equation, whose parameter $\beta$ runs from accelerating flows to separation at $\beta=-0.19884$.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a laminar layer grows on a flat plate, or under a power-law edge velocity $U_e=cx^m$.
> - **Assumptions:** 2-D, steady, incompressible, laminar; similarity needs a power-law $U_e$.
> - **Method:** 1. Find $m$, then $\beta=2m/(m+1)$. 2. $\xi=\sqrt{2\nu x/((m+1)U_e)}$, $\eta=y/\xi$. 3. Read $f''(0)$, $\eta^*$, $\theta^*$ from the table. 4. $\delta^*=\xi\eta^*$, $\theta=\xi\theta^*$, $C_f=2\nu f''(0)/(U_e\xi)$. 5. Check $H$; separation at $\beta=-0.19884$.
> - **Common mistakes:** using the Blasius $\xi$ ($m=0$) for a Falkner–Skan flow; confusing the wedge angle $\beta\pi$ with $\beta$.
> - **Practise:** [[#7.2 Revision 3.2 Q4: the wedge with β = 0.3]] · [[SESA3043 Mock Questions - Chapters 1-3#Q7. Blasius plate, displacement effect and an adverse-gradient wedge flow|Mock Q7]]

Velocity profiles at three stations along a flat plate get thicker downstream, but plotted against $y/\delta(x)$ they lie on top of one another. The layer at $x=1$ m is the layer at $x=10$ cm, magnified by $\sqrt{10}$ in $y$. Nothing in the flat-plate problem has a length scale of its own (the plate is semi-infinite), so the only way to make $y$ dimensionless is with the diffusion length $\sqrt{\nu x/U_e}$ from 1.4. Similarity is the mathematical statement of that: one shape, one ODE, solved once for every station.

Wedge flows keep this property because a power-law $U_e=cx^m$ still has no intrinsic length (scale $x$ by any factor and $U_e$ just scales too). Any other $U_e(x)$, such as a real aerofoil, breaks similarity; that is what the integral methods of 3.3 and 3.5 are for.

## 1. The reduced BL equation in stream-function form

From 1.4, for a flat plate ($U_e$ constant, $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx=0$):

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0,
\qquad
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\nu\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
$$

Introduce $\psi$ with $u=\partial\psi/\partial y$, $v=-\partial\psi/\partial x$. Continuity is then automatic:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\partial y}-\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\partial x}=0.
$$

Substituting into $x$-momentum gives the five-term reduced BL equation:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}}_{①}\underbrace{\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\partial y}}_{②}-\underbrace{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}}_{③}\underbrace{\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2}}_{④}=\nu\underbrace{\frac{\partial^3\psi}{\partial y^3}}_{⑤}.
$$

The streamfunction has turned two second-order PDEs (continuity and $x$-momentum) into one third-order PDE in $\psi$, the reduced boundary-layer equation. It's still a PDE in $x$ and $y$; the similarity variables of §2 are what turn it into an ODE.

The pressure gradient is carried as $U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ rather than $\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx$. Written with $\mathrm dp/\mathrm dx$, the BL equations have three unknowns ($p$, $u$, $v$) and only two equations. With $U_e(x)$ taken from the outer potential flow there are two unknowns for two equations, and the system can be solved numerically. Blasius and Falkner–Skan are the special cases that can be solved (almost) analytically.

## 2. The Blasius similarity variables

![[aa_blasius_shooting.png|900]]

The length $\xi$ starts from the order-of-magnitude analysis of 1.4, which gave only $\delta/x\sim Re_x^{-1/2}$, not an equation. Blasius replaced it with an *equality* for a stand-in length $\xi$, with a constant $k$ chosen for convenience as $\sqrt2$:

$$
\frac{\xi}{x}=\frac{k}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{U_ex/\nu}}.
$$

Multiply by $x$:

$$
\xi=\sqrt2\,x\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{U_ex}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}}.
$$

So $\xi$ resembles $\delta$ without being equal to it, and $\eta=y/\xi$ looks like $y/\delta$. The $\sqrt2$ is a choice, which is why two scalings exist (warning below).

**Variable 1**, depends only on $x$:

$$
\xi(x)=\left(\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}\right)^{1/2},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx}=\left(\frac{2\nu}{U_e}\right)^{1/2}\frac12x^{-1/2}=\left(\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{2x}=\frac{\xi}{2x}.
$$

**Variable 2**, the similarity coordinate:

$$
\eta=\frac{y}{\xi},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}=-\frac{y}{\xi^2}\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx}=-\frac{y}{\xi^2}\frac{\xi}{2x}=-\frac{\eta}{2x},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}=\frac1\xi.
$$

**Variable 3**, the dimensionless stream function, a function of $\eta$ only:

$$
f(\eta)=\frac{\psi}{U_e\xi},
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=f'\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}=-\frac{\eta}{2x}f',
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=f'\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}=\frac1\xi f'.
$$

| No. | Variable | $\partial/\partial x$ | $\partial/\partial y$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\xi=(2\nu x/U_e)^{1/2}$ | $\xi/2x$ | 0 |
| 2 | $\eta=y/\xi$ | $-\eta/2x$ | $1/\xi$ |
| 3 | $f=\psi/(U_e\xi)$ | $-\eta f'/2x$ | $f'/\xi$ |

> [!warning] Two scalings of Blasius in the vault
> The course puts $\sqrt2$ inside $\xi$. [[SESA3043 1.4 - Two-Dimensional Incompressible Boundary Layers#5. Check: the exact flat-plate solution (Blasius)|1.4 §5]] and SESA2029 use $\eta_o=y\sqrt{U/\nu x}$, $\psi=\sqrt{\nu Ux}\,f_o$, which gives $f_o'''+\tfrac12f_of_o''=0$ with $f_o''(0)=0.33206$.
> - The two are the same solution: $\eta_o=\sqrt2\,\eta$ and $f_o(\eta_o)=\sqrt2\,f(\eta)$.
> - Hence $f''(0)=\sqrt2\times0.33206=0.4696$, $\eta^*_o=\sqrt2\times1.2168=1.7208$ and $\eta_{99,o}=\sqrt2\times3.472=4.91$.
> - Every physical result ($\delta^*/x$, $C_f$, …) is identical. Use the course scaling in this module's exams.

## 3. Deriving the Blasius equation

**First derivatives of $\psi=U_e\xi f$.** In $y$ ($\xi$ does not depend on $y$):

$$
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}=U_e\xi\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=U_e\xi\cdot\frac1\xi f'=U_ef'.
$$

So $f'=u/U_e$: the slope of $f$ is the velocity profile. In $x$ (product rule, both $\xi$ and $f$ depend on $x$):

$$
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=U_e\left(\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx}f+\xi\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)=U_e\left(\frac{\xi}{2x}f-\xi\frac{\eta}{2x}f'\right)=\frac{U_e\xi}{2x}\left(f-\eta f'\right).
$$

**Higher derivatives.** Each $y$-derivative brings a factor $1/\xi$:

$$
\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(U_ef')=U_ef''\frac1\xi=\frac{U_e}{\xi}f'',
\qquad
\frac{\partial^3\psi}{\partial y^3}=\frac{U_e}{\xi}f'''\frac1\xi=\frac{U_e}{\xi^2}f''',
$$

$$
\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(U_ef')=U_ef''\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}=-\frac{U_e\eta}{2x}f''.
$$

**Substitute into ①②−③④=ν⑤.**

$$
U_ef'\left(-\frac{U_e\eta}{2x}f''\right)-\frac{U_e\xi}{2x}\left(f-\eta f'\right)\frac{U_e}{\xi}f''=\nu\frac{U_e}{\xi^2}f'''.
$$

The $\xi$ cancels in the second term:

$$
-\frac{U_e^2}{2x}\eta f'f''-\frac{U_e^2}{2x}\left(f-\eta f'\right)f''=\nu\frac{U_e}{\xi^2}f'''.
$$

**Tidy up.** Multiply by $\dfrac{2x}{U_e^2}$:

$$
-\eta f'f''-ff''+\eta f'f''=\frac{2\nu x}{U_e\,\xi^2}f'''.
$$

By definition $\xi^2=2\nu x/U_e$, so the coefficient on the right is exactly 1, and the $\eta f'f''$ terms cancel:

$$
\boxed{f'''+ff''=0}
$$

A shorter route does the tidying in two moves. Divide the substituted line by $\nu U_e$:

$$
-\frac{U_e}{2\nu x}\eta f'f''-\frac{U_e}{2\nu x}\left(f-\eta f'\right)f''=\frac{1}{\xi^2}f'''.
$$

Multiply by $\xi^2=2\nu x/U_e$, which removes every constant:

$$
-\eta f'f''-\left(f-\eta f'\right)f''=f'''.
$$

Expand the bracket; the $\eta f'f''$ terms cancel and $f'''+ff''=0$ again.

The exam wants to see where the equation comes from, not a memorised derivation. The route is two PDEs → stream function → one PDE → similarity variables → one nonlinear ODE. Terms ②, ④ and ⑤ are home-study derivations (step-by-step Blackboard notes, or White's *Viscous Fluid Flow*); all five are worked above.

**Boundary conditions:**

| Physical condition | In $\psi$, $u$ | In $f$ |
|---|---|---|
| the wall is a streamline | $\psi(y=0)=0$ | $f(0)=0$ |
| no slip | $u(y=0)=0$ | $f'(0)=0$ |
| edge | $u\to U_e$ as $y\to\infty$ | $f'(\infty)=1$ |

Once the $\eta f'f''$ terms cancel, $x$ and $y$ only appear through $\eta$. That isn't luck: it happens because $\xi\propto\sqrt x$, the scaling found by order of magnitude in 1.4, and any other power of $x$ would leave an explicit $x$ behind.

## 4. Solving it: guess-and-shooting method

Three conditions are needed at one end to march a third-order ODE, but only two are known at the wall ($f$, $f'$); the third is at infinity. So (panel a of the Blasius figure in §2):

1. guess $s=f''(0)$;
2. integrate $f'''=-ff''$ from $\eta=0$ as an initial-value problem (e.g. RK4, as in [[SESA2029 A6 - Higher-Order Time Integration, Runge-Kutta and the Blasius Equation|SESA2029 A6]]);
3. check $f'$ at large $\eta$: too big → reduce $s$; too small → increase it;
4. repeat (bisection or secant) until $f'(\infty)=1$.

Result: $f''(0)=0.46960$ (course scaling; RK45 with Brent's method, to five figures).

The ODE is still nonlinear (the $ff''$ term), so the solution is pseudo-exact: exact apart from the numerical shooting. The plotted solution comes from a MATLAB code by A. El-Tahan (2016). The three curves read as follows:
- $f\propto\psi$: zero at the wall, like a stream function;
- $f'=u/U_e$: the velocity profile, from 0 at the wall to 1;
- $f''\propto\tau$: largest at the wall (that is the wall shear) and zero at the edge.

All three are functions of $\eta$, not of $y$ or $y/\delta$. White & Majdalani also tabulate them against $\eta$.

## 5. Blasius boundary-layer properties

![[aa_falkner_skan_solutions.png|900]]

### Thickness

$f'=0.99$ at $\eta_{99}=y_{99}/\xi$. Read off the plot, $\eta_{99}\approx3.5$; the computed value is 3.47. Then

$$
\delta_{99}=\eta_{99}\,\xi=\eta_{99}\sqrt{\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}}=\eta_{99}\sqrt2\,\frac{x}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{\delta_{99}}{x}=\frac{4.91}{\sqrt{Re_x}}}\quad(\text{with }\eta_{99}=3.5:\ 4.95\approx5.0).
$$

> [!important] Know where it comes from, and its limits
> Reading $\eta_{99}=3.5$ off the plot gives $\delta_{99}/x=5.0/\sqrt{Re_x}$, familiar from Part 1 thermofluids and Part 2 aerodynamics. It comes from Blasius, so it holds only for a laminar layer with zero pressure gradient. It does not work on a body that cannot be treated as a flat plate, or in a turbulent layer.

### Integral type A

For large $\eta$, $f'\to1$ and $f$ grows linearly: $f\to\eta-1.217$ (the dotted asymptote in panel b of the Blasius figure).

$$
\eta^*\equiv\int_0^\infty(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta=\big[\eta-f\big]_0^\infty=\lim_{\eta\to\infty}\left[\eta-(\eta-1.217)\right]-\left[0-f(0)\right]=1.217.
$$

The displacement thickness follows by changing variable, $\mathrm dy=\xi\,\mathrm d\eta$:

$$
\delta^*=\int_0^\infty\left(1-\frac{u}{U_e}\right)\mathrm dy=\xi\int_0^\infty(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta=\xi\eta^*=1.217\sqrt{\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}},
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\delta^*}{x}=\frac{1.721}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.}
$$

### Integral type B

$$
\theta^*\equiv\int_0^\infty f'(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta.
$$

**Integrate by parts**, with $u=(1-f')$ and $\mathrm dv=f'\,\mathrm d\eta$, so $v=f$ and $\mathrm du=-f''\,\mathrm d\eta$:

$$
\theta^*=\big[f(1-f')\big]_0^\infty+\int_0^\infty ff''\,\mathrm d\eta.
$$

**Use the Blasius equation**, $ff''=-f'''$:

$$
\theta^*=\big[f(1-f')\big]_0^\infty-\int_0^\infty f'''\,\mathrm d\eta=\big[f(1-f')\big]_0^\infty-\big[f''\big]_0^\infty.
$$

**Evaluate the limits.**

- At $\eta=0$: $f(0)=0$, so $f(1-f')=0$.
- As $\eta\to\infty$: $f\sim\eta$ grows but $1-f'$ decays faster than any power (it decays like a Gaussian; writing $1-f'=c_1/\eta^2+\dots$ is enough to make the point). So $f(1-f')\to0$.
- $f''(\infty)=0$ (the shear dies out).

$$
\theta^*=0-0-\left[0-f''(0)\right]=f''(0)=0.4696.
$$

**The limit at infinity.** Since $f\to\eta-1.217$, write the far field as a series in $1/\eta$:

$$
f=\eta-1.217+\frac{c_1}{\eta}+\frac{c_2}{\eta^2}+\frac{c_3}{\eta^3}+\dots
$$

Differentiate term by term:

$$
f'=1-\frac{c_1}{\eta^2}-\frac{2c_2}{\eta^3}-\frac{3c_3}{\eta^4}-\dots
$$

Subtract from 1:

$$
1-f'=\frac{c_1}{\eta^2}+\frac{2c_2}{\eta^3}+\frac{3c_3}{\eta^4}+\dots
$$

Multiply by $f$. The leading term is $\eta\cdot c_1/\eta^2$:

$$
f(1-f')=\left(\eta-1.217+\dots\right)\left(\frac{c_1}{\eta^2}+\frac{2c_2}{\eta^3}+\dots\right)=\frac{c_1}{\eta}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\eta^2}\right).
$$

Every term has more powers of $\eta$ below than above, so

$$
\lim_{\eta\to\infty}f(1-f')=0.
$$

> [!check] Computed
> $f(1-f')=0.0556$, $0.0062$, $2.4\times10^{-4}$ and $3.4\times10^{-6}$ at $\eta=3$, 4, 5 and 6. It vanishes much faster than $c_1/\eta$, because $1-f'$ really decays like a Gaussian: in a power series every $c_i$ is zero. The series argument is still enough to prove the limit.

The momentum integral equals the wall shear parameter: no extra integration is needed. Then

$$
\theta=\xi\theta^*=0.4696\sqrt{\frac{2\nu x}{U_e}},
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\theta}{x}=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}}},
\qquad
\boxed{H=\frac{\eta^*}{\theta^*}=\frac{1.217}{0.4696}=2.59.}
$$

The $\xi$ cancels in $H=\delta^*/\theta=\xi\eta^*/(\xi\theta^*)$, so $H$ is the same at every station: the layer keeps its shape, as similarity requires. This is where the 2.59 quoted in 3.1 comes from. The rounded values give 2.592; unrounded it is $1.21678/0.46960=2.591$.

### Skin friction

$$
C_f=\frac{\tau_w}{\tfrac12\rho U_e^2}=\frac{2\mu}{\rho U_e^2}\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_{0}
=\frac{2\nu}{U_e^2}\,U_ef''(0)\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
=\frac{2\nu}{U_e\xi}f''(0)
=\frac{2\nu}{U_e}\left(\frac{U_e}{2\nu x}\right)^{1/2}f''(0).
$$

Simplify: $\dfrac{2\nu}{U_e}\sqrt{\dfrac{U_e}{2\nu x}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\nu}{U_ex}}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{Re_x}}$, so

$$
\boxed{C_f=\frac{\sqrt2\times0.4696}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}}.}
$$

> [!check] The drag link from 3.1
> $\dfrac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dx}=\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left(0.664\,x^{1/2}\sqrt{\nu/U_e}\right)=\dfrac{0.664}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\nu}{U_ex}}=\dfrac{0.332}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\dfrac{C_f}{2}$ ✓. The numbers agree because $\theta^*=f''(0)$: the same 0.4696 appears in both.

## 6. Generalised laminar BLs: Falkner–Skan

### Where Uₑ = c xᵐ comes from

The 1.3 potential $\Phi=-kz^{m+1}$ is the flow over a wedge of total angle $\beta\pi$ with $\beta=2m/(m+1)$, and its wall speed is a power law in the distance from the apex, $U_e=cx^m$ ([[Wedge and Corner Flows]], [[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli|1.3]]). Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=cmx^{m-1},\qquad\frac{1}{U_e}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{m}{x}.
$$

### Special cases and the wedge angle

Falkner–Skan extends Blasius to pressure gradients. $m>0$ is flow past a wedge, which accelerates the outer flow; $m<0$ is an expansion corner, which decelerates it.

| Case | $m$ | $\beta$ | $U_e$ |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| flat plate (Blasius) | 0 | 0 | constant |
| stagnation point (Hiemenz) | 1 | 1 | $cx$ |
| separation | −0.09043 | −0.19884 | $cx^{-0.09043}$ |

Check: $m=\beta/(2-\beta)=-0.19884/2.19884=-0.09043$ ✓.

> [!warning] What "the wedge angle is $\beta\pi$" measures
> $\beta\pi$ is the included angle of a symmetric wedge, with walls at $\theta=\pm\pi/(m+1)$ ([[Wedge and Corner Flows]]). Each face turns the flow by half of it. For a single wall that turns away from the flow by $\delta_w$ at a convex corner, the fluid fills an angle $\alpha=\pi+\delta_w$. The corner potential $\Phi\propto z^{\pi/\alpha}$ then gives
>
> $$U_e\propto s^{\pi/\alpha-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad m=\frac{\pi}{\pi+\delta_w}-1=-\frac{\delta_w}{\pi+\delta_w}.$$
>
> $$m+1=\frac{\pi}{\pi+\delta_w}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\beta=\frac{2m}{m+1}=-\frac{2\delta_w}{\pi}.$$
>
> So $\delta_w=-\beta\pi/2$. Separation, $\beta=-0.19884$, is a wall turned away by only $0.0994\pi=17.9^\circ$, not $35.8^\circ$. (Check with $\beta=1$: $\delta_w=-90^\circ$, a wall facing the flow, the stagnation point ✓.) Here $U_e$ and $m$ are defined slightly differently from 1.3, where $U_e=k(m+1)s^m$.

### The six-term equation

The pressure-gradient term $U_e\,\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx$ is now non-zero, so the reduced BL equation has six terms:

$$
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\partial y}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2}=\nu\frac{\partial^3\psi}{\partial y^3}+U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}.
$$

### Falkner–Skan variables and their derivatives

$$
\xi=\left[\frac{2\nu x}{(m+1)U_e}\right]^{1/2},\qquad\eta=\frac y\xi,\qquad f=\frac{\psi}{U_e\xi}.
$$

Since $U_e\propto x^m$, $\xi\propto x^{(1-m)/2}$. Differentiate $\xi$ with the product rule:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx}=\left(\frac{2\nu}{m+1}\right)^{1/2}\frac12\left[(xU_e)^{-1/2}-x^{1/2}U_e^{-3/2}\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}\right]
=\left(\frac{2\nu}{m+1}\right)^{1/2}\frac12\left(\frac{x}{U_e}\right)^{1/2}\left[\frac1x-\frac mx\right]
=\frac{\xi}{2x}(1-m).
$$

Then

$$
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}=-\frac{y}{\xi^2}\frac{\xi}{2x}(1-m)=\frac{\eta}{2x}(m-1),
\qquad\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}=\frac1\xi,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{\eta}{2x}(m-1)f',\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\frac1\xi f'.
$$

(With $m=0$ these reduce to the Blasius table ✓.)

### Derivatives of ψ = Ue ξ f

As before, $\psi_y=U_ef'$, $\psi_{yy}=\dfrac{U_e}{\xi}f''$, $\psi_{yyy}=\dfrac{U_e}{\xi^2}f'''$ ($\xi$ and $U_e$ do not depend on $y$).

Now three factors depend on $x$:

$$
\psi_x=\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}\xi f+U_e\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx}f+U_e\xi\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}f'
=U_e\left[\frac mx\xi f+\frac{\xi}{2x}(1-m)f+\frac{\xi\eta}{2x}(m-1)f'\right].
$$

Collect over $\dfrac{U_e\xi}{2x}$: $2m+(1-m)=m+1$, so

$$
\psi_x=\frac{U_e\xi}{2x}\left[(m+1)f+(m-1)\eta f'\right].
$$

Differentiate in $y$ (each $\partial/\partial y$ of a function of $\eta$ gives $1/\xi$; $\partial(\eta f')/\partial y=(f'+\eta f'')/\xi$):

$$
\psi_{xy}=\frac{U_e\xi}{2x}\cdot\frac1\xi\left[(m+1)f'+(m-1)(f'+\eta f'')\right]=\frac{U_e}{2x}\left[2mf'+(m-1)\eta f''\right].
$$

### Substituting into the six-term equation

$$
U_ef'\cdot\frac{U_e}{2x}\left[2mf'+(m-1)\eta f''\right]-\frac{U_e\xi}{2x}\left[(m+1)f+(m-1)\eta f'\right]\frac{U_e}{\xi}f''=\nu\frac{U_e}{\xi^2}f'''+U_e\cdot\frac{U_em}{x}.
$$

### Simplifying the substituted equation

Multiply by $\dfrac{2x}{U_e^2}$:

$$
2mf'^2+(m-1)\eta f'f''-(m+1)ff''-(m-1)\eta f'f''=\frac{2\nu x}{U_e\xi^2}f'''+2m.
$$

The $(m-1)\eta f'f''$ terms cancel. From the definition of $\xi$, $\dfrac{2\nu x}{U_e\xi^2}=m+1$:

$$
2mf'^2-(m+1)ff''=(m+1)f'''+2m.
$$

Rearrange:

$$
(m+1)f'''+(m+1)ff''+2m\left(1-f'^2\right)=0.
$$

### The Falkner–Skan equation

Divide by $(m+1)$:

$$
f'''+ff''+\frac{2m}{m+1}\left(1-f'^2\right)=0,
\qquad
\boxed{f'''+ff''+\beta\left(1-f'^2\right)=0,\quad\beta=\frac{2m}{m+1}.}
$$

Same boundary conditions as Blasius; solved by the guess-and-shooting method. $\beta=0$ recovers Blasius.

The steps are the same as for Blasius, with most of the algebra again left for home study in the Blackboard notes or White. The only new piece is term ⑥:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=cmx^{m-1}=cx^m\cdot\frac mx=U_e\frac mx,
$$

$$
U_e\frac{\mathrm dU_e}{\mathrm dx}=U_e^2\frac mx.
$$

### Thicknesses and friction for any β

With $\mathrm dy=\xi\,\mathrm d\eta$, exactly as for Blasius:

$$
\delta^*=\xi\eta^*(\beta),\qquad\theta=\xi\theta^*(\beta),\qquad C_f=\frac{2\nu}{U_e\xi}f''(0;\beta).
$$

The $\theta^*=f''(0)$ shortcut does not carry over: integrating by parts now leaves a $\beta\int(1-f'^2)$ term. Read $\eta^*$ and $\theta^*$ from the table. Since $\xi=x\sqrt{2/(m+1)}/\sqrt{Re_x}$ with $Re_x=U_e(x)x/\nu$:

$$
\frac{\delta^*}{x}=\frac{\eta^*\sqrt{2/(m+1)}}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad
\frac{\theta}{x}=\frac{\theta^*\sqrt{2/(m+1)}}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad
C_f\sqrt{Re_x}=2f''(0)\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{2}}.
$$

In an accelerating flow ($\beta>0$) the wall shear is larger, the layer thinner and $H$ lower, because the favourable pressure gradient pushes the slow near-wall fluid along. In a decelerating flow ($\beta<0$) the wall shear falls, and at $\beta=-0.19884$, a wall turned away from the flow by only $0.0994\pi\approx18^\circ$, $\partial u/\partial y|_0=0$ and the layer is about to separate. A laminar layer only tolerates a very mild adverse gradient.

### What β means physically: a boundary layer over a bump

A flat plate leading into a bump, like the curved upper surface of an aerofoil, passes through four stages:
1. On the flat part upstream, $U_e$ is constant: Blasius, $\beta=0$.
2. Up the front of the bump the outer flow accelerates, $\mathrm dU_e/\mathrm dx>0$. This is a positive-$\beta$ profile: fuller, with more wall shear.
3. Past the crest the flow rolls off the bump and decelerates, so $\beta<0$ and the profiles empty.
4. At $\beta=-0.1988$ the wall shear is zero: this is the separation point. Further downstream the flow reverses, and no similarity solution exists.

Blasius covers only the flat plate; Falkner–Skan represents the boundary layer over a curved surface, up to separation.

![[aa_falkner_skan_bump.png|900]]

> [!warning] A picture, not a method
> A bump is not a wedge. Its $U_e(x)$ is not a power law, so the real layer is not exactly any Falkner–Skan profile. Labelling each station with the $\beta$ of its local pressure gradient is a *local-similarity* approximation, and the $\beta$ values in the figure are illustrative. What the picture shows is qualitative: an adverse gradient empties the profile and leads to separation. For a real body, use the integral methods of [[SESA3043 3.3 - Pohlhausen Method and Pressure Gradients|3.3]] and [[SESA3043 3.5 - Momentum Integral Equation|3.5]].

### The effective body

Knowing $\beta$, Falkner–Skan gives $\eta^*$ and hence $\delta^*(x)=\eta^*\xi(x)$. Adding $\delta^*$ to the wedge gives the apparent body that the outer potential flow actually sees. Viscous solution inside, inviscid outside: the idea developed into [[SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction|3.6]] for arbitrary bodies.

Chapter 2 gives the potential flow over the bare wedge; Chapter 3 gives $\delta^*$, hence the effective body, hence where each solution applies. Revision 3.2 Q4 asks for this, worked in [[#7.2 Revision 3.2 Q4: the wedge with β = 0.3|§7.2]]. For an arbitrary body the viscous extension of the Chapter 2 panel method is in [[SESA3043 3.6 - Viscous-Inviscid Interaction|3.6]].

## 7. Worked examples

### 7.1 δ99 near the front stagnation point of a cylinder

Find $\delta_{99}$ near the front stagnation point of a circular cylinder in a stream $U_\infty=30$ m/s, with radius $R=0.1$ m and $\nu=1.46\times10^{-5}$ m²/s. This is the model Falkner–Skan exam question: identify $\beta$, read what you need from the table, then do the arithmetic.

The cylinder is where Chapter 1's potential flow and Chapter 3's viscous solutions meet. Potential flow gives a streamline pattern that closes up behind the cylinder, which a real flow never does; the real layer separates and leaves a wake. Near the front, though, the layer is thin and attached, and potential flow supplies the edge velocity that Falkner–Skan needs. Measure $x$ along the surface from the forward stagnation point, in the direction the layer grows, and $\theta$ as the angle round the cylinder from the stagnation point, so $x=R\theta$.

The surface speed from potential flow ([[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli|1.3]]) is the edge velocity just outside the layer:

$$
U_e(x)=2U_\infty\sin\theta .
$$

Close to the stagnation point $\theta$ is small, so $\sin\theta\approx\theta=x/R$ (radians), and

$$
U_e(x)\approx\frac{2U_\infty}{R}\,x .
$$

This has the Falkner–Skan power-law form $U_e=cx^m$ with

$$
c=\frac{2U_\infty}{R}=\frac{2\times30}{0.1}=600\ \text{s}^{-1},\qquad m=1 ,
$$

so

$$
\beta=\frac{2m}{m+1}=\frac{2}{2}=1 ,
$$

the stagnation-point flow. In the $\beta=1.0$ column of White's Falkner–Skan table, $f'=u/U_e$ first reaches 0.99 at $\eta=2.4$, where it is 0.99055:

$$
\eta_{99}=\frac{y_{99}}{\xi}=\frac{\delta_{99}}{\xi}=2.4 .
$$

The Falkner–Skan length scale is $\xi=\sqrt{2\nu x/[(m+1)U_e]}$. Substitute $m=1$ and $U_e=2U_\infty x/R$:

$$
\delta_{99}=\eta_{99}\,\xi=\eta_{99}\sqrt{\frac{2\nu x}{(m+1)U_e(x)}}
$$

$$
=\eta_{99}\sqrt{\frac{2\nu xR}{(1+1)\,2U_\infty x}}
$$

$$
=\eta_{99}\sqrt{\frac{\nu R}{2U_\infty}} .
$$

Then the numbers:

$$
\sqrt{\frac{1.46\times10^{-5}\times0.1}{2\times30}}=\sqrt{2.433\times10^{-8}}=1.560\times10^{-4}\ \text{m},
$$

$$
\delta_{99}=2.4\times1.560\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=\boxed{0.374\ \text{mm}}
$$

which is the course's answer. The tabulated grid is coarse: interpolating the table, or shooting, puts $f'=0.99$ at $\eta_{99}=2.38$, giving 0.371 mm.

The $x$ cancelled. For $m=1$, $\xi=\sqrt{\nu/c}$ does not depend on $x$, so the layer has constant thickness near a stagnation point. Flow arrives along the stagnation streamline and is spread sideways as fast as viscosity thickens it, so the layer starts with a finite thickness, unlike the flat plate's $\delta=0$ at the leading edge. From the same column, $\delta^*=\eta^*\xi=0.648\xi=0.101$ mm and $\theta=\theta^*\xi=0.292\xi=0.046$ mm.

> [!check] Numbers (computed)
> $\xi=1.560\times10^{-4}$ m; $\delta_{99}=0.374$ mm with the table's $\eta_{99}=2.4$ and 0.371 mm with the shooting value 2.38; $\delta^*=0.101$ mm, $\theta=0.046$ mm, $H=\eta^*/\theta^*=0.64790/0.29235=2.216$, well below the flat plate's 2.59, as a strongly accelerating flow should be.

### Where the similarity solutions sit

The chapter's equations form a hierarchy. The 3D compressible Navier–Stokes equations have six unknowns ($\rho,u,v,w,p,E_T$) and five PDEs; making the flow incompressible removes $\rho$ and the energy equation, and making it 2D removes $w$. The 2D incompressible boundary-layer equations are two parabolic PDEs (continuity and $x$-momentum) in $u$ and $v$ alone, because $p$ (or $U_e$) is handed down from the potential flow. A similarity solution goes one step further: Blasius and Falkner–Skan replace $u$ and $v$ by one unknown, $f(\eta)$, satisfying one third-order ODE solved by the guess-and-shooting method. The formulae to know by heart are the Blasius and Falkner–Skan equations and variables, $\delta^*=\eta^*\xi$ with $\eta^*=\int_0^\infty(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta$, and $\theta=\theta^*\xi$ with $\theta^*=\int_0^\infty f'(1-f')\,\mathrm d\eta$. The last two lines are the ones that connect the ODE solution to the thicknesses of a real laminar layer.

### 7.2 Revision 3.2 Q4: the wedge with β = 0.3

$m=\beta/(2-\beta)=0.3/1.7=0.1765$ (a $54^\circ$ wedge). From the table: $\eta^*=0.9110$, $\theta^*=0.3857$. $\sqrt{2/(m+1)}=\sqrt{2/1.1765}=1.3038$:

$$
\frac{\delta^*}{x}=\frac{0.9110\times1.3038}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\frac{1.188}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad
\frac{\theta}{x}=\frac{0.3857\times1.3038}{\sqrt{Re_x}}=\frac{0.503}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad H=2.36.
$$

**As a function of distance along the wall:** $Re_x=cx^{m+1}/\nu$, so

$$
\delta^*=1.188\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{c}}\,x^{(1-m)/2}=1.188\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{c}}\,x^{0.412},\qquad\theta=0.503\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{c}}\,x^{0.412}.
$$

The layer grows more slowly than on a flat plate ($x^{0.41}$ against $x^{0.5}$) because the accelerating flow keeps thinning it.

## 8. Common mistakes

1. Mixing the two Blasius scalings: $f''(0)=0.332$ belongs to $f'''+\tfrac12ff''=0$; this module uses 0.4696.
2. Forgetting $(m+1)$ inside $\xi$ for Falkner–Skan, which changes every thickness by $\sqrt{2/(m+1)}$.
3. Using $\theta^*=f''(0)$ for $\beta\neq0$: it holds only for Blasius.
4. Taking $Re_x$ with $U_\infty$ instead of the local $U_e(x)$.
5. Confusing $\beta$ (wedge angle $/\pi$) with $m$ (velocity exponent); $\beta=2m/(m+1)$.
6. Taking $\beta\pi$ as the turning angle of a single wall: it is the included angle of a symmetric wedge, so one wall turns by $\beta\pi/2$ ([[#Special cases and the wedge angle]]).
