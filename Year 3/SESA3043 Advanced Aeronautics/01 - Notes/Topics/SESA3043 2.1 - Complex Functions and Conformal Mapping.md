# SESA3043 2.1 - Complex Functions and Conformal Mapping

> [!abstract] Summary
> Any analytic function of $z=x+iy$ has harmonic real and imaginary parts, so a complex potential $\Phi(z)$ is a ready-made potential flow. A **conformal mapping** $z=f(\bar z)$ carries a known flow in one plane into a new flow in another, preserving angles wherever $\mathrm df/\mathrm d\bar z\neq0$. Because a harmonic function stays harmonic under such a map, the recipe is: take the cylinder flow $\Phi=U_\infty(\bar z+a^2/\bar z)$, push its geometry and its potential through $f$, and read off the new flow. The **Joukowski map** $z=\bar z+b^2/\bar z$ turns a circle of radius $a=b$ into a flat plate of length $4b$; a larger circle into an ellipse; a circle shifted left (by $\varepsilon b$) into a symmetric aerofoil; and shifted left and up (angle $\beta$) into a cambered aerofoil. Requiring smooth flow off the sharp trailing edge (the **Kutta condition**) fixes the circulation at $\Gamma=4\pi aU_\infty\sin(\alpha+\beta)$, so $C_L=8\pi(a/c)\sin(\alpha+\beta)$, which is above $2\pi\alpha$ because of thickness, with $\alpha_{L=0}=-\beta$. The Joukowski trailing edge is cusped (zero angle); the **Kármán–Trefftz** map gives a finite trailing-edge angle $\tau$ and is the basis of the coursework.

## The whole topic in one physical picture

Imagine the flow round a cylinder drawn on a rubber sheet. Stretch the sheet, without tearing it and without twisting any small square out of shape, and the circle becomes an aerofoil. The streamlines go along for the ride and stay streamlines. They remain smooth, they still meet the equipotentials at right angles, and they still satisfy Laplace's equation. The only thing that changes is the *speed*, by the local stretch factor $|\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z|$. That is conformal mapping: solve the problem once for the cylinder, and get a whole family of shapes for free.

The sharp trailing edge is where the rubber sheet is *folded* rather than stretched ($\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z=0$). There the speed blows up unless the cylinder flow happens to have a stagnation point at exactly that spot. Choosing the circulation to put it there is the Kutta condition, and it is what gives an aerofoil its lift.

## 1. Complex functions (slide 4)

A complex function $f(z)=g(x,y)+ih(x,y)$ of $z=x+iy$ is **differentiable** (analytic) if its derivative does not depend on the direction from which $\Delta z\to0$. The [[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli#Why the derivative is u − iv (derivation)|derivation in 1.3]] shows this requires the Cauchy–Riemann conditions

$$
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial y},\qquad\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}=-\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}.
$$

The ordinary rules of calculus then carry over: $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}z^n=nz^{n-1}$, $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}\ln z=\dfrac1z$, $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}e^z=e^z$, linearity, the chain rule. Polar form: $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)$ (in the correct quadrant).

**Complex velocity potential** (slide 5): $\Phi=\phi+i\psi$, with $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz=u-iv$. See [[Complex Velocity Potential]]. The lecture stressed the minus sign: *"it's not a typo, it is indeed $u-iv$"* (L-a, l. 189). Showing it is a revision question set in the lecture (L-a, ll. 183–191; solved in [[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli#Why the derivative is u − iv (derivation)|1.3]]). Why it works: $\phi$ and $\psi$ satisfy Cauchy–Riemann by construction, since $u=\partial\phi/\partial x=\partial\psi/\partial y$ and $v=\partial\phi/\partial y=-\partial\psi/\partial x$, so $\Phi$ is analytic (L-a, ll. 175–181).

> [!warning] Slide typo (slide 5)
> The Cauchy–Riemann line reads $\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y}=u$, $\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}=-\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}=v$. The second function is $\psi$, and the second relation is $\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}=-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=v$.

## 2. Mappings and conformality (slides 6–8)

A complex function $w=f(z)$ links **two complex planes**: every point $z$ has an image $w$. Real functions map numbers to numbers; complex functions map *regions to regions*.

![[aa_conformal_mapping_family.png|900]]

### Why an analytic map preserves angles (derivation)

Take a point $\bar z_0$ and two short curves leaving it in directions $\mathrm d\bar z_1$ and $\mathrm d\bar z_2$. Under $z=f(\bar z)$, to first order,

$$
\mathrm dz_k=f'(\bar z_0)\,\mathrm d\bar z_k,\qquad k=1,2.
$$

Write $f'(\bar z_0)=Me^{i\lambda}$. Multiplying a complex number by $Me^{i\lambda}$ **stretches** it by $M$ and **rotates** it by $\lambda$. Both directions are rotated by the same $\lambda$, so the angle between them is unchanged:

$$
\arg\mathrm dz_2-\arg\mathrm dz_1=(\arg\mathrm d\bar z_2+\lambda)-(\arg\mathrm d\bar z_1+\lambda)=\arg\mathrm d\bar z_2-\arg\mathrm d\bar z_1.\ \checkmark
$$

This fails only where $f'=0$ (then there is no well-defined rotation). Those **critical points** are where corners can be created: the Joukowski map has $f'(\bar z)=1-b^2/\bar z^2=0$ at $\bar z=\pm b$, the red crosses in the figure.

> [!tip] Why that means "free" potential flows
> If $\Phi(\bar z)$ is analytic and $\bar z=f^{-1}(z)$ is analytic, then $\Phi(f^{-1}(z))$ is an analytic function of $z$: a composition of analytic functions. So its real and imaginary parts are harmonic in the $z$-plane. Laplace's equation survives the mapping automatically.

### What happens to streamlines and velocities

- **Streamlines map to streamlines.** $\psi$ is just carried along: a point with $\psi=\psi_0$ in $\bar z$ maps to a point with $\psi=\psi_0$ in $z$. In particular a body that is a streamline in $\bar z$ maps to a body that is a streamline in $z$. Flow tangency is automatic.
- **Velocities transform with the chain rule:**

$$
u-iv=\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm d\bar z}\,\frac{\mathrm d\bar z}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm d\bar z}{\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z}.
$$

  Speeds are divided by the local stretch factor $|\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z|$.
- **Circulation and source strength are unchanged.** They are line integrals of $\mathrm d\Phi$ round a closed curve, and $\oint\mathrm d\Phi$ is the same whichever plane you evaluate it in.
- **The far field.** If $z\approx\bar z$ far away (true for Joukowski), the free stream is the same in both planes.

> [!tip] Where else this is used (L-a, ll. 249–256)
> Conformal maps keep a grid **orthogonal**, which is one way to generate good CFD meshes. The lecturer linked this to individual projects that involve CFD.

### The five-step recipe (slide 8)

1. Start from a **known** complex potential in the $\bar z$-plane (usually the cylinder).
2. Find a **transfer function** $z=f(\bar z)$ that turns the circle into the wanted body.
3. **Transform the geometry** to the $z$-plane.
4. **Transform the potential** to the $z$-plane (substitute $\bar z=f^{-1}(z)$ if you can invert it).
5. **Solve**: $u-iv=\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dz$, then $p$ from Bernoulli.

In the lecture (L-a, ll. 269–308): how do you know which body a solution describes? Find the streamline $\psi=0$ (*"Correct, exactly"*, l. 288); the solid surface is a streamline.

> [!question] Two questions from the class
> - **Can one problem use two different maps, e.g. one per quadrant?** (L-a, ll. 310–316.) No: keep one transfer function per problem. A different map turns the same $\bar z$-plane solution into a different body, i.e. a different problem (shown in L-b with the plus-sign map).
> - **Do we have to invent our own transfer functions in the exam?** (L-a, ll. 367–373.) Not required. Any analytic function can be used, and one that produces a useful shape is worth having, but the exam works with the maps given here.

## 3. Flow impinging on a finite plate (slides 9–12; revision 2.1 Q3)

Here every step can be done by hand, which is why the slides use it.

![[aa_plate_impinging_mapping.png|900]]

**Step 1: the known flow.** Cylinder of radius $a$ in the $\bar z$-plane:

$$
\Phi=U_\infty\bar z+\frac{\mu}{2\pi\bar z}=U_\infty\left(\bar z+\frac{a^2}{\bar z}\right),\qquad a=\left(\frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty}\right)^{1/2}.
$$

**Step 2: the map.** $z=\bar z-\dfrac{a^2}{\bar z}$.

**Step 3: the geometry.** On the circle $\bar z=ae^{i\theta}$:

$$
z=ae^{i\theta}-\frac{a^2}{ae^{i\theta}}=a\left(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)=a\left[(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)-(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)\right]=2ia\sin\theta.
$$

So $x=0$ and $y=2a\sin\theta$: the circle becomes the segment $-2a\le y\le2a$ of the $y$-axis, a plate **normal** to the stream. ($\theta=0,\pi\Rightarrow z=0$; $\theta=\pm\pi/2\Rightarrow z=\pm2ia$.)

> [!warning] Slide typo (slide 10), confirmed in the lecture
> The middle line reads $a(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)-a(\cos\theta\ i\sin\theta)$: a sign is missing in the second bracket. The lecturer spotted it live, *"I lost the plus sign there"* (L-a, l. 349): written as $\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)$ it needs a $+$; simplified, it is $e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta$. The slide's "Note $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+\sin\theta$" is also missing an $i$.

**Step 4: invert the map.** Multiply $z=\bar z-a^2/\bar z$ by $\bar z$:

$$
\bar z^2-z\bar z-a^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bar z=\frac{z\pm\sqrt{z^2+4a^2}}{2},
$$

taking the root with $|\bar z|\ge a$ (outside the cylinder).

**Step 5: transform the potential.** The trick on slide 11 (*"we add a $\bar z$, we take away a $\bar z$"*, L-a, ll. 378–386) is to write the potential in terms of $z$ *before* substituting:

$$
\bar z+\frac{a^2}{\bar z}=2\bar z-\left(\bar z-\frac{a^2}{\bar z}\right)=2\bar z-z,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\Phi=U_\infty(2\bar z-z)=U_\infty\left(z\pm\sqrt{z^2+4a^2}-z\right)=\pm U_\infty\sqrt{z^2+4a^2}.
$$

The sign is $+$ for $x\ge0$ and $-$ for $x<0$ (the side the flow comes from). Check far away: $\sqrt{z^2+4a^2}\approx z$, so $\Phi\approx U_\infty z$ on the right, a uniform stream ✓.

**Step 6: on the plate**, $z=iy$ with $|y|\le2a$. The lecture did only this surface case, because the surface pressure is what gives the force (L-a, ll. 392–408). $z^2=(iy)^2=-y^2$, so $z^2+4a^2=4a^2-y^2\ge0$ and

$$
\Phi=\pm U_\infty\sqrt{4a^2-y^2}\quad\text{is real}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\psi=0,\ \ \phi=\pm U_\infty\sqrt{4a^2-y^2}.
$$

$\psi=0$ confirms the plate is a streamline. The velocity along it:

$$
u=0\ (\text{flow is along the plate}),\qquad v=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}=\pm U_\infty\cdot\frac{-y}{\sqrt{4a^2-y^2}}=\mp\frac{U_\infty y}{\sqrt{4a^2-y^2}}.
$$

**Step 7: pressure.** Bernoulli from the free stream:

$$
C_p=\frac{p-p_\infty}{\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2}=1-\frac{u^2+v^2}{U_\infty^2}=\boxed{1-\frac{y^2}{4a^2-y^2}}.
$$

- $y=0$: $C_p=1$, the stagnation point.
- $C_p=0$ where $y^2=4a^2-y^2$, i.e. $y=\pm\sqrt2\,a$ ($\pm71\%$ of the half-width).
- $y\to\pm2a$: $C_p\to-\infty$. Potential flow cannot turn a sharp edge without infinite speed. A real fluid separates there instead, which is why a plate normal to a stream has a large drag ($C_D\approx2$) that potential flow cannot predict.

### Flow along a plate, and an ellipse (slides 13–14)

The same map with a **plus** sign, $z=\bar z+b^2/\bar z$, applied to a circle of radius $a$:

$$
z=ae^{i\theta}+\frac{b^2}{a}e^{-i\theta}=\left(a+\frac{b^2}{a}\right)\cos\theta+i\left(a-\frac{b^2}{a}\right)\sin\theta.
$$

- $a=b$: $z=2b\cos\theta$, a **flat plate** from $-2b$ to $2b$ along $x$ (zero thickness).
- $a>b$: an **ellipse** with semi-axes $a+b^2/a$ and $a-b^2/a$ (slide 14's "$b<a$").

> [!note] Slide 13 labels
> The text says radius $a$ and plate $(-2a,2a)$, but the sketch labels $a=b$ and $(-2b,2b)$. They agree because $a=b$ here. Keep $b$ as the map constant and $a$ as the circle radius from now on.

![[aa_plate_parallel_mapping.png|900]]

**The flow along the plate** ($a=b$; set as *"you can try and work out afterwards"*, L-b, l. 68). This was the class's first answer in the lecture (ll. 37–64).

1. **Geometry.** On $\bar z=ae^{i\theta}$:

$$
z=ae^{i\theta}+\frac{a^2}{ae^{i\theta}}=a(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)+a(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)=2a\cos\theta.
$$

   The imaginary parts cancel, so the plate lies on the real axis, $-2a\le x\le2a$ (L-b, ll. 23–35).

2. **Solution.** The cylinder potential is $\Phi=U_\infty\left(\bar z+a^2/\bar z\right)$, and the bracket **is** the map:

$$
\Phi=U_\infty\left(\bar z+\frac{a^2}{\bar z}\right)=U_\infty z.
$$

   No inversion is needed, unlike the impinging case (L-b, ll. 38–43).

3. **Velocity.** $\dfrac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=U_\infty=u-iv$, so $u=U_\infty$ and $v=0$ everywhere (ll. 45–46).

4. **Pressure.** $C_p=1-\dfrac{u^2+v^2}{U_\infty^2}=1-1=0$ on both faces. *"Zero. Exactly."* (ll. 57–62).

Physically, a zero-thickness plate parallel to an inviscid stream changes nothing, because potential flow slips along it (ll. 47–54).

> [!tip] Why bother with a result that does nothing? (L-b, ll. 69–98)
> It is the seed of every aerofoil in this chapter. The circle's points $\bar z=\pm a$ map to the plate's two **sharp** ends, because they are exactly the critical points $\bar z=\pm b$ of the map. Read the plate as a zero-thickness, zero-camber aerofoil with its LE at $-2a$ and TE at $+2a$. Adding thickness and camber means moving the circle, keeping it through $\bar z=+b$ (sharp TE) and away from $\bar z=-b$ (round LE). Panel (5) and §4 below show the result.

## 4. The Joukowski aerofoil (slides 15–18)

![[aa_joukowski_aerofoil.png|900]]

### Building the shape

The map $z=\bar z+b^2/\bar z$ has critical points at $\bar z=\pm b$. A circle that **passes through** $\bar z=b$ and **encloses** $\bar z=-b$ maps to a closed curve with a sharp corner (in fact a cusp) at $z=2b$: the **trailing edge**.

- Circle centred at the origin with $a=b$: flat plate (sharp at both ends).
- Shift the centre **left** to $\bar z_0=-\varepsilon b$, keeping it through $\bar z=b$: the left end becomes blunt (rounded LE), the right end stays sharp: a **symmetric aerofoil**. $\varepsilon$ controls thickness.
- Shift the centre **up** as well, $\bar z_0=-\varepsilon b+ia\sin\beta$: the aerofoil becomes asymmetric, i.e. **cambered**. $\beta$ controls camber.

The lecture built this in three steps (L-b, ll. 104–245):

1. **Ellipse** ($b<a$, circle centred): thickness appears, but the shape is symmetric **front and back**, with both ends blunt (ll. 104–168). The ends are sharp only where the circle passes through a critical point $\bar z=\pm b$.
2. **Shift left** by $\varepsilon b$ so the circle passes through $\bar z=+b$ but encloses $\bar z=-b$: sharp TE, blunt LE, still top–bottom symmetric (ll. 169–227).
3. **Shift up** by the angle $\beta$: the upper and lower arcs of the circle differ, so the aerofoil gets camber. *"Generating the camber is easier to understand"* (ll. 218–245).

**Geometry relation** (slide 16). The centre is at horizontal distance $b+\varepsilon b$ from the TE point $\bar z=b$, and the radius from the centre to $\bar z=b$ makes the angle $\beta$ with the real axis:

$$
\boxed{a\cos\beta=b(1+\varepsilon)},\qquad\bar z_0=-\varepsilon b+ia\sin\beta,\qquad\bar z=ae^{i\theta}+\bar z_0.
$$

### Chord length (derivation; revision 2.1 Q4)

Take $\beta=0$ (camber changes the chord only at second order). Then $a=b(1+\varepsilon)$.

**Trailing edge:** $\bar z=b$ maps to $z=b+b^2/b=2b$.

**Leading edge:** the point of the circle furthest left, $\bar z=-\varepsilon b-a=-b(1+2\varepsilon)$, maps to

$$
z_{LE}=-b(1+2\varepsilon)+\frac{b^2}{-b(1+2\varepsilon)}=-b\left(1+2\varepsilon+\frac{1}{1+2\varepsilon}\right).
$$

**Chord:**

$$
c=z_{TE}-z_{LE}=2b+b\left(1+2\varepsilon+\frac{1}{1+2\varepsilon}\right).
$$

Put everything over $1+2\varepsilon$:

$$
c=b\,\frac{(3+2\varepsilon)(1+2\varepsilon)+1}{1+2\varepsilon}=b\,\frac{4+8\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^2}{1+2\varepsilon}=\frac{4b(1+\varepsilon)^2}{1+2\varepsilon}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac bc=\frac{1+2\varepsilon}{4(1+\varepsilon)^2}}\qquad\text{and for small }\varepsilon,\ c\approx4b\ \ (\text{error }O(\varepsilon^2)).
$$

*Check:* $\varepsilon=0.1$ gives $b/c=1.2/4.84=0.2479$, matching the generator (chord $=1.0000$ with $b=0.2479$) ✓.

In the lecture the chord came from the same geometry, $a\cos\beta=b+\varepsilon b$ (L-b, ll. 300–317), and was then approximated as *"about four times the transform constant"* for small $\varepsilon$ and $\beta$ (l. 321).

### The flow and the Kutta condition (slide 17)

In the $\bar z$-plane: a cylinder centred at $\bar z_0$, in a stream at incidence $\alpha$, with circulation $\Gamma$ (clockwise positive here):

$$
\Phi=U_\infty(\bar z-\bar z_0)e^{-i\alpha}+\frac{U_\infty a^2e^{i\alpha}}{\bar z-\bar z_0}+\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln(\bar z-\bar z_0).
$$

Each term: uniform stream at angle $\alpha$ (rotated by $e^{-i\alpha}$); a doublet aligned with it (the circle theorem keeps $|\bar z-\bar z_0|=a$ a streamline); a vortex at the centre.

> [!note] Slide 17 drops the shift
> The slide writes $\Phi=U_\infty\bar ze^{-i\alpha}+U_\infty a^2e^{i\alpha}/\bar z+i\Gamma\ln\bar z/2\pi$, i.e. a circle centred at the origin. For the offset circle every $\bar z$ must be $\bar z-\bar z_0$; the MATLAB code on slide 18 does this implicitly by parametrising $\theta$ about the centre.

**Velocity in the $\bar z$-plane:**

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm d\bar z}=U_\infty e^{-i\alpha}-\frac{U_\infty a^2e^{i\alpha}}{(\bar z-\bar z_0)^2}+\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi(\bar z-\bar z_0)}.
$$

**The TE problem.** In the $z$-plane, $u-iv=\dfrac{\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm d\bar z}{\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z}$, and $\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z=1-b^2/\bar z^2=0$ at $\bar z=b$. The speed at the TE is infinite **unless** $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm d\bar z=0$ there too. That is the **Kutta condition**: flow leaves the sharp TE smoothly (see panels b and c).

**Solve for Γ (derivation).** At the TE, $\bar z-\bar z_0=ae^{-i\beta}$ (the TE is at angle $-\beta$ seen from the centre). Substitute:

$$
0=U_\infty e^{-i\alpha}-\frac{U_\infty a^2e^{i\alpha}}{a^2e^{-2i\beta}}+\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi ae^{-i\beta}}
=U_\infty e^{-i\alpha}-U_\infty e^{i(\alpha+2\beta)}+\frac{i\Gamma e^{i\beta}}{2\pi a}.
$$

Multiply through by $e^{-i\beta}$:

$$
0=U_\infty\left(e^{-i(\alpha+\beta)}-e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\right)+\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi a}=-2iU_\infty\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi a}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\Gamma=4\pi aU_\infty\sin(\alpha+\beta)}
$$

**Lift.** By [[Kutta-Joukowski Theorem|Kutta–Joukowski]] (circulation is the same in both planes):

$$
L'=\rho U_\infty\Gamma,\qquad C_L=\frac{L'}{\tfrac12\rho U_\infty^2c}=\frac{2\Gamma}{U_\infty c}=\boxed{\frac{8\pi a}{c}\sin(\alpha+\beta)}.
$$

Reading the result:

- **Zero-lift angle** $\alpha_{L=0}=-\beta$: camber shifts the lift curve left, as in thin-aerofoil theory.
- **Lift slope** $8\pi a/c$. With $a\approx b(1+\varepsilon)$ and $c\approx4b(1+\varepsilon)^2/(1+2\varepsilon)$: slope $\approx2\pi(1+2\varepsilon)/(1+\varepsilon)\approx2\pi(1+\varepsilon)$. Thickness **increases** the inviscid lift slope above $2\pi$ (by about $0.77\,t/c$ in relative terms).
- **Small angles, thin section** ($\varepsilon\to0$): $C_L\to2\pi(\alpha+\beta)$, the thin-aerofoil result with $\alpha_{L=0}=-\beta=-2h/c$ ✓.

> [!example] Numbers (verified in the generator)
> $\varepsilon=0.1$, $\alpha=5^\circ$:
> - symmetric ($\beta=0$): $C_L=2\pi\,\dfrac{1.2}{1.1}\sin5^\circ=0.597$ (thin aerofoil: $0.548$);
> - cambered ($\beta=6^\circ$): $C_L=1.315$; at $\alpha=0$ it still gives $0.720$.

### The Cp distribution (slide 17)

$$
C_p=1-\frac{u^2+v^2}{U_\infty^2}=1-\frac{1}{U_\infty^2}\left|\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}\right|^2.
$$

The lecturer's shortcut (L-b, ll. 327–348): you never need $\Phi$ as a function of $z$. Use the chain rule, $\dfrac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm dz}=\dfrac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm d\bar z}\dfrac{\mathrm d\bar z}{\mathrm dz}$, then take the **modulus** squared for Bernoulli. You don't need $u$ and $v$ separately; one complex modulus gives $C_p$, which is easy in code.

Panel (d) shows the suction peak near the LE, $C_p=+1$ at the front stagnation point, and the pressures on the two surfaces closing to a common value at the TE (Kutta). At the cusped TE, $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm d\bar z$ and $\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z$ both vanish; their ratio is finite (L'Hôpital), so the TE speed is finite but not zero.

### The MATLAB code (slide 18)

The handwritten notes on the slide explain each block. Annotated:

| Line | What it does |

*Check of the surface velocity line.* On the circle $\bar z-\bar z_0=ae^{i\theta}$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Phi}{\mathrm d\bar z}=e^{-i\alpha}-e^{i\alpha}e^{-2i\theta}+\frac{i\cdot4\pi a\sin(\alpha+\beta)}{2\pi ae^{i\theta}}=e^{-i\alpha}\left(1-e^{2i(\alpha-\theta)}\right)+2i\sin(\alpha+\beta)e^{-i\theta}.\ \checkmark
$$

> [!warning] The Joukowski TE is cusped
> Both surfaces meet tangentially at the TE (zero included angle): impossibly thin to build, and numerically awkward. (While validating the panel code of [[SESA3043 2.3 - Panel Methods|2.3]], the doublet panel method converged correctly on symmetric Joukowski and NACA sections but **not** on cambered cusped Joukowski sections: nearly coincident TE panels make the system ill-posed.) Hence the next section.

## 5. The Kármán–Trefftz aerofoil (slides 19–20)

$$
z=2b\,\frac{(\bar z+b)^p+(\bar z-b)^p}{(\bar z+b)^p-(\bar z-b)^p},\qquad p=2-\frac{\tau}{\pi},
$$

where $\tau$ is the **included TE angle** in radians.

The lecture's motivation (L-b, ll. 376–395): the Joukowski TE is sharp but *"too sharp"*. It is a cusp, with zero angle and zero thickness over a finite length, which cannot be built. Kármán and Trefftz changed the map so the TE has a finite angle $\tau$; $\tau=0$ ($p=2$) recovers Joukowski (ll. 412–421). Geometry and solution remain exact, *"just a bit more calculation"* (l. 427).

**Why it works.** Near $\bar z=b$, the map behaves like $(\bar z-b)^p$. A straight angle of $\pi$ (the smooth circle) is turned into an angle $p\pi=2\pi-\tau$ on the outside, i.e. an interior wedge of $\tau$ at the TE. The Joukowski map is the special case $p=2$ ($\tau=0$). Check:

$$
p=2:\quad z=2b\,\frac{2\bar z^2+2b^2}{4b\bar z}=\bar z+\frac{b^2}{\bar z}.\ \checkmark
$$

**Geometry** (slide 19): the same circle construction, $a\cos\beta=b(1+\varepsilon)$, with chord relation

$$
\frac bc=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^p-\varepsilon^p}{4(1+\varepsilon)^p}.
$$

*Derivation:* TE at $\bar z=b$ gives $z=2b$. LE at $\bar z=-b(1+2\varepsilon)$: $\bar z+b=-2b\varepsilon$, $\bar z-b=-2b(1+\varepsilon)$, so $z_{LE}=-2b\,\dfrac{(1+\varepsilon)^p+\varepsilon^p}{(1+\varepsilon)^p-\varepsilon^p}$. Then $c=2b+|z_{LE}|=\dfrac{4b(1+\varepsilon)^p}{(1+\varepsilon)^p-\varepsilon^p}$ ✓. For $p=2$ this reduces to the Joukowski result.

**The flow.** Exactly as for Joukowski, except $\mathrm dz/\mathrm d\bar z$, which is now

$$
\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm d\bar z}=2b\,\frac{p\left[(\bar z+b)^{p-1}+(\bar z-b)^{p-1}\right](A-B)-(A+B)\,p\left[(\bar z+b)^{p-1}-(\bar z-b)^{p-1}\right]}{(A-B)^2},
$$

with $A=(\bar z+b)^p$ and $B=(\bar z-b)^p$ (quotient rule).

> [!warning] Far-field scaling with the slide's $2b$ prefactor
> For large $\bar z$, $A+B\approx2\bar z^p$ and $A-B\approx2pb\,\bar z^{p-1}$, so $z\approx(2/p)\bar z$. Unless $p=2$, the map **rescales** the far field. A free stream $U_\infty$ in the physical plane therefore needs $U_{\bar z}=(2/p)U_\infty$ in the circle plane. (Many textbooks write the KT map with prefactor $pb$ instead, so that $z\approx\bar z$.) I hit this while validating: with the wrong scaling the "exact" $C_L$ came out about 6% low for $\tau=10^\circ$. Check how the provided coursework code normalises before comparing numbers.

## 6. Worked examples

### 6.1 Plate normal to a stream (suggested)

A 2 m plate ($a=0.5$ m) faces a 20 m/s air stream ($\rho=1.225$). Where does the surface pressure equal $p_\infty$, and what is $p-p_\infty$ 0.9 m from the centre?

- $C_p=0$ at $y=\sqrt2\,a=0.707$ m from the centre.
- $y=0.9$ m: $C_p=1-0.81/(1-0.81)=1-4.26=-3.26$, so $p-p_\infty=-3.26\times\tfrac12(1.225)(400)=-799$ Pa.

### 6.2 Joukowski lift (suggested)

$\varepsilon=0.1$, $\beta=6^\circ$, $c=1.5$ m, $U_\infty=40$ m/s, $\alpha=3^\circ$, sea-level air.

- $b=c\times0.2479=0.3719$ m; $a=b(1.1)/\cos6^\circ=0.4113$ m.
- $\Gamma=4\pi(0.4113)(40)\sin9^\circ=32.35$ m²/s.
- $L'=\rho U\Gamma=1.225(40)(32.35)=1585$ N/m; $C_L=2\Gamma/(Uc)=1.078$.
- Thin-aerofoil estimate with $h/c=0.0525$: $C_L\approx2\pi(\alpha+2h/c)=2\pi(0.0524+0.105)=0.989$; thickness adds about 9%.

### 6.3 Mapping practice (suggested)

Under $z=\bar z+b^2/\bar z$, where does the circle $|\bar z|=2b$ go? $z=2be^{i\theta}+\tfrac b2e^{-i\theta}=\tfrac52b\cos\theta+i\tfrac32b\sin\theta$: an ellipse with semi-axes $2.5b$ and $1.5b$.
