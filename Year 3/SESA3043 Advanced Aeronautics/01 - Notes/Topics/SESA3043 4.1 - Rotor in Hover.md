# SESA3043 4.1 - Rotor in Hover

> [!abstract] Summary
> A hovering rotor holds the aircraft up by pushing air down. Actuator disc theory replaces the rotor by a thin disc carrying a uniform pressure jump and applies mass, momentum and Bernoulli to the streamtube through it: the far wake moves at $v_2=2v_i$, the induced velocity is $v_0=\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$ and the ideal induced power is $P_i=Tv_0=T\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$, so a large disc at low disc loading $T/A$ is the efficient way to hover. Splitting the wake into annuli shows that any non-uniform downwash costs extra induced power (the induced power factor $k_i\geq1$). Blade element theory brings in the blades themselves and gives $C_T/sa=\theta_0/3-\lambda_i/2$, the total power coefficient $C_P=C_T\lambda_i+sC_{D0}/4$ and the figure of merit, the ratio of ideal induced power to actual power.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a hovering rotor (helicopter, drone, lift fan) is given its weight and size and the question asks for induced velocity, induced power, $C_T$, power with profile drag, collective pitch or figure of merit.
> - **Assumptions:** steady, incompressible, inviscid flow outside the disc; uniform $v_i$ over the disc and no swirl (actuator disc); for blade elements, small $\phi$, small angle of attack ($C_L=a\alpha$), $U\approx\Omega r$, constant chord, blades to the centre; angles in radians.
> - **Method:** 1. $T=mg$, $A=\pi R^2$, $V_T=\Omega R$. 2. $v_0=\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$, $P_i=Tv_0$. 3. $C_T=T/\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A$, $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$, $C_{P_i}=C_T\lambda_i$. 4. With blades: $s=Nc/\pi R$, $\theta_0=3\left(C_T/sa+\lambda_i/2\right)$, $C_P=C_T\lambda_i+sC_{D0}/4$. 5. $\mathrm{FM}=C_T\lambda_i/C_P$.
> - **Common mistakes:** using the US definition $C_T=T/\rho A(\Omega R)^2$ (no $\tfrac12$), which changes $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$ to $\sqrt{C_T/2}$; leaving $\theta_0$ in degrees in $\theta_0/3-\lambda_i/2$.
> - **Practise:** [[#7. Worked example: Westland Lynx in hover]] · [[#14. Worked example: blade-element estimate for the Lynx]]

The idea of a lifting rotor is far older than the machines, and the rotor was the first flying device people understood well enough to make fly. The Chinese top of more than 2000 years ago is spun by hand (modern toy versions use a launcher instead); its blades push air downwards, so the top rises. Leonardo da Vinci's fifteenth-century helical screw works the same way, but human power alone could never turn it fast enough to push down enough air to lift a person, and it stayed a design that was never built. Sir George Cayley's elastic-driven models did fly, briefly, to about ninety feet.

The modern family runs from search-and-rescue helicopters, which lift people off ships in trouble, and air ambulances, through small multirotor drones used as toys and for fast automated delivery, to electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft. Electric power makes eVTOL attractive for its lower environmental impact (Chapter 5); these aircraft fly low and for short durations, carrying people or cargo, delivering supplies after a disaster such as an earthquake, or fighting fires. All of them hover the same way, and hover, staying stationary in the air, is the manoeuvre that sets rotorcraft apart: a fixed-wing aircraft can hardly do it.

Each blade of a hovering rotor is a wing flying in a circle, and the lift of all the blades together is the rotor thrust $T$. To push up on the aircraft the rotor must push down on the air, so it sets up a column of air, the wake, moving downwards. Air at rest far above is drawn into the disc, speeds up through it and leaves in a jet narrower than the disc. The power spent giving that jet its kinetic energy is the induced power, and it is the minimum power any rotor of that size needs to hover.

The chapter treats hover in the most depth, then axial flight (climb and descent, [[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight|4.2]]) and forward flight ([[SESA3043 4.3 - Rotor in Forward Flight|4.3]]), with three models of increasing detail. Actuator disc theory ignores the blades entirely, whether the rotor has two, three or four of them, and treats the rotor as a disc that adds pressure. It couples inviscid (Bernoulli) flow with momentum theory and gives the induced velocity and the ideal power; simple as it is, it applies well to rotorcraft in many situations and is most useful at the early stages of a design. It is a one-dimensional theory, with uniform flow across the wake. Annular wake division keeps the disc but lets the downwash vary with radius, and shows why a uniform downwash is best. Blade element theory finally puts the blades back in: it chops each blade into segments with chord, pitch, lift slope and drag, works out the forces on each segment and adds them up, giving the thrust and power of a real rotor and its figure of merit. All three are fundamental models rather than design codes, but they give useful results for rotor aerodynamics and rotor design.

## 1. Actuator disc theory: model and assumptions

![[aa_4_1_actuator_disc_hover.png|900]]

Actuator disc theory (ADT) is an elementary method for rotor aerodynamics based on steady, incompressible, irrotational flow. The flow is treated as a potential flow, with no viscosity, so Bernoulli's equation can be used on either side of the disc. ADT does not consider the rotor geometry, the blade pitch or the control angles, so it says nothing about how a rotor makes its thrust, only what the flow must look like once it does. It works well for performance near the rotor's design condition, away from its aerodynamic limits (stall, compressibility at the tips).

Physically, the spinning blades push the air downwards and the air pushes back up on the rotor. ADT replaces the blades, however many there are, by a circular disc of the blade radius $R$, with area $A=\pi R^2$, and represents that exchange of force as a pressure difference across the disc: the static pressure just below it, $p_l$, exceeds the pressure just above it, $p_u$, and the difference acting on $A$ is the rotor thrust $T$, which in hover balances the aircraft's weight. The disc is only a model. A real rotor is a set of blades, and the air passes between them.

Air is drawn in from far above, where it is at rest at the atmospheric pressure $p_\infty$, passes through the disc at the induced velocity $v_i$ and leaves in the wake at $v_2$. The analysis follows only the air inside the streamtube that passes through the edge of the disc. The key parameters are the disc area $A$, the wake area $A_2$, the induced velocity at the rotor $v_i$, the velocity in the wake far downstream $v_2$, the atmospheric static pressure $p_\infty$, the constant atmospheric density $\rho$, and the static pressures just above and just below the disc, $p_u$ and $p_l$. Velocities are positive downwards.

The model rests on six assumptions:

1. the rotor has an infinite number of blades, so the system is totally uniform;
2. the rotor is a constant pressure difference across the disc;
3. the downwash $v_i$ is constant across the disc;
4. the vertical velocity is continuous through the disc (mass conservation through a disc of zero thickness);
5. there is no swirl in the wake: no rotational velocity is given to the air, so all velocities are axial;
6. the flow divides into two distinct regions, the air that passes through the disc and the air outside it. The boundary is the streamtube through the disc's perimeter, and the wake is a uniform jet.

Assumptions 2 and 4 together are the heart of the model. The pressure jumps across the disc, and the velocity does not. Upstream and downstream of the disc, separately, nothing adds energy to the flow, so Bernoulli holds along each streamline above the disc and along each streamline below it, but not across it. This is the same model as the propeller form in [[Actuator Disk Theory]] (SESA2023) with the free-stream speed set to zero.

The flow inside the streamtube is one-dimensional: at any cross-section it is uniform, and it changes only along the axis, so the velocity has a single, axial component. A real rotor's wake is far more complicated, with swirl and a trailing tip vortex from every blade, and ADT leaves all of that out. The one-dimensional picture also explains the shape of the streamtube. The mass flow rate is the same through every cross-section and the density is constant, so where the air moves faster the cross-section must be smaller: the tube narrows from a wide inlet far above, through the disc, to a slender jet below. The arrows in the figure grow in the same way, from almost nothing far upstream to $v_i$ at the disc and $v_2$ in the wake. Every result of ADT follows from this picture together with Bernoulli's equation and a momentum balance; the rest is algebra.

## 2. Rotor thrust from Bernoulli

The disc carries a pressure difference $p_l-p_u$ over area $A$, so the force it exerts on the air, and the reaction on the rotor, is

$$
T=A\left(p_l-p_u\right).
$$

The upstream end of the streamtube is taken so far above the rotor that its cross-section is very large, so by continuity the air there moves very slowly. Bernoulli's equation contains the square of that speed, which makes the term negligible beside $\tfrac12\rho v_i^2$ and $\tfrac12\rho v_2^2$: an inlet of ten times the disc area has $v=v_i/10$ and a dynamic pressure of 1 % of $\tfrac12\rho v_i^2$, and the inlet can be pushed as far up as needed. So the inlet velocity is set to zero.

Above the rotor, Bernoulli applies along a streamline from far upstream, where the pressure is $p_\infty$ and $v\approx0$, to just above the disc, where the speed is $v_i$:

$$
p_\infty+0=p_u+\tfrac12\rho v_i^2.
$$

Below the rotor, Bernoulli applies from just below the disc, where the speed is still $v_i$ (assumption 4), to the far wake, where the jet has reached $v_2$ and its pressure has returned to $p_\infty$ (the jet's boundary is in contact with the surrounding still air):

$$
p_l+\tfrac12\rho v_i^2=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2.
$$

Subtract the first equation from the second:

$$
p_l-p_u+\tfrac12\rho v_i^2=\tfrac12\rho v_2^2+\tfrac12\rho v_i^2-0.
$$

The $\tfrac12\rho v_i^2$ terms cancel:

$$
p_l-p_u=\tfrac12\rho v_2^2.
$$

Multiply by $A$:

$$
\boxed{T=\tfrac12\rho v_2^2A.}
$$

The pressure jump equals the dynamic pressure of the far wake. Panel (c) of the figure plots the static pressure along the axis: it falls below $p_\infty$ as the air accelerates towards the disc (by $\tfrac12\rho v_i^2$ just above it), jumps up by $T/A$ at the disc, then relaxes back to $p_\infty$ as the jet accelerates further below. The velocity in panel (b) rises smoothly through the disc with no jump at all.

This result is not yet usable on its own. The velocity $v_2$ and the area $A_2$ belong to the far wake, where neither is known; the only known area is the disc area $A$.

## 3. Hover induced velocity v0

The second relation comes from mass and momentum. Mass is conserved in the streamtube, so the mass flow rate is the same through every cross-section, and it is evaluated at the disc, where the area is known. The density is constant (incompressible flow), so

$$
\dot m=\rho Av_i.
$$

By Newton's second law applied to the flow, the force on the air equals the rate of change of its momentum flow rate between the ends of the streamtube; this is the integral momentum equation of [[SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations|1.2]] applied to the streamtube. The rate at which momentum leaves the control volume through the bottom of the streamtube is $\dot mv_2=\rho Av_iv_2$ (downwards). The momentum flow rate in at the top is zero, because the air there is at rest. The pressure on the whole boundary of the streamtube far from the disc is $p_\infty$, which gives no net force. The only net force on the air is the reaction of the rotor thrust, so the momentum created per second equals the thrust:

$$
T=\dot mv_2-0=\rho Av_iv_2.
$$

Equate this with the Bernoulli result:

$$
\rho Av_iv_2=\tfrac12\rho Av_2^2.
$$

Divide both sides by $\rho Av_2$ (non-zero for a rotor producing thrust):

$$
v_i=\tfrac12v_2,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{v_2=2v_i.}
$$

Half of the final wake velocity is reached by the time the air arrives at the disc. The result is not special to hover: with a free stream through the disc, the velocity the far wake gains is still twice the velocity gained at the disc, in climb and descent ([[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight#The wake velocity|4.2]]) and in forward flight ([[SESA3043 4.3 - Rotor in Forward Flight#The wake velocity|4.3]]). Substitute $v_2=2v_i$ back into $T=\rho Av_iv_2$:

$$
T=\rho Av_i(2v_i)=2\rho Av_i^2.
$$

Solve for $v_i$:

$$
v_i^2=\frac{T}{2\rho A},
$$

$$
\boxed{v_0\equiv v_i=\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)^{1/2}\qquad\text{(hover, uniform disc).}}
$$

The hover value of the induced velocity is called $v_0$ wherever it is used as a reference, as it is in total-power expressions and throughout axial and forward flight ([[SESA3043 4.2 - Rotor in Axial Flight|4.2]], [[SESA3043 4.3 - Rotor in Forward Flight|4.3]]): $v_0$ is the induced velocity of the same rotor producing the same thrust in hover. In hover itself $v_i=v_0$.

Continuity between the disc and the far wake also fixes how much the jet contracts:

$$
\rho Av_i=\rho A_2v_2=\rho A_2(2v_i),
$$

$$
A_2=\frac A2,\qquad R_2=\frac{R}{\sqrt2}=0.707R.
$$

The fully developed wake is half the disc area, as drawn in panel (a).

> [!tip] Why the air speeds up before it reaches the disc
> Above the disc the pressure is below $p_\infty$ (it is $p_\infty-\tfrac12\rho v_i^2$ just above it), so air from all around is sucked in and accelerated before it ever meets a blade. Below the disc the pressure is above $p_\infty$ and still falling towards it, so the jet keeps accelerating. The disc only adds pressure; the surrounding flow converts it into velocity on both sides.

## 4. Induced power and rotor efficiency

In hover the thrust must balance the weight, $T=mg$, so the weight fixes $T$ and the rotor has to be driven just hard enough to produce that thrust. The rotor produces thrust $T$ by generating the induced velocity $v_i$, so the rate of work it does on the air is force times the velocity of the air at the disc. This is the (ideal) induced power, the power required to maintain the thrust:

$$
P_i=Tv_i.
$$

With $v_i=v_0$ from section 3:

$$
\boxed{P_i=Tv_0=T\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)^{1/2}=\frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A}}.}
$$

The same answer follows from energy. Air enters at rest and leaves at $v_2$, so the kinetic energy given to it per second is

$$
\tfrac12\dot mv_2^2=\tfrac12(\rho Av_i)(2v_i)^2=2\rho Av_i^3.
$$

Using $T=2\rho Av_i^2$,

$$
2\rho Av_i^3=(2\rho Av_i^2)v_i=Tv_i,
$$

which is $P_i$. All of the induced power ends up as kinetic energy in the wake; in this model nothing else absorbs it.

For a required thrust, the less power it takes the better, so the rotor efficiency is defined as the thrust generated per unit power:

$$
\frac{T}{P_i}=\frac{1}{v_i}=\left(\frac{2\rho A}{T}\right)^{1/2}.
$$

The larger the induced velocity, the less efficient the rotor. For a given weight the only free variable is $A$: doubling the rotor radius quadruples $A$, halves $v_i$ and halves the induced power. This is why helicopters have large, slow rotors and why a jet-lift aircraft hovering on a small, fast jet burns so much fuel.

Thrust is a force and power is a force times a velocity, so unlike most efficiencies this one is not dimensionless: it is the reciprocal of a velocity, with units of N/W (or N/kW), and is often called the power loading.

## 5. Disc loading across vehicles

![[aa_4_1_disc_loading.png|900]]

The disc loading $T/A$ is the single parameter that sets the hover induced velocity:

$$
v_0=\sqrt{\frac{T/A}{2\rho}}.
$$

Because $v_0\propto(T/A)^{1/2}$, a hundredfold increase in disc loading raises the induced velocity, and lowers the rotor efficiency, only tenfold, which is why the comparison between vehicles needs a logarithmic scale. In increasing order of induced velocity typical vehicles are the Mil Mi-10 heavy-lift helicopter and the Westland Lynx (both about 10–15 m/s), the Sikorsky S-64 and CH-53E, the Bell–Boeing V-22 Osprey tilt-rotor (about 20 m/s), the Hiller–Ryan XC-142 tilt-wing (about 30 m/s), the Ryan XV-5A lift-fan aircraft (about 80 m/s), the Harrier GR3 jet (about 200 m/s), the Space Shuttle at lift-off (about 450 m/s) and the Saturn V F-1 engine (about 1100 m/s). These values are approximate. Only the first five are rotorcraft; the rest are a tilt-wing, a lift-fan aircraft, a jet and two rocket systems, included for comparison. The helicopters generate their thrust with the least power, and the Space Shuttle and the Saturn V engine are the least efficient, with induced velocities up to about a hundred times the helicopters'. The XV-5A buries its fans in the wing and nose and drives them with the exhaust of two jet engines, so it sits between a rotor and a jet.

The Lynx points on the plot are worked in [[#7. Worked example: Westland Lynx in hover]]: 251 N/m² empty and 406 N/m² at maximum take-off.

## 6. Standard parameters

Non-dimensional parameters do for a rotor what $C_L$ and $C_D$ do for a wing, and they are most useful in design calculations. A wing's coefficients use the dynamic pressure of the oncoming flow and a reference area. A hovering rotor has no oncoming flow, and its key length is the rotor radius, so the reference velocity is the rotor tip velocity $V_T=R\Omega$ ($R$ the rotor radius, $\Omega$ the rotor angular velocity in rad/s) and the reference area is the disc area $A=\pi R^2$:

$$
\lambda_i=\frac{v_i}{V_T}\qquad\text{(non-dimensional downwash velocity, or inflow ratio)},
$$

$$
C_T=\frac{T}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A},\qquad C_P=\frac{P}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A},\qquad C_{P_i}=\frac{P_i}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A}.
$$

The power coefficients carry $V_T^3$ where $C_T$ has $V_T^2$, because power is force times velocity. Both coefficients carry the factor $\tfrac12$, as wing coefficients do. Many textbooks (Leishman, Johnson) leave it out, writing $C_T=T/\rho A(\Omega R)^2$; their $C_T$ is half the one used here, so formulas must not be mixed between the two conventions.

Divide $v_0=\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$ by $V_T$:

$$
\lambda_i=\frac{1}{V_T}\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\frac{T}{2\rho V_T^2A}\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Write the denominator as $4\times\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A$:

$$
\lambda_i=\left(\frac14\cdot\frac{T}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\frac{C_T}{4}\right)^{1/2},
$$

$$
\boxed{\lambda_i=\frac{C_T^{1/2}}{2},\qquad C_T=4\lambda_i^2\qquad\text{(hover, ADT).}}
$$

For the induced power coefficient, divide $P_i=Tv_i$ by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A$ and split one $V_T$ off:

$$
C_{P_i}=\frac{T}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A}\cdot\frac{v_i}{V_T}=C_T\lambda_i,
$$

and with $\lambda_i=C_T^{1/2}/2$,

$$
\boxed{C_{P_i}=C_T\lambda_i=\frac{C_T^{3/2}}{2}.}
$$

The induced power coefficient and the thrust coefficient are linked through the induced velocity alone, in the form of $\lambda_i$. The disc loading is $T/A$, the thrust per unit disc area. In coefficient form it is $T/A=C_T\cdot\tfrac12\rho V_T^2$.

## 7. Worked example: Westland Lynx in hover

> Westland WG13 Lynx in hover. Mass 3291 kg empty, 5330 kg maximum take-off. Main rotor radius $R=6.4$ m, $N=4$ blades, tip speed $V_T=218.69$ m/s. $g=9.81$ m/s², $\rho=1.2256$ kg/m³. Find the thrust and induced power coefficients, and the rotor efficiency.

In hover the thrust balances the weight (the fuselage download and tail-rotor thrust are ignored). The disc area and rotor speed are

$$
A=\pi(6.4)^2=128.68\ \text{m}^2,\qquad\Omega=\frac{V_T}{R}=\frac{218.69}{6.4}=34.17\ \text{rad/s}\ (326\ \text{rpm}).
$$

The dynamic-pressure scale is $\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A=\tfrac12(1.2256)(218.69)^2(128.68)=3.771\times10^6$ N.

At maximum take-off:

$$
T=5330\times9.81=52\,287\ \text{N},\qquad\frac TA=406.3\ \text{N/m}^2,
$$

$$
C_T=\frac{52\,287}{3.771\times10^6}=0.01386,
$$

$$
\lambda_i=\frac{\sqrt{0.01386}}{2}=0.05887,\qquad v_0=\lambda_iV_T=12.88\ \text{m/s},
$$

$$
C_{P_i}=C_T\lambda_i=\frac{C_T^{3/2}}{2}=8.16\times10^{-4},
$$

$$
P_i=Tv_0=52\,287\times12.875=673\ \text{kW},
$$

$$
\frac{T}{P_i}=\frac{1}{v_0}=0.0777\ \text{N/W}=77.7\ \text{N/kW}.
$$

Empty, with the same working:

| | empty | maximum take-off |
|---|---:|---:|
| $T$ (N) | 32 285 | 52 287 |
| $T/A$ (N/m²) | 250.9 | 406.3 |
| $C_T$ | 0.00856 | 0.01386 |
| $\lambda_i$ | 0.0463 | 0.0589 |
| $v_0$ (m/s) | 10.12 | 12.88 |
| $C_{P_i}$ | $3.96\times10^{-4}$ | $8.16\times10^{-4}$ |
| $P_i$ (kW) | 327 | 673 |
| $T/P_i$ (N/kW) | 98.8 | 77.7 |

> [!check] Numbers verified in Python
> $C_{P_i}\cdot\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A$ reproduces $P_i=673.2$ kW, and $\dot m=\rho Av_0=2031$ kg/s with $v_2=25.75$ m/s gives $\tfrac12\dot mv_2^2=673.2$ kW, the energy route of section 4. The pressure jump is $p_l-p_u=406$ Pa, about 0.4 % of atmospheric pressure. The 62 % heavier aircraft needs $1.62^{3/2}=2.06$ times the induced power, because $P_i\propto T^{3/2}$, and its rotor efficiency is $1/\sqrt{1.62}=0.79$ times as good. The computed $v_0$ of 10–13 m/s agrees with the Lynx value of about 12 m/s in [[#5. Disc loading across vehicles]].

The induced power is the ideal minimum; the real rotor also needs profile power and has a non-uniform inflow, which [[#14. Worked example: blade-element estimate for the Lynx]] adds.

## 8. Annular wake division

ADT gives the right order of magnitude but, as a one-dimensional theory, assumes a uniform downwash over every cross-section, which no real rotor has. A blade's tip moves much faster than its root, so the sections push the air down unequally: those near the root move slowly and do little, while the tips do most of the work. Annular wake division keeps the actuator-disc idea but applies it to each thin ring of the disc separately, so the induced velocity may vary with radius (but not round the circumference). The uniform wake of ADT becomes a nest of concentric rings, each uniform round its circumference and differing from ring to ring.

### Elemental annulus

The annulus between $r$ and $r+\mathrm dr$ has area

$$
\mathrm dA=2\pi r\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Each annulus is treated as its own actuator disc, with its own streamtube, induced velocity $v_i(r)$ and far-wake velocity $v_2=2v_i(r)$ (the section 3 result applied ring by ring, which neglects any interaction between neighbouring rings). The mass flow through it is

$$
\mathrm d\dot m=\rho v_i\,\mathrm dA=2\pi\rho v_ir\,\mathrm dr.
$$

The thrust of the annulus is its mass flow times the velocity it gives the air:

$$
\mathrm dT=\mathrm d\dot m\,v_2=(2\pi\rho v_ir\,\mathrm dr)(2v_i)=4\pi\rho v_i^2r\,\mathrm dr.
$$

The induced power of the annulus is its thrust times the velocity at the disc:

$$
\mathrm dP_i=\mathrm dT\,v_i=4\pi\rho v_i^3r\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Adding up all the rings, from the centre $r=0$ to the tip $r=R$, is an integration along the radius, which gives the rotor totals:

$$
\boxed{T=4\pi\rho\int_0^Rv_i^2r\,\mathrm dr,\qquad P_i=4\pi\rho\int_0^Rv_i^3r\,\mathrm dr.}
$$

With $v_i$ constant these give back $T=2\pi\rho v_i^2R^2=2\rho Av_i^2$ and $P_i=2\rho Av_i^3=Tv_i$, the ADT results. For any other rotor the integrals need the induced velocity as a function of radius, $v_i(r)$.

### Power-law induced velocity distributions

![[aa_4_1_induced_velocity_distributions.png|900]]

A convenient family is the power law

$$
v_i(r)=v_i^{T_s}\left(\frac rR\right)^n,
$$

where $n$ sets the shape and $v_i^{T_s}$, the value at the tip $r=R$, sets the scale. $n=0$ is constant $v_i$ (ADT), $n=1$ a linear variation, $n=2$ a parabola and $n=3$ a cubic. Larger $n$ concentrates the downwash towards the tip and leaves the inner disc doing little.

Thrust. Substitute the distribution into the thrust integral and take the constants out:

$$
T=4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2\int_0^R\left(\frac rR\right)^{2n}r\,\mathrm dr=\frac{4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{R^{2n}}\int_0^Rr^{2n+1}\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Integrate:

$$
T=\frac{4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{R^{2n}}\cdot\frac{R^{2n+2}}{2n+2}=\frac{4\pi R^2\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{2(n+1)}.
$$

With $A=\pi R^2$:

$$
\boxed{T=\frac{2\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{n+1}.}
$$

Dividing by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A$ gives the thrust coefficient:

$$
C_T=\frac{4}{n+1}\left(\frac{v_i^{T_s}}{V_T}\right)^2.
$$

Solving the thrust result for the tip value:

$$
\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2=(n+1)\frac{T}{2\rho A},\qquad v_i^{T_s}=\sqrt{n+1}\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}=\sqrt{n+1}\,v_0.
$$

For $n=0$ this is $v_0$ itself. For a fixed thrust, a more tip-heavy distribution needs a larger peak induced velocity, as panel (a) shows: all four curves carry the same thrust.

Induced power. Substitute into the power integral in the same way:

$$
P_i=4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3\int_0^R\left(\frac rR\right)^{3n}r\,\mathrm dr=\frac{4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3}{R^{3n}}\int_0^Rr^{3n+1}\,\mathrm dr,
$$

$$
P_i=\frac{4\pi\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3}{R^{3n}}\cdot\frac{R^{3n+2}}{3n+2}=\frac{4\pi R^2\rho\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3}{3n+2},
$$

$$
\boxed{P_i=\frac{4\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3}{3n+2},\qquad C_{P_i}=\frac{8}{3n+2}\left(\frac{v_i^{T_s}}{V_T}\right)^3.}
$$

To compare with ADT at the same thrust, the power is rewritten in terms of $T$, which links it to the uniform result $Tv_i$. Split off one factor of $v_i^{T_s}$ and write the 4 as $2\times2$:

$$
P_i=\frac{2\cdot2\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{3n+2}\,v_i^{T_s}.
$$

Multiply top and bottom by $n+1$, so that the thrust result appears:

$$
P_i=\frac{2(n+1)}{3n+2}\cdot\frac{2\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{n+1}\,v_i^{T_s}.
$$

The second fraction is $T$:

$$
P_i=\frac{2(n+1)}{3n+2}\,Tv_i^{T_s}.
$$

Substitute $v_i^{T_s}=\sqrt{n+1}\,v_0$:

$$
P_i=\frac{2(n+1)}{3n+2}\,T\sqrt{n+1}\,\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}=\frac{2(n+1)^{3/2}}{3n+2}\,Tv_0.
$$

### Induced power factor

The ideal induced power is the power with the flow uniform across the disc, the ADT value at the same thrust, $P_{iIDEAL}=Tv_0=T\sqrt{T/2\rho A}$. The difference between the two lies entirely in the factors containing $n$, and their ratio is the induced power factor:

$$
k_i=\frac{P_i}{P_{iIDEAL}}=\frac{2(n+1)^{3/2}}{3n+2},
$$

and dividing top and bottom by 2,

$$
\boxed{k_i=\frac{P_i}{P_{iIDEAL}}=\frac{(n+1)^{3/2}}{1+3n/2}.}
$$

| $n$ | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $k_i$ | 1.000 | 1.050 | 1.131 | 1.299 | 1.455 | 1.729 |

$k_i\geq1$ for every $n\geq0$, with equality only at $n=0$. Square both sides of $(n+1)^{3/2}\geq1+3n/2$ (both are positive):

$$
(n+1)^3=1+3n+3n^2+n^3\geq1+3n+\tfrac94n^2=\left(1+\tfrac32n\right)^2,
$$

which holds because $3n^2+n^3\geq\tfrac94n^2$ for $n\geq0$, with equality only at $n=0$. So any non-uniform distribution in this family costs more induced power than the uniform one for the same thrust; as $n$ increases, $v_i$ increases and the rotor becomes less efficient. The reason is that thrust grows as $v_i^2$ but power as $v_i^3$: concentrating the downwash where it is already large buys thrust at a disproportionate cost in power. Put another way, the thrust is the momentum the wake carries away each second and the induced power is the kinetic energy it carries away. For a given momentum flow the kinetic-energy flow is least when the velocity is the same everywhere, so any variation across the wake is power spent without extra thrust. Real hovering rotors have $k_i$ of about 1.1–1.2.

## 9. Ground effect, wake recirculation and brownout

![[aa_4_1_ground_effect_recirculation.png|900]]

Ground effect matters far more to a rotorcraft than to a fixed-wing aircraft, which spends almost all its flight far above the ground. A helicopter hovers close to the ground in take-off, landing and rescue work, and the downwash it pushes down is reflected by the ground. Out of ground effect the wake contracts and leaves the rotor freely, as ADT assumes. Close to the ground the wake cannot continue downwards: the ground is a solid, non-penetrable surface, so it turns the wake outwards into a radial wall jet along the ground, and the slowing of the flow beneath the rotor raises the pressure there. For the same thrust the rotor then needs a smaller induced velocity and less induced power, or for the same power it makes more thrust. This is ground effect, the rotor version of the image-vortex ground effect on a wing in [[SESA3043 2.2 - Lumped Vortex Method|2.2]]. The same mirror-image method carries over: actuator disc theory stays the basic model, and an image rotor below the ground plane enforces the no-penetration condition ([[Method of Images]]); CFD is the other route. The benefit fades as the rotor climbs and is small beyond about one rotor diameter. Cheeseman and Bennett's classical image-source estimate, outside the formula set of this chapter and given only for scale, puts the thrust ratio at constant power as $T_{IGE}/T_{OGE}=1/\left[1-(R/4z)^2\right]$ for rotor height $z$: 1.33 at $z=R/2$, 1.07 at $z=R$ and 1.02 at $z=2R$.

CFD vorticity fields of a helicopter hovering at decreasing heights (DOI 10.1016/j.ast.2022.107401) show the tip vortices trailing straight down out of ground effect and spreading sideways along the ground in ground effect, where they also wrap back up towards the fuselage.

Near a wall, a building or a ship's hangar the outwash cannot spread freely either. It is turned up and back over the rotor and ingested again, which is wake recirculation. The rotor then flies in its own downwash: the inflow increases, more power is needed for the same thrust, and the loads become unsteady, which makes hovering beside obstacles demanding for the pilot. Ship-borne rotorcraft get the worst of it: landing on or taking off from a deck, the wake and tip vortices interact with the ship's structure and with the sea surface at once, and the ground effect, the recirculation and the ship's own airwake all affect the blade aerodynamics and the handling together. Problems like these are why rotorcraft aerodynamics is much less mature than fixed-wing aerodynamics.

Over sand, dust or snow the ground jet lifts the loose material, and the recirculating flow carries it up through the rotor and round the aircraft. The pilot can lose all visual reference with the ground in seconds. This is brownout (whiteout over snow), a major cause of helicopter landing accidents in desert operations.

## 10. Blade element theory

The model and the actual rotor have to be kept apart. ADT replaces the blades by a disc that carries a pressure difference while the air passes through it, and annular wake division only lets that disc's downwash vary from ring to ring. A real rotor has two, three or four blades turning through the air, each pushing the fluid downwards, and two rotors with different blade numbers, aerofoil sections, pitch or twist have different aerodynamics. ADT ignores all of these: the number of blades, the aerofoil characteristics (lift and drag), the blade twist distribution and compressibility.

Blade element theory (BET) rectifies some of these. It models each blade as many segments of twisted aerofoil, each following a helical path and treated as an ordinary 2-D wing section with its own $C_L$ and $C_D$, and sums the contributions of all segments of all blades to give the total thrust, torque and power.

Basic BET takes untwisted blades at a constant collective pitch, the same pitch at every radius. BET itself can take any pitch distribution; the linearly twisted blade (§12) and annulus theory (§13) are extensions of the same procedure, needed for awareness rather than derivation. Two diagrams carry the whole method: the top view of the disc with the element $\mathrm dr$ at radius $r$, and the section through the element with its velocities, angles and forces (panels a and b of the figure below). Once those are understood, the rest is integration.

### Assumptions and rotor solidity

Basic BET assumes the blades continue to the centre of the rotor and have constant chord $c$. With $N$ blades of radius $R$ the total blade area is $NcR$. The rotor solidity is the ratio of blade area to disc area:

$$
s\equiv\frac{\text{blade area}}{\text{disc area}}=\frac{NcR}{\pi R^2}=\frac{Nc}{\pi R}.
$$

Helicopter main rotors have $s$ of about 0.05–0.12.

### Velocities, angles and forces on an element

![[aa_4_1_blade_element.png|900]]

The element of span $\mathrm dr$ at radius $r$ on a blade turning at $\Omega$ sees two velocity components (panel b). In the plane of rotation it meets the air at $U_T=r\Omega$, its own speed. Perpendicular to that plane the air comes down through the disc at the induced velocity, $U_P=v_i$. The resultant is $U=\sqrt{U_P^2+U_T^2}$, inclined at the inflow angle $\phi$ below the plane of rotation:

$$
\tan\phi=\frac{U_P}{U_T}=\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}.
$$

The blade pitch angle $\theta$ is set by the pilot through the controls and measured from the plane of rotation to the chord. The aerofoil meets the resultant flow at the angle of attack

$$
\alpha=\theta-\phi.
$$

The lift and drag of the element act perpendicular and parallel to $U$:

$$
\mathrm dL=\tfrac12\rho U^2C_Lc\,\mathrm dr,\qquad\mathrm dD=\tfrac12\rho U^2C_Dc\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Because $U$ is tilted by $\phi$, the lift is tilted back by $\phi$ from the rotor axis. Resolving into the thrust direction (along the axis, up) and the in-plane direction $\mathrm d\hat H$ (opposing the rotation):

$$
\mathrm dT=\mathrm dL\cos\phi-\mathrm dD\sin\phi,\qquad\mathrm d\hat H=\mathrm dL\sin\phi+\mathrm dD\cos\phi.
$$

$\mathrm d\hat H$ is what the engine must overcome. Its $\mathrm dL\sin\phi$ part is the price of tilting the lift (the induced part) and its $\mathrm dD\cos\phi$ part is the aerofoil's profile drag.

Even with a constant pitch $\theta$, the inflow angle varies along the blade, because $U_T=r\Omega$ grows with radius while ADT keeps $v_i$ uniform; a non-uniform $v_i$ makes $\phi$ vary further. The induced velocity is a few metres per second to a few tens of metres per second, while the outer blade moves at a large fraction of the speed of sound, so over most of the blade $U_P\ll U_T$ ($v_i\ll r\Omega$), $\phi$ is small and, in radians (with $U\approx U_T$ in magnitude only: the resultant is still tilted by $\phi$, which is why $\mathrm dL\sin\phi$ survives in $\mathrm d\hat H$),

$$
\sin\phi\approx\phi\approx\frac{v_i}{r\Omega},\qquad\cos\phi\approx1,\qquad U\approx U_T=r\Omega.
$$

The approximation fails near the root, where $r\Omega\to0$; the root contributes little thrust because $U^2$ is small there, so the error in the totals is small. With the collective pitch angle $\theta_0$ (the same pitch on every section and every blade) and the small-angle lift law $C_L=a\alpha$ with lift slope $a$ (per radian, with $\alpha$ and $\theta_0$ measured from the zero-lift line, or for a symmetric section),

$$
\alpha=\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega},\qquad C_L=a\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right).
$$

### Rotor thrust

Since $D\ll L$ and $\phi$ is small, $\mathrm dD\sin\phi$ is a small force times a small factor, much smaller than $\mathrm dL\cos\phi$, and it is dropped. The in-plane force keeps both its terms, because there each force is multiplied by a different factor ($\sin\phi$ on the lift, $\cos\phi\approx1$ on the drag) and the two products can be of similar size. So the element thrust is the lift:

$$
\mathrm dT\approx\mathrm dL=\tfrac12\rho U^2C_Lc\,\mathrm dr=\tfrac12\rho(r\Omega)^2ca\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right)\mathrm dr.
$$

Integrate along one blade and multiply by $N$:

$$
T=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2Nca\int_0^R\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right)r^2\,\mathrm dr=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2Nca\int_0^R\left(\theta_0r^2-\frac{v_i}{\Omega}r\right)\mathrm dr.
$$

With $v_i$ uniform (the ADT inflow), integrate term by term:

$$
T=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2Nca\left(\frac{\theta_0R^3}{3}-\frac{v_i}{\Omega}\frac{R^2}{2}\right).
$$

Take $R^3$ out, using $v_i/(\Omega R)=\lambda_i$:

$$
T=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcaR^3\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right),
$$

and with $\Omega^2R^2=V_T^2$:

$$
\boxed{T=\tfrac12\rho V_T^2NcRa\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right).}
$$

### Rotor torque and power

Each element's in-plane force $\mathrm d\hat H$ acts at radius $r$, so it adds a torque about the shaft (torque = length × force):

$$
\mathrm dQ=r\,\mathrm d\hat H=r\left(\mathrm dL\sin\phi+\mathrm dD\cos\phi\right).
$$

Substitute the element forces:

$$
\mathrm dQ=\tfrac12\rho U^2c\left(C_L\sin\phi+C_D\cos\phi\right)r\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Apply the small-angle forms ($U=r\Omega$, $\sin\phi=v_i/r\Omega$, $\cos\phi=1$, $C_L=a\alpha$):

$$
\mathrm dQ=\tfrac12\rho(r\Omega)^2c\left[a\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right)\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}+C_D\right]r\,\mathrm dr=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2c\left[a\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right)\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}+C_D\right]r^3\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Integrate along the blade and multiply by $N$, taking $C_D$ constant along the blade:

$$
Q=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2Nca\int_0^R\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}\right)\frac{v_i}{r\Omega}r^3\,\mathrm dr+\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcC_D\int_0^Rr^3\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Change to the non-dimensional radius $x=r/R$, so $r=xR$ and $\mathrm dr=R\,\mathrm dx$. In the first integral,

$$
\left(\theta_0-\frac{v_i}{xR\Omega}\right)\frac{v_i}{xR\Omega}x^3R^3\cdot R\,\mathrm dx=R^4\left(\theta_0x^2-\frac{v_i}{R\Omega}x\right)\frac{v_i}{R\Omega}\,\mathrm dx=R^4\left(\theta_0x^2-\lambda_ix\right)\lambda_i\,\mathrm dx,
$$

and in the second $r^3\,\mathrm dr=R^4x^3\,\mathrm dx$. So

$$
Q=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcR^4a\,\lambda_i\int_0^1\left(\theta_0x^2-\lambda_ix\right)\mathrm dx+\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcR^4C_D\int_0^1x^3\,\mathrm dx.
$$

Integrate:

$$
Q=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcR^4a\left[\frac{\theta_0x^3}{3}-\frac{\lambda_ix^2}{2}\right]_0^1\lambda_i+\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcR^4C_D\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^1,
$$

$$
\boxed{Q=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2NcR^4a\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)\lambda_i+\tfrac18\rho\Omega^2NcR^4C_D.}
$$

For circular motion the power is torque times rotational speed, $P=Q\Omega$:

$$
P=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^3NcR^4a\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)\lambda_i+\tfrac18\rho\Omega^3NcR^4C_D.
$$

With $\Omega^3R^3=V_T^3$:

$$
\boxed{P=\tfrac12\rho V_T^3NcRa\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)\lambda_i+\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D.}
$$

### Total power: induced plus profile

Compare the first term of $P$ with the thrust. Since $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3NcRa(\theta_0/3-\lambda_i/2)=T\,V_T$,

$$
\tfrac12\rho V_T^3NcRa\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)\lambda_i=TV_T\lambda_i=Tv_i,
$$

which is exactly the induced power $P_i=Tv_i$ of the momentum analysis. The blade-element route recovers it independently, from the backward tilt of the lift vector. The second term is the profile power, the work done against the aerofoils' drag:

$$
P_{profile}=\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D.
$$

So the total power is induced power plus profile power, and with the induced velocity from the momentum analysis,

$$
\boxed{P=T\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}+\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D.}
$$

The induced term depends on the disc area $A$; the profile term depends on the blade area $NcR$. A rotor designer wants a large disc (low induced power) carried by a small blade area (low profile power), which is the reason for long, slender blades. The profile term also grows as $V_T^3$, so the tip speed is kept as low as the blade's stall margin allows.

## 11. Coefficients and the figure of merit

Divide the BET thrust by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A$:

$$
C_T=\frac{NcR}{A}a\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right).
$$

With $A=\pi R^2$, $NcR/A=Nc/\pi R=s$:

$$
C_T=sa\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right),\qquad\boxed{\frac{C_T}{sa}=\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}.}
$$

$C_T/s$ is the blade loading coefficient, the thrust per unit blade area in coefficient form; it is proportional to the mean lift coefficient of the blades and is limited by blade stall.

For the power coefficient, divide $P=Tv_i+\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_{D0}$ by $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A$. The first term gives $C_T\lambda_i$ as in section 6. The second gives

$$
\frac{\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_{D0}}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A}=\frac14\frac{NcR}{A}C_{D0}=\frac14sC_{D0},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{C_P=C_T\lambda_i+\tfrac14sC_{D0},}
$$

where $C_{D0}$ is the profile drag coefficient of the blade sections (the $C_D$ of section 10, taken constant).

The figure of merit compares ideal induced power with the actual rotor power:

$$
\mathrm{FM}=\frac{\text{ideal induced power}}{\text{actual rotor power}}=\frac{C_T\lambda_i}{C_T\lambda_i+sC_{D0}/4}.
$$

In hover $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$, so $C_T\lambda_i=C_T^{3/2}/2$:

$$
\mathrm{FM}=\frac{C_T^{3/2}/2}{C_T^{3/2}/2+sC_{D0}/4}.
$$

Multiply top and bottom by 2:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{FM}=\frac{C_T^{3/2}}{C_T^{3/2}+sC_{D0}/2}.}
$$

![[aa_4_1_figure_of_merit.png|900]]

FM is perfection over reality. The actual power exceeds the ideal induced power for two reasons: the real downwash varies with radius, so the induced power is $k_iP_{iIDEAL}$ rather than $P_{iIDEAL}$ (§8), and the blades also need profile power. FM = 1 would be a rotor with no profile drag and uniform inflow. For most rotors it lies between 0.7 and 0.8. High solidity (more blades, longer chord, larger blade area) raises the profile power and lowers FM; low-drag aerofoils or low solidity improve it. The formula also shows that FM rises with $C_T$, since induced power grows as $C_T^{3/2}$ while profile power is fixed by $sC_{D0}$, so a lightly loaded rotor always shows a poor FM, and two rotors should only be compared by FM at the same disc loading.

> [!warning] FM here assumes uniform inflow
> The FM formula above takes the induced power as the ideal $C_T^{3/2}/2$. A real rotor has $k_i>1$ ([[#Induced power factor]]), and a fuller version is $\mathrm{FM}=\dfrac{C_T^{3/2}/2}{k_iC_T^{3/2}/2+sC_{D0}/4}$, which is lower. Use the uniform-inflow form unless $k_i$ is given.

## 12. Blade twist

A blade with constant pitch has $\alpha=\theta_0-v_i/(r\Omega)$, which is largest at the tip, and it loads the outer blade most. Twisting the blade nose-down towards the tip unloads the tip and spreads the load inwards. The simplest twist is a linear variation of pitch with radius:

$$
\theta=\theta_0-\kappa\frac rR,
$$

where $\theta_0$ is the pitch extrapolated to the centre and $\kappa$ the total twist from centre to tip (positive for washout, the tip at lower pitch). The incidence becomes

$$
\alpha=\theta_0-\kappa\frac rR-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r},
$$

that is, angle of attack = collective − twist − inflow.

The thrust integral of section 10 picks up one extra term:

$$
T=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2caN\int_0^R\left[\theta_0-\kappa\left(\frac rR\right)-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r}\right]r^2\,\mathrm dr.
$$

Integrate term by term, with $v_i$ uniform:

$$
T=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2caN\left(\frac{\theta_0R^3}{3}-\frac{\kappa}{R}\frac{R^4}{4}-\frac{v_i}{\Omega}\frac{R^2}{2}\right)=\tfrac12\rho\Omega^2caNR^3\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\kappa}{4}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right).
$$

So

$$
\boxed{T=\tfrac12\rho V_T^2NcRa\left(\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac\kappa4-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right),\qquad\frac{C_T}{sa}=\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac\kappa4-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}.}
$$

The pitch at three-quarter radius is $\theta_{0.75}=\theta_0-\tfrac34\kappa$, and $\theta_{0.75}/3=\theta_0/3-\kappa/4$, so

$$
\frac{C_T}{sa}=\frac{\theta_{0.75}}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}.
$$

A linearly twisted blade gives the same thrust (with uniform inflow) as an untwisted blade whose constant pitch equals the twisted blade's pitch at $r=0.75R$. This is why rotor pitch is usually quoted at three-quarter radius.

## 13. Annulus theory

Annulus theory extends the combined momentum and blade-element analysis to examine non-uniform downwash. It applies both theories to each annulus and sets them equal, so the downwash at each radius follows from the local blade geometry, allowing variable chord $c(r)$, variable lift slope $a(r)$ and variable pitch $\theta(r)$.

The momentum thrust of the annulus at $r$ is (section 8)

$$
\mathrm dT=4\pi\rho v_i^2r\,\mathrm dr.
$$

The blade-element thrust of the same annulus, $N$ elements each of chord $c(r)$, is (section 10)

$$
\mathrm dT=N\cdot\tfrac12\rho(\Omega r)^2c(r)a(r)\left(\theta-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r}\right)\mathrm dr.
$$

Equate and cancel $\rho\,\mathrm dr$:

$$
4\pi v_i^2r=\tfrac12\Omega^2r^2Nc\,a\left(\theta-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r}\right).
$$

Divide by $4\pi r$:

$$
v_i^2=\frac18\cdot\frac{Nc\,a}{\pi}\,\Omega^2r\left(\theta-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r}\right).
$$

Write $\Omega^2r=V_T^2r/R^2$ and take one $1/R$ into the $Nc/\pi$ factor:

$$
v_i^2=\frac18V_T^2\frac{Nc}{\pi R}\,a\,\frac rR\left(\theta-\frac{v_i}{\Omega r}\right).
$$

Multiply the bracket through by $r/R$, noting $\dfrac rR\cdot\dfrac{v_i}{\Omega r}=\dfrac{v_i}{\Omega R}=\dfrac{v_i}{V_T}$:

$$
\boxed{v_i^2(r)=\frac18V_T^2\,\frac{Nc(r)}{\pi R}\,a(r)\left(\frac{\theta r}{R}-\frac{v_i(r)}{V_T}\right),\qquad T=\int_0^R4\pi\rho r\,v_i^2(r)\,\mathrm dr.}
$$

For constant chord and lift slope the factor $Nc/\pi R$ is the solidity $s$. Dividing by $V_T^2$ and writing $x=r/R$, $\lambda_i(x)=v_i/V_T$:

$$
\lambda_i^2+\frac{sa}{8}\lambda_i-\frac{sa}{8}\theta x=0.
$$

The physical (positive) root is

$$
\lambda_i(x)=\frac{sa}{16}\left[\sqrt{1+\frac{32\theta x}{sa}}-1\right].
$$

The inflow is uniform only if the product $\theta x$ is constant, that is, if

$$
\theta=\frac{\theta_{tip}}{x}=\theta_{tip}\frac Rr.
$$

This hyperbolic distribution is the ideal twist. It is the only pitch distribution that gives uniform downwash, and therefore, by the induced power factor result, the minimum induced power, $k_i=1$. It is impractical near the root, where $\theta\to\infty$, so real blades use linear twist as an approximation to it over the outer blade.

![[aa_4_1_twist_annulus_inflow.png|900]]

For the Lynx at maximum take-off ($C_T=0.01386$) with an assumed $s=0.08$, $a=5.73$/rad, the annulus equation integrated numerically gives these results at the same thrust:

| blade | pitch needed | $k_i$ |
|---|---|---:|
| untwisted | $\theta_0=10.09^\circ$ | 1.077 |
| linear twist, $\kappa=8^\circ$ | $\theta_0=16.18^\circ$ ($\theta_{0.75}=10.18^\circ$) | 1.036 |
| ideal twist | $\theta_{tip}=6.84^\circ$ | 1.000 |

The untwisted blade's inflow rises roughly as $\sqrt x$ towards the tip, the linear twist flattens it over the outer blade, and the ideal twist makes it exactly uniform. The untwisted blade needs 7.7 % more induced power than the ideal one. The twisted blade's $\theta_{0.75}$ is within $0.1^\circ$ of the untwisted pitch, as the three-quarter-radius result of section 12 predicts.

## 14. Worked example: blade-element estimate for the Lynx

> The Lynx of section 7 at maximum take-off, with blade data assumed for illustration: solidity $s=0.08$, lift slope $a=5.73$ per radian, profile drag coefficient $C_{D0}=0.01$, untwisted blades and uniform inflow. Find the collective pitch, the total power, the torque and the figure of merit.

The chord follows from the solidity: $c=s\pi R/N=0.08\pi(6.4)/4=0.402$ m.

From section 7, $C_T=0.013865$ and $\lambda_i=0.058874$. The blade loading is

$$
\frac{C_T}{sa}=\frac{0.013865}{0.08\times5.73}=0.030246.
$$

Rearrange $C_T/sa=\theta_0/3-\lambda_i/2$ for the collective pitch:

$$
\theta_0=3\left(\frac{C_T}{sa}+\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)=3(0.030246+0.029437)=0.17905\ \text{rad}=10.26^\circ.
$$

The power coefficient:

$$
C_P=C_T\lambda_i+\tfrac14sC_{D0}=8.163\times10^{-4}+2.000\times10^{-4}=1.0163\times10^{-3}.
$$

With $\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A=\tfrac12(1.2256)(218.69)^3(128.68)=8.247\times10^8$ W,

$$
P=1.0163\times10^{-3}\times8.247\times10^8=838\ \text{kW}\quad(673\ \text{kW induced}+165\ \text{kW profile}),
$$

$$
Q=\frac P\Omega=\frac{838\,000}{34.17}=24.5\ \text{kN m},
$$

$$
\mathrm{FM}=\frac{C_T\lambda_i}{C_P}=\frac{8.163\times10^{-4}}{1.0163\times10^{-3}}=0.803,
$$

and from the closed form, $\mathrm{FM}=C_T^{3/2}/(C_T^{3/2}+sC_{D0}/2)=0.001633/(0.001633+0.0004)=0.803$.

> [!check] Consistency checks (Python)
> Solving the combined momentum and BET quadratic $4\lambda_i^2+\tfrac{sa}{2}\lambda_i-\tfrac{sa}{3}\theta_0=0$ with $\theta_0=0.17905$ returns $\lambda_i=0.05887$, the momentum value, so the pitch is self-consistent. FM = 0.80 sits at the top of the 0.7–0.8 band for real rotors; the non-uniform inflow of [[#13. Annulus theory]] ($k_i=1.077$ untwisted) would lower it to $8.163/(1.077\times8.163+2.000)=0.76$.

The collective of about $10^\circ$ is a typical hover value. Since the blade data are assumed, the answer shows the method, not the Lynx's actual performance.

## 15. Key formulae

Hover, actuator disc (steady, incompressible, uniform $v_i$, no swirl):

$$
v_2=2v_i,\qquad v_0=v_i=\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)^{1/2},\qquad T=\tfrac12\rho v_2^2A=2\rho v_i^2A,\qquad A_2=\frac A2,
$$

$$
P_i=Tv_i=T\left(\frac{T}{2\rho A}\right)^{1/2},\qquad\frac{T}{P_i}=\frac1{v_i}.
$$

Coefficients ($V_T=\Omega R$, $A=\pi R^2$, convention with $\tfrac12$):

$$
\lambda_i=\frac{v_i}{V_T}=\frac{C_T^{1/2}}{2},\qquad C_T=\frac{T}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A}=4\lambda_i^2,\qquad C_P=\frac{P}{\tfrac12\rho V_T^3A},\qquad C_{P_i}=C_T\lambda_i=\frac{C_T^{3/2}}{2}.
$$

Annular wake, $v_i=v_i^{T_s}(r/R)^n$:

$$
T=4\pi\rho\int_0^Rv_i^2r\,\mathrm dr=\frac{2\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^2}{n+1},\qquad P_i=4\pi\rho\int_0^Rv_i^3r\,\mathrm dr=\frac{4\rho A\left(v_i^{T_s}\right)^3}{3n+2},\qquad k_i=\frac{P_i}{P_{iIDEAL}}=\frac{(n+1)^{3/2}}{1+3n/2}.
$$

Blade elements (small $\phi$ and $\alpha$ in radians, $C_L=a\alpha$, constant chord, uniform $v_i$, blades to the centre):

$$
s=\frac{Nc}{\pi R},\qquad\frac{C_T}{sa}=\frac{\theta_0}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\ \left(-\frac\kappa4\ \text{with linear twist}\right),\qquad C_P=C_T\lambda_i+\tfrac14sC_{D0},
$$

$$
P=T\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}+\tfrac18\rho V_T^3NcRC_D,\qquad\mathrm{FM}=\frac{C_T\lambda_i}{C_T\lambda_i+sC_{D0}/4}=\frac{C_T^{3/2}}{C_T^{3/2}+sC_{D0}/2}.
$$

Annulus theory: $v_i^2(r)=\tfrac18V_T^2\dfrac{Nc(r)}{\pi R}a(r)\left(\dfrac{\theta r}{R}-\dfrac{v_i(r)}{V_T}\right)$.

## Common mistakes

1. Mixing $C_T=T/\tfrac12\rho V_T^2A$ with the US textbook $C_T=T/\rho A(\Omega R)^2$: the first is twice as large, and $\lambda_i=\sqrt{C_T}/2$ only holds for it.
2. Leaving $\theta_0$ or $\kappa$ in degrees in $C_T/sa=\theta_0/3-\kappa/4-\lambda_i/2$.
3. Writing $v_2=v_i$, or taking the wake area equal to the disc area: the wake is twice as fast and half the area.
4. Using $P_i=\tfrac12Tv_2$ and $Tv_i$ as if they differed: they are the same, since $v_2=2v_i$.
5. Forgetting that thrust in hover is the weight, so $P_i\propto(mg)^{3/2}$, not $\propto mg$.
6. Quoting FM for a lightly loaded rotor as evidence of a bad design, without comparing at the same $C_T$.
7. Expecting the small-$\phi$ BET to hold at the root, where $v_i\ll r\Omega$ fails.
