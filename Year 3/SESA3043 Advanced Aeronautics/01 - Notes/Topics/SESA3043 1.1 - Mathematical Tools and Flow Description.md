# SESA3043 1.1 - Mathematical Tools and Flow Description

> [!abstract] Summary
> The governing equations become manageable only after the notation is fixed. Scalars carry magnitude; vectors carry magnitude and direction; second-order tensors map one direction into another. Einstein notation compresses three component equations into one indexed expression. The Eulerian view observes a fixed point or control volume, whereas the Lagrangian view follows a material element. The material derivative and Reynolds transport theorem connect the two descriptions. Flow rate, flux and specific quantity then provide a single template for transporting mass, momentum and energy.

## 1. Coordinate convention

For the external-flow work in Chapters 1–3:

- $x$: streamwise;
- $y$: wall-normal, positive from the wall toward the freestream;
- $z$: spanwise.

Most derivations later reduce to two-dimensional $x$–$y$ flow, but the general conservation laws are first written in three dimensions.

## 2. Scalars, vectors and tensors

| Object | Order | Fluid-mechanics examples | Component form |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Scalar | 0 | $p,\rho,T,e$ | one value |
| Vector | 1 | $\mathbf x,\mathbf u,\mathbf f$ | $u_i\mathbf e_i$ |
| Second-order tensor | 2 | stress $\boldsymbol\tau$ | $\tau_{ij}\mathbf e_i\mathbf e_j$ |

The velocity vector may be written as

$$
\mathbf u=u\mathbf e_x+v\mathbf e_y+w\mathbf e_z=u_i\mathbf e_i.
$$

The stress tensor has nine components:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=
\begin{bmatrix}
\tau_{11}&\tau_{12}&\tau_{13}\\
\tau_{21}&\tau_{22}&\tau_{23}\\
\tau_{31}&\tau_{32}&\tau_{33}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

## 3. Einstein summation and free indices

A repeated index in one term is summed over all coordinate directions:

$$
u_i u_i=u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}
=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}.
$$

In

$$
u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j},
$$

$j$ is the repeated **dummy index** and $i$ is the **free index**. The expression represents one equation for each choice $i=1,2,3$.

For a stress divergence,

$$
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
=\frac{\partial\tau_{i1}}{\partial x_1}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{i2}}{\partial x_2}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{i3}}{\partial x_3}.
$$

> [!warning] Index consistency
> Every additive term in one tensor equation must carry the same free indices. A dummy index may be renamed, but it must occur exactly twice in a product.

## 4. Vector differential operators

The del operator is

$$
\nabla=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}.
$$

### Gradient

For scalar $\phi$,

$$
\nabla\phi=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

It points in the direction of greatest increase of $\phi$.

### Divergence

For vector $\mathbf u$,

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}.
$$

It measures the local volumetric expansion rate of a fluid element. Positive divergence means expansion; negative divergence means contraction.

### Curl and vorticity

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u.
$$

In a two-dimensional $x$–$y$ flow, the non-zero component is

$$
\omega_z=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}.
$$

In three dimensions, expand the curl as a determinant (see the cross product below):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
\partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\
u&v&w
\end{vmatrix}
=\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right)\mathbf i
+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)\mathbf j
+\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)\mathbf k.
$$

The curl measures local spin. Irrotational (potential) flow is defined by $\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf 0$.

### Laplacian

The Laplacian is the divergence of the gradient, $\nabla^2\phi=\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi$. For a scalar,

$$
\nabla^2\phi=\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}
=\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}.
$$

Applied to a vector, it acts on each component: $\nabla^2\mathbf u=(\nabla^2u,\nabla^2v,\nabla^2w)$. This is the viscous term of incompressible Navier–Stokes.

### Dot and cross products

The **dot product** of two vectors is a scalar and is **commutative**:

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_iv_i=u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z,
\qquad
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u.
$$

The **cross product** of two vectors is a vector and is **anti-commutative**:

$$
\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
u_x&u_y&u_z\\
\omega_x&\omega_y&\omega_z
\end{vmatrix}
=(u_y\omega_z-u_z\omega_y)\mathbf i
-(u_x\omega_z-u_z\omega_x)\mathbf j
+(u_x\omega_y-u_y\omega_x)\mathbf k,
$$

$$
\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=-\,\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf u.
$$

The lecture said to "look up your math notes" for evaluating the determinant. The line above is **cofactor expansion** along the first row, with the minus sign on the middle term. **Sarrus' rule** gives the same result for 3×3 determinants: repeat the first two columns, add the three down-right diagonal products and subtract the three up-right ones. The direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers from the first vector to the second and the thumb gives $\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$.

> [!tip] Two identities used later
> $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla\!\left(\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$ turns the convective term into a gradient for irrotational flow, which is the Bernoulli step. $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi)=\nabla^2\phi$ turns continuity into Laplace's equation.

## 5. Taylor expansion for a differential element

The local conservation derivations compare fluxes on opposite faces of a small element. For any smooth $f(x)$,

$$
f\left(x+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=f(x)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2}
+O(\Delta x^2),
$$

$$
f\left(x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=f(x)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2}
+O(\Delta x^2).
$$

Subtracting cancels the centre value:

$$
f\left(x+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
-f\left(x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x+O(\Delta x^3).
$$

In the limit $\Delta x\to0$, the net flux through opposite faces becomes a spatial derivative times the element volume. This is the local step behind the differential continuity and momentum equations.

## 6. Eulerian and Lagrangian descriptions

The [[Eulerian and Lagrangian Flow Descriptions]] answer different questions:

- **Lagrangian:** how does a particular material particle change as it moves?
- **Eulerian:** how does the field at a fixed point or fixed control volume change?

Let a scalar field be $\phi(\mathbf x,t)$ and let a particle follow $\mathbf x(t)$. The chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dt}
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
+\frac{\mathrm dx_i}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

Since $\mathrm dx_i/\mathrm dt=u_i$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{D\phi}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+u_i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\phi
}.
$$

The first term is the local/unsteady change. The second is convective change caused by moving through a spatial gradient.

For velocity,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
}
$$

is the acceleration of a fluid particle.

## 7. Flow rate, flux and specific quantity

Take an oriented surface element $\mathrm dA$ with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$. The normal velocity is

$$
u_n=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n.
$$

For an extensive transported quantity $B$ with specific value $b=B/m$, the rate crossing $\mathrm dA$ is

$$
\mathrm d\dot B=\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\dot B=\int_A\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA}.
$$

| Quantity | Specific quantity $b$ | Flux through a surface |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | $1$ | $\rho u_n$ |
| Momentum | $\mathbf u$ | $\rho\mathbf u u_n$ |
| Total energy | $e_t=e+\tfrac12u_i u_i$ | $\rho e_tu_n$ |

Flow **rate** integrates over area. Flux is rate per unit area. A specific quantity is amount per unit mass.

## 8. Reynolds transport theorem

For a fixed control volume,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\mathrm dB_{sys}}{\mathrm dt}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho b\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA
}.
$$

![[aa_reynolds_transport_theorem.png|760]]

The left-hand side follows the material system. On the right:

- the volume integral is accumulation within the control volume;
- the surface integral is net outward transport across the control surface.

Choosing $b=1$, $\mathbf u$, or $e_t$ produces the starting point for mass, momentum, or energy conservation.
