# SESA3043 1.1 - Mathematical Tools and Flow Description

> [!abstract] Summary
> The governing equations become manageable only after the notation is fixed. Scalars carry magnitude; vectors carry magnitude and direction; second-order tensors map one direction into another. Einstein notation compresses three component equations into one indexed expression. The Eulerian view observes a fixed point or control volume, whereas the Lagrangian view follows a material element. The material derivative and Reynolds transport theorem connect the two descriptions. Flow rate, flux and specific quantity then provide a single template for transporting mass, momentum and energy.

> [!summary]- Exam use: quick path
> - **Use it when:** a question asks for index notation, a vector operator, the material derivative or the Reynolds transport theorem.
> - **Assumptions:** Cartesian axes; summation over a repeated index; RTT for a fixed control volume.
> - **Method:** 1. Mark free and dummy indices. 2. Write the operator (gradient, divergence, curl, Laplacian) in index form. 3. $D/Dt=\partial/\partial t+u_j\,\partial/\partial x_j$. 4. Pick the extensive $B$, its specific $b=B/m$ and the flux $\rho b\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n$. 5. Apply the RTT.
> - **Common mistakes:** an index appearing three times in one term; dropping the convective term in a steady flow ($D/Dt\neq0$ even when $\partial/\partial t=0$).
> - **Practise:** [[#9.2 Acceleration in a steady stagnation-point flow (suggested)]] · [[SESA3043 Ch1 Revision Questions - Worked Solutions#1.1 Introduction|Ch1 revision 1.1]]

## The topic in one physical picture

The lecture's own question: *how do you measure the temperature of a river?* (L1, ll. 232–255). You can sit on the bank with a thermometer in the water, or you can drift with the current on a buoy. On the same day, at the same place, the two thermometers record **different** time series. Nothing is wrong with either. The bank thermometer is **Eulerian**: it watches a fixed point while different water flows past it. The buoy's thermometer is **Lagrangian**: it stays with the same water as that water moves. The material derivative is the exact bookkeeping between the two: what the buoy sees equals what the bank sees plus the change caused by being carried into warmer or colder water,

$$
\frac{DT}{Dt}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T.
$$

Now replace the thermometer with a window held in the stream and count what passes through it each second. That count is a **flow rate**; per square metre it is a **flux**; and what each kilogram of fluid carries (1 for mass, $\mathbf u$ for momentum, $E_T$ for energy) is the **specific quantity**. Every conservation law in [[SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations|1.2]] is "what is stored in a box changes because of what crosses its faces plus what acts on it". This note supplies the language (scalars, vectors, tensors, index notation, $\nabla$) to write that sentence once for all three directions. As the lecturer put it: *"math is another language… please be fluent"* (L1, ll. 280–281).

## 1. Coordinate convention

For the external-flow work in Chapters 1–3 (aerodynamic coordinates, L1, ll. 108–118):

- $x$: streamwise;
- $y$: wall-normal, positive from the wall toward the freestream;
- $z$: spanwise.

Most derivations later reduce to two-dimensional $x$–$y$ flow, but the general conservation laws are first written in three dimensions.

> [!warning] Two axis systems were mentioned; only one is used here
> The lecture first showed **aircraft** axes (roll axis $x$, pitch axis $y$, yaw axis $z$ pointing down, L1, ll. 104–107) and then switched to **aerodynamic** axes for Chapters 1–3. Here $y$ is measured *up from the wall*, the opposite sense to the aircraft's downward $z$. The transcript also describes the aircraft axes with compass directions ("north", "east"); those belong to the Earth-fixed frame, not the body axes (see [[#Transcript slips (L1, L2)]]).

## 2. Scalars, vectors and tensors

| Object | Order | Fluid-mechanics examples | Component form |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Scalar | 0 | $p,\rho,T,e$ | one value |
| Vector | 1 | $\mathbf x,\mathbf u,\mathbf f$ | $u_i\mathbf e_i$ |
| Second-order tensor | 2 | stress $\boldsymbol\tau$ | $\tau_{ij}\mathbf e_i\mathbf e_j$ |

> [!warning] Why pressure is a scalar (revision 1.1 Q3)
> The lecture justified this by saying pressure's *"direction is always normal to a surface"* (L1, ll. 124–125). The cleaner statement: at a point, pressure has **the same value on a surface of any orientation** (it is isotropic), so it needs no direction. The **force** it exerts, $-p\,\mathbf n\,\mathrm dA$, is a vector, and its direction comes from the surface normal $\mathbf n$, not from $p$. In stress-tensor terms pressure contributes $-p\,\delta_{ij}$, the same on every face.

The velocity vector may be written as

$$
\mathbf u=u\mathbf e_x+v\mathbf e_y+w\mathbf e_z=u_i\mathbf e_i.
$$

Here $u_i$ is the **component** of $\mathbf u$ along $\mathbf e_i$; the magnitude (speed) is $|\mathbf u|=\sqrt{u_iu_i}$. (The transcript calls $u_i$ "the magnitude", L1, l. 146.)

The stress tensor has nine components:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=
\begin{bmatrix}
\tau_{11}&\tau_{12}&\tau_{13}\\
\tau_{21}&\tau_{22}&\tau_{23}\\
\tau_{31}&\tau_{32}&\tau_{33}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

## 3. Einstein summation and free indices

A repeated index in one term is summed over all coordinate directions:

$$
u_i u_i=u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}
=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}.
$$

In

$$
u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j},
$$

$j$ is the repeated **dummy index** and $i$ is the **free index**. The expression represents one equation for each choice $i=1,2,3$.

For a stress divergence,

$$
\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
=\frac{\partial\tau_{i1}}{\partial x_1}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{i2}}{\partial x_2}
+\frac{\partial\tau_{i3}}{\partial x_3}.
$$

> [!warning] Index consistency
> Every additive term in one tensor equation must carry the same free indices. A dummy index may be renamed, but it must occur exactly twice in a product.

> [!tip] Reading an indexed term aloud
> Count the indices. In $u_j\,\partial u_i/\partial x_j$ the index $j$ appears twice, so sum over it; $i$ appears once, so the term is a **vector** (one value per $i$). In $\partial u_i/\partial x_i$ every index is paired, so the result is a **scalar**. In $\tau_{ij}$ two free indices make a second-order tensor. Hence $\partial u_i/\partial x_i=\partial u_j/\partial x_j$ (revision 1.1 Q8): renaming a dummy index changes nothing.

## 4. Vector differential operators

The del operator is

$$
\nabla=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}.
$$

### Gradient

For scalar $\phi$,

$$
\nabla\phi=\mathbf e_i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

It points in the direction of greatest increase of $\phi$.

### Divergence

For vector $\mathbf u$,

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}.
$$

It measures the local volumetric expansion rate of a fluid element. Positive divergence means expansion; negative divergence means contraction.

### Curl and vorticity

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u.
$$

In a two-dimensional $x$–$y$ flow, the non-zero component is

$$
\omega_z=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}.
$$

In three dimensions, expand the curl as a determinant (see the cross product below):

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
\partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\
u&v&w
\end{vmatrix}
=\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right)\mathbf i
+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)\mathbf j
+\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)\mathbf k.
$$

The curl measures local spin. Irrotational (potential) flow is defined by $\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf 0$.

> [!tip] Spin, not circulation
> $\boldsymbol\omega$ is **twice the angular velocity** of a small fluid element about its own centre. A flow can go round in circles and still be irrotational: in a point vortex every parcel orbits the centre but does not spin about its own axis, like a capsule on a Ferris wheel (L4, ll. 66–70). Conversely a straight shear flow $u=ky$ has parallel streamlines but $\omega_z=-k\neq0$.

### Laplacian

The Laplacian is the divergence of the gradient, $\nabla^2\phi=\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi$. For a scalar,

$$
\nabla^2\phi=\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}
=\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}.
$$

Applied to a vector, it acts on each component: $\nabla^2\mathbf u=(\nabla^2u,\nabla^2v,\nabla^2w)$. This is the viscous term of incompressible Navier–Stokes.

The Laplacian is a **linear** operator (revision 1.1 Q7). For constants $a$ and $b$,

$$
\nabla^2(a\phi_1+b\phi_2)=a\nabla^2\phi_1+b\nabla^2\phi_2,
$$

because each second derivative is linear. So if $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ both satisfy $\nabla^2\phi=0$, so does any combination of them. That one line is the **principle of superposition** used for potential flow in [[SESA3043 1.2b - Potential-Flow Review|1.2b]] and [[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli|1.3]].

> [!warning] Linear operator, nonlinear equations
> Navier–Stokes is **not** linear: the convective term $u_j\,\partial u_i/\partial x_j$ multiplies the unknown by its own derivative. Solutions of Navier–Stokes cannot be added. Superposition works only after the problem has been reduced to Laplace's equation.

### Dot and cross products

The **dot product** of two vectors is a scalar and is **commutative**:

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_iv_i=u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z,
\qquad
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u.
$$

The **cross product** of two vectors is a vector and is **anti-commutative**:

$$
\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
u_x&u_y&u_z\\
\omega_x&\omega_y&\omega_z
\end{vmatrix}
=(u_y\omega_z-u_z\omega_y)\mathbf i
-(u_x\omega_z-u_z\omega_x)\mathbf j
+(u_x\omega_y-u_y\omega_x)\mathbf k,
$$

$$
\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=-\,\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf u.
$$

The lecture said to "look up your math notes" for evaluating the determinant. The line above is **cofactor expansion** along the first row, with the minus sign on the middle term. **Sarrus' rule** gives the same result for 3×3 determinants: repeat the first two columns, add the three down-right diagonal products and subtract the three up-right ones. The direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers from the first vector to the second and the thumb gives $\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$.

> [!tip] Two identities used later
> $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla\!\left(\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$ turns the convective term into a gradient for irrotational flow, which is the Bernoulli step. $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi)=\nabla^2\phi$ turns continuity into Laplace's equation.

## 5. Taylor expansion for a differential element

The local conservation derivations compare fluxes on opposite faces of a small element. The lecture introduced this at the start of L2 (ll. 92–140): a quantity $q(x,y,z,t)$ known at point $P$ is estimated at a nearby point $Q=(x+\epsilon,\,y+\gamma,\,z+\theta)$ by the first-order expansion

$$
q_Q\approx q_P+\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}\epsilon+\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}\gamma+\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}\theta,
$$

valid when $\epsilon,\gamma,\theta$ are small. (The transcript renders the time argument $t$ as "P", ll. 110–124.) In one direction, for any smooth $f(x)$,

$$
f\left(x+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=f(x)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2}
+O(\Delta x^2),
$$

$$
f\left(x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=f(x)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\Delta x}{2}
+O(\Delta x^2).
$$

Subtracting cancels the centre value:

$$
f\left(x+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
-f\left(x-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)
=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x+O(\Delta x^3).
$$

In the limit $\Delta x\to0$, the net flux through opposite faces becomes a spatial derivative times the element volume. This is the local step behind the differential continuity and momentum equations.

## 6. Eulerian and Lagrangian descriptions

The [[Eulerian and Lagrangian Flow Descriptions]] answer different questions:

- **Lagrangian:** how does a particular material particle change as it moves?
- **Eulerian:** how does the field at a fixed point or fixed control volume change?

Let a scalar field be $\phi(\mathbf x,t)$ and let a particle follow $\mathbf x(t)$. The chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dt}
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
+\frac{\mathrm dx_i}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

Since $\mathrm dx_i/\mathrm dt=u_i$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{D\phi}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+u_i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\phi
}.
$$

The first term is the local/unsteady change. The second is convective change caused by moving through a spatial gradient.

For velocity,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t}+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
}
$$

is the acceleration of a fluid particle. In index form its $i$-th component is

$$
a_i=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}+u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$

> [!tip] What each term means (L1, ll. 270–277)
> The slides call $D/Dt$ the **substantial derivative** (CH1-2 slides 30–31; *"substantial/material derivative"* on the Key Formulae slide), and the lecture uses both names (L1, ll. 256–259).
> **Local** derivative $\partial/\partial t$: how the field changes in time at one fixed point (the bank thermometer). **Convective** derivative $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$: how the property changes because the particle is carried to a place where the value is different (the buoy drifting into colder water). The particle sees the sum. Worked numbers: [[#9.1 Two thermometers in a river (suggested)]].

> [!warning] Steady does not mean unaccelerated
> In steady flow $\partial\mathbf u/\partial t=\mathbf 0$ everywhere, but $D\mathbf u/Dt=(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u$ need not vanish. Water speeding up through a nozzle that never changes in time is accelerating. Conversely $\partial\phi/\partial t\neq0$ at a point does not mean the particles there are changing. Keep three symbols distinct: $\partial/\partial t$ (fixed point), $D/Dt$ (following a particle), and $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ (the rate of a whole system, as in [[#8. Reynolds transport theorem]]). See [[#9.2 Acceleration in a steady stagnation-point flow (suggested)]].

## 7. Flow rate, flux and specific quantity

Take an oriented surface element $\mathrm dA$ with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$. The normal velocity is

$$
u_n=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n.
$$

For an extensive transported quantity $B$ with specific value $b=B/m$, the rate crossing $\mathrm dA$ is

$$
\mathrm d\dot B=\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\dot B=\int_A\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA}.
$$

| Quantity | Specific quantity $b$ | Flux through a surface |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | $1$ | $\rho u_n$ |
| Momentum | $\mathbf u$ | $\rho\mathbf u u_n$ |
| Total energy | $E_T=e+\tfrac12u_i u_i$ | $\rho E_Tu_n$ |

Flow **rate** integrates over area. Flux is rate per unit area. A specific quantity is amount per unit mass (L1, ll. 291–321).

> [!warning] Only the normal component crosses a surface
> Use $u_n=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n$, not $|\mathbf u|$. Fluid moving parallel to a surface carries nothing through it. With $\mathbf n$ the **outward** normal of a closed surface, $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n>0$ is outflow and $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n<0$ is inflow, so the closed-surface integral is the **net outflow**. And the momentum flux is $\rho\mathbf u\,u_n$, not $\rho u_n^2$: the specific quantity is the whole vector $\mathbf u$, while only $u_n$ sets how much mass passes. See [[#9.3 Mass, momentum and energy through an inclined window (suggested)]].

## 8. Reynolds transport theorem

For a fixed control volume,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\mathrm dB_{sys}}{\mathrm dt}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho b\,\mathrm dV
+\oint_{CS}\rho b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathrm dA
}.
$$

![[aa_reynolds_transport_theorem.png|760]]

The left-hand side follows the material system. On the right:

- the volume integral is accumulation within the control volume;
- the surface integral is net outward transport across the control surface.

Choosing $b=1$, $\mathbf u$, or $E_T$ produces the starting point for mass, momentum, or energy conservation.

> [!note] Not named in the Week 1 lectures
> The lectures did not use the name "Reynolds transport theorem". They derived the conservation laws directly on a **fixed infinitesimal element**, in the "infinitesimal form… the derivative form rather than the integral form" (L2, ll. 83–88). The theorem above is the finite-volume version of the same bookkeeping: shrink the control volume to $\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dz$ and the surface integral becomes the Taylor-expanded face fluxes of [[SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations#Differential-element derivation|1.2]]. Both routes give identical equations. It is included here because it links the Lagrangian system to the Eulerian control volume in one line.

## 9. Worked examples

### 9.1 Two thermometers in a river (suggested)

The lecture's example (L1, ll. 232–277) with numbers chosen for illustration. A river flows at $u=0.8$ m/s in $x$. The sun warms the water, so at any fixed point the temperature rises at $\partial T/\partial t=+1.0$ K/h. A cold tributary upstream leaves the water cooler downstream, $\partial T/\partial x=-0.5$ K/km. What does each thermometer record?

**Bank (Eulerian).** The probe is fixed, so it records only the local rate:

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=+1.0\ \text{K/h}.
$$

**Buoy (Lagrangian).** Convert the speed: $u=0.8\times3.6=2.88$ km/h. The convective term is

$$
u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=(2.88\ \text{km/h})(-0.5\ \text{K/km})=-1.44\ \text{K/h}.
$$

Add the local term:

$$
\frac{DT}{Dt}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=1.00-1.44=-0.44\ \text{K/h}.
$$

The bank sees the river **warming** while the buoy sees its water **cooling**. In one hour the buoy drifts 2.88 km into water that was 1.44 K colder, and the sun warms it by only 1.0 K.

> [!check] Python check
> With $T(x,t)=T_0-5\times10^{-4}x+(1/3600)\,t$ (SI units): local rate $2.778\times10^{-4}$ K/s $=1.000$ K/h; convective rate $-4.000\times10^{-4}$ K/s $=-1.440$ K/h; $DT/Dt=-1.222\times10^{-4}$ K/s $=-0.440$ K/h. Following the particle directly, $T(ut,t)-T(0,0)$ at $t=3600$ s gives $-0.440$ K, and the bank gives $T(0,t)-T(0,0)=+1.000$ K ✓.

### 9.2 Acceleration in a steady stagnation-point flow (suggested)

Take the 2-D field $u=kx$, $v=-ky$ with $k=2$ s⁻¹: flow coming down onto a wall at $y=0$ and turning sideways (it is the $m=1$ case of the wedge flows in [[SESA3043 1.3 - Complex Potential, Wedge Flows and Unsteady Bernoulli#Step 4: what m does (slide 58; L4, ll. 333–342)|1.3]]). Find the divergence, vorticity and particle acceleration at $(x,y)=(1,0.5)$ m.

**Divergence:**

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=k-k=0.
$$

Incompressible: elements change shape but not volume.

**Vorticity:**

$$
\omega_z=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=0-0=0.
$$

Irrotational.

**Acceleration.** The field does not depend on $t$, so the local term is zero and only the convective term remains, $a_i=u_j\,\partial u_i/\partial x_j$. For $i=1$:

$$
a_x=u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=(kx)(k)+(-ky)(0)=k^2x.
$$

For $i=2$:

$$
a_y=u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=(kx)(0)+(-ky)(-k)=k^2y.
$$

At $(1,0.5)$ m: $\mathbf u=(2,-1)$ m/s and $\mathbf a=(4,2)$ m/s², $|\mathbf a|=4.47$ m/s². The flow is steady, yet every particle away from the origin accelerates.

> [!tip] Sense check
> The particle is moving **down** ($v=-1$ m/s) but $a_y=+2$ m/s² points **up**: it is being slowed as it approaches the wall. Meanwhile $a_x>0$ speeds it sideways. That is a stagnation point in words. Since the flow is also irrotational and steady, Bernoulli applies everywhere: $p_0-p=\tfrac12\rho(u^2+v^2)=\tfrac12\rho k^2(x^2+y^2)$, which is 3.06 Pa at this point in air ($\rho=1.225$ kg/m³).

> [!check] Python check
> Symbolic: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, $\omega_z=0$, $\mathbf a=(k^2x,\,k^2y)$. Numerically at $(1,0.5)$ m: $\mathbf u=(2,-1)$ m/s, $\mathbf a=(4.0,2.0)$ m/s², $|\mathbf a|=4.472$ m/s². Independent check along the particle path $x(t)=x_0e^{kt}$: a finite-difference $\ddot x$ at $t=0$ gives $4.000$ m/s² ✓. Bernoulli: $\tfrac12(1.225)(4)(1.25)=3.0625$ Pa ✓.

### 9.3 Mass, momentum and energy through an inclined window (suggested)

Air ($\rho=1.2$ kg/m³, $T=288$ K, $c_v=718$ J/(kg K)) flows uniformly at $\mathbf u=(10,0,0)$ m/s through a flat 0.5 m × 0.2 m window whose unit normal is tilted $30^\circ$ from $x$: $\mathbf n=(\cos30^\circ,\sin30^\circ,0)$. Find the mass, momentum and energy flow rates (L1, ll. 291–321).

**Normal velocity:**

$$
u_n=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n=10\cos30^\circ=8.660\ \text{m/s}.
$$

**Mass** ($b=1$). Flux $\rho u_n=10.39$ kg/(m² s); area $A=0.1$ m²:

$$
\dot m=\rho u_nA=1.2(8.660)(0.1)=1.039\ \text{kg/s}.
$$

**Momentum** ($b=\mathbf u$):

$$
\dot{\mathbf M}=\int_A\rho\mathbf u\,u_n\,\mathrm dA=\dot m\,\mathbf u=(10.39,\,0,\,0)\ \text{N}.
$$

The momentum flow points along $\mathbf u$, not along $\mathbf n$.

**Energy** ($b=E_T$). Specific internal energy $e=c_vT=718(288)=206\,784$ J/kg; specific kinetic energy $\tfrac12u_iu_i=50$ J/kg:

$$
E_T=206\,834\ \text{J/kg},\qquad\dot E=\dot m\,E_T=1.039(206\,834)=2.149\times10^5\ \text{W}.
$$

The kinetic part is only 0.024% of $E_T$ at 10 m/s, one reason energy decouples from low-speed incompressible flow ([[SESA3043 1.2 - Conservation Laws and Governing Equations#7. Incompressible reduction|1.2 §7]]).

**Non-uniform profile.** If instead the velocity varies across a section of height $h=0.1$ m as $u(y)=Uy/h$ with $U=10$ m/s (per unit span):

$$
\dot m'=\int_0^h\rho\frac{Uy}{h}\,\mathrm dy=\frac{\rho Uh}{2}=0.600\ \text{kg/(m s)},
$$

$$
\dot M'=\int_0^h\rho\left(\frac{Uy}{h}\right)^2\mathrm dy=\frac{\rho U^2h}{3}=4.00\ \text{N/m}.
$$

The mean velocity is $\bar u=U/2=5$ m/s, so $\dot m'\bar u=3.00$ N/m: the true momentum flow is $4/3$ times larger. Flux must be integrated point by point; it cannot be built from the average velocity.

> [!check] Python check
> $u_n=8.6603$ m/s, $\rho u_n=10.392$ kg/(m² s), $\dot m=1.0392$ kg/s, $\dot{\mathbf M}=(10.392,0,0)$ N, $E_T=206\,834$ J/kg, $\dot E=214\,948$ W, kinetic share $2.42\times10^{-4}$. Linear profile by trapezoidal integration (200 001 points): $\dot m'=0.6000$ kg/(m s), $\dot M'=4.0000$ N/m, ratio to $\dot m'\bar u$ = 1.3333 ✓.

The transport theorem|SESA3047 2.1 §8]]: **same name, different theorem.** The Reynolds transport theorem (§8 here) links the rate of change of a property of a *material system* to a *control volume*; its extra term is the net flux through the control surface. The vector transport theorem, ${}^a\dot{\mathbf p}={}^b\dot{\mathbf p}+\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$ (Eq. 2.22), links the time derivatives of one *vector* seen from two *frames* in relative rotation; its extra term is $\boldsymbol\omega_{b/a}\times\mathbf p$.
